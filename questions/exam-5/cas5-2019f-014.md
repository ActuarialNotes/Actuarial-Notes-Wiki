Given the following:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Home Value | \$300,000 |
| Insured Value | \$250,000 |
| Amount of Loss | \$260,000 |

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the amount paid by the policyholder at the time of loss under the following:

i. Coinsurance percentage of 80%

ii. Coinsurance percentage of 90%

### Answer
i. $10,000; ii. $19,259

### Explanation
A coinsurance clause requires the insured to carry insurance of at least the coinsurance percentage times the property's value. If they fall short, the indemnity payment is reduced by the **apportionment factor**

$$a = \min\left(\frac{\text{Insured Value}}{\text{Coinsurance \%} \times \text{Property Value}},\ 1\right)$$

and the insurer pays $\min(a \times \text{Loss},\ \text{Insured Value})$ — the insured value still caps the payment regardless of the penalty. Note the question asks what the **policyholder** pays, which is the loss less the indemnity.

**i. Coinsurance percentage of 80%.** Required insurance:

$$0.80 \times 300{,}000 = \$240{,}000$$

The insured carries \$250,000, which exceeds the requirement, so there is **no coinsurance penalty**:

$$a = \min\left(\frac{250{,}000}{240{,}000},\ 1\right) = 1$$

The insurer pays $\min(1 \times 260{,}000,\ 250{,}000) = \$250{,}000$ — limited not by the coinsurance clause but by the amount of insurance purchased. The policyholder bears the rest:

$$260{,}000 - 250{,}000 = \$10{,}000$$

**ii. Coinsurance percentage of 90%.** Required insurance:

$$0.90 \times 300{,}000 = \$270{,}000$$

The insured carries only \$250,000, so the property is underinsured relative to the requirement and the penalty applies:

$$a = \frac{250{,}000}{270{,}000} = 0.9259$$

$$\text{Indemnity} = \min(0.9259 \times 260{,}000,\ 250{,}000) = \min(240{,}740.74,\ 250{,}000) = \$240{,}740.74$$

$$\text{Policyholder pays} = 260{,}000 - 240{,}740.74 = \$19{,}259.26$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the coinsurance penalty and then the amount owed by the policyholder. Common mistakes included calculating the amount paid by the *insurer* rather than by the policyholder, and treating the question as though it concerned a copayment rather than a coinsurance requirement — for example assuming every insured pays 20% of the loss alongside the insurer.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Describe how coinsurance provisions promote equitable rates.

### Explanation
Property rates are generally calculated on the assumption that insureds carry insurance to value. If some insureds do not, and no coinsurance provision existed, an underinsured risk would collect the same proportion of its loss as a fully insured risk while paying premium on a smaller amount of insurance. Its rate per dollar of insurance would then be too low, and the fully insured risks would be **subsidizing** it — an inequitable outcome, since insureds presenting different expected costs would be charged the same relative rate.

The coinsurance provision restores equity by **reducing the loss payment to the underinsured risk at the time of loss**, in proportion to how far short of the requirement it falls. Underinsured and fully insured risks then produce the same loss cost per dollar of insurance, so the premium each pays reflects its own expected losses rather than one group's premium covering the other's shortfall.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe how coinsurance provisions promote equitable rates. Common mistakes included referring to a penalty without saying that it applies to losses or at the time of loss, referring to a penalty without saying it applies only to the underinsured, and confusing the definitions of *equitable* and *adequate* rates.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Describe how coinsurance provisions promote adequate rates.

### Explanation
Because rates are set assuming insureds carry insurance to value, widespread underinsurance leaves the insurer collecting premium on less insured value than the exposure it is actually carrying, so the rate level in aggregate is **inadequate**.

The coinsurance provision works on this in two ways. Directly, the penalty reduces the losses the insurer pays on underinsured risks, lowering the loss cost toward what the collected premium supports. Indirectly — and more importantly — the prospect of a penalty at the time of loss **encourages insureds to insure to value**. As more of the book carries insurance to value, the insured values on which premium is charged line up with the exposure actually at risk, so the insurer is not exposed to unknown levels of risk and the overall rate level is adequate.

Note that the provision *encourages* insurance to value; it does not guarantee or require it.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe how coinsurance provisions promote adequate rates. Common mistakes included restating the question without ever explaining how the provisions make rates adequate, asserting that coinsurance provisions guarantee or require insurance to value when they only encourage it, and confusing the definitions of *equitable* and *adequate* rates.
