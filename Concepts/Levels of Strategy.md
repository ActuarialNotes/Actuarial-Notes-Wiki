**Levels of Strategy** are the organizational levels at which strategy is set. [[Corporate-Level Strategy|Corporate-level strategy]] decides in what industry or industries the organization competes, treating it as a portfolio of businesses, and [[Business-Level Strategy|business-level strategy]] decides how each of those businesses competes within its own industry.

| Level | Question it answers | In an insurer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Corporate | In which industries to compete | Which lines, companies and markets the group owns |
| Business | How to compete in one industry | How each underwriting company or line wins customers |

Both levels are formulated in the [[Strategic Management Process|strategic management process]], after the analysis of the environment. Formulation and implementation are steps of the strategic management process. The levels say where in the organization a strategy is set.

## Corporate-level strategy

An insurance group choosing which lines, companies and markets to own is making corporate-level decisions. The logic of corporate strategy is synergy, where businesses under common ownership do better together than apart, and diversification, which spreads risk and opportunity over several businesses. Its main options are:

- **Concentration**, competing in a single industry through market penetration (more share of existing markets with existing products), market development (existing products in new markets) or product development (new products for existing markets).
- **Vertical integration**, entering a new part of the value chain: backward into a supplier's business, or forward into a buyer's. It is attractive when suppliers or buyers have too much power ([[Porter's Five Forces]]).
- **Diversification**, entering new industries: related, when the new industry has important similarities with the existing ones, or unrelated, when it has none.
- **Getting smaller**, by retrenchment (reducing the size of part of the operations) or by exiting an industry.

Forward vertical integration moves toward the customer, into a buyer's business. Backward integration moves toward the inputs, into a supplier's business.

> [!example]- How would you classify each of these four decisions by level and strategy? {Example}
> An insurance group makes four decisions in one year:
>
> 1. It buys the independent agencies that sell most of its policies.
> 2. It sells its life insurance subsidiary to concentrate on property-casualty insurance.
> 3. Its personal auto company cuts its expenses so it can charge less than its rivals across the whole market.
> 4. Its commercial company starts selling its existing contractors coverage in two new states.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Decision 1 is corporate level, forward vertical integration: the group moves into the business of the agencies that sell its product.
> > - Decision 2 is corporate level, getting smaller: the group exits an industry, so the decision is about which industries it competes in.
> > - Decision 3 is business level, cost leadership: a broad market won on low cost.
> > - Decision 4 is corporate level, concentration by market development: an existing product in new markets, while the company stays in the same industry.

## Business-level strategy

Business strategy answers how a firm will compete in a particular industry. The source of advantage is low cost or uniqueness, and the scope is a broad market or a narrow segment, so the business level has four generic strategies, Porter's:

| Scope | Low cost | Uniqueness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Broad market | Cost leadership | Differentiation |
| Narrow segment | Focused cost leadership | Focused differentiation |

A firm that offers both low prices and features customers value follows a best-cost strategy, and one that offers neither is stuck in the middle.

> [!example]- Which generic strategy does each of these two insurers follow? {Example}
> A specialty insurer writes only coverage for veterinary clinics. Its premiums are higher than general commercial insurers', but its coverage forms and claims staff are built around veterinary practices. A second insurer writes small business coverage for every kind of business online, at the lowest price in the market.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The veterinary insurer serves a narrow segment and competes on uniqueness, so it follows **focused differentiation**.
> > - The online insurer serves the broad market and competes on low cost, so it follows **cost leadership**.

> [!trap] Calling a new market a new industry
> Selling an existing product in new territories is market development, a concentration strategy. Diversification means entering a new industry.
