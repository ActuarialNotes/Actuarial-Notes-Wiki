Given the following information as of December 31, 2017:

| Calendar Year | Earned Premium (\$000) | On-Level Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 127,500 | 0.710 |
| 2015 | 117,600 | 0.660 |
| 2016 | 64,300 | 0.850 |
| 2017 | 58,900 | 1.000 |

| Accident Year | Ultimate Claim Counts | Ultimate Severity (\$) |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 2,200 | 32,600 |
| 2015 | 1,970 | 35,300 |

| | |
|---|---|
| −1.3% | Annual claim count trend |
| 6.0% | Annual severity trend |
| 15% | Estimated savings on claims occurring after January 1, 2017 due to legislative change |

## Part a (3 points)

Estimate the ultimate claims for accident years 2016 and 2017 using an appropriate frequency-severity technique.

### Answer

AY 2016: approximately \$49,300 thousand; AY 2017: approximately \$47,250 thousand

### Explanation

Premium is the exposure base here, so it must be brought to a common rate level before it can measure exposure. On-level premium = earned premium × on-level adjustment:

| CY | Earned Premium | On-Level Adj | On-Level Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 127,500 | 0.710 | 90,525 |
| 2015 | 117,600 | 0.660 | 77,616 |
| 2016 | 64,300 | 0.850 | 54,655 |
| 2017 | 58,900 | 1.000 | 58,900 |

**Step 1 — Frequency, trended to the 2017 level.** Trend the ultimate counts at −1.3% per year, then divide by on-level premium:

| AY | Ult Counts | Count Trend to 2017 | Trended Counts | On-Level Premium | Trended Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 2,200 | $0.987^3 = 0.962$ | 2,115 | 90,525 | 2.34% |
| 2015 | 1,970 | $0.987^2 = 0.974$ | 1,919 | 77,616 | 2.47% |

Selected 2017 frequency = average = **2.40%**.

Detrending one year gives the 2016 frequency level:

$$f_{2016} = \frac{0.0240}{0.987} = 2.44\% \ \text{(per on-level premium)}$$

**Step 2 — Severity, trended to the 2017 level.** Trend at 6% per year, and apply the 15% legislative savings so that the historical severities are stated at the post-reform (2017) level:

| AY | Ult Severity | Severity Trend to 2017 | Tort Adjustment | Trended Severity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 32,600 | $1.06^3 = 1.191$ | 0.85 | 33,003 |
| 2015 | 35,300 | $1.06^2 = 1.124$ | 0.85 | 33,714 |

Selected 2017 severity = average = **33,358**.

Accident year 2016 predates the legislative change, so the tort adjustment must be reversed and the severity detrended one year:

$$S_{2016} = \frac{33{,}358}{1.06 \times 0.85} = 37{,}024$$

**Step 3 — Ultimate claims** = frequency × on-level premium × severity:

| AY | Selected Frequency | On-Level Premium | Ultimate Counts | Selected Severity | Ultimate Claims (\$000) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 2.44% | 54,655 | 1,331 | 37,024 | 49,300 |
| 2017 | 2.40% | 58,900 | 1,416 | 33,358 | 47,247 |

Trending the selections to 2016 instead and then forward to 2017 is algebraically equivalent and received full credit, as did selecting the 2014 or 2015 value rather than the average.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to use a frequency-severity technique incorporating trend, trending the accident years consistently and separately for frequency and severity, and then applying the result to the premium exposure base for 2016 and 2017. That required adjusting for the change in premium per exposure (on-leveling), applying the frequency and severity trends, and applying the tort adjustment — to the 2017 losses only.

Common mistakes included failing to convert claim counts into a frequency, applying the on-level factors incorrectly or inconsistently, omitting the tort factor or applying it to both years (or to 2016 only), multiplying frequency by severity without multiplying by premium, attempting to calculate a loss ratio instead, and averaging the 2014 and 2015 data without trending each year individually first.

## Part b (0.5 point)

Briefly describe two key assumptions of frequency-severity techniques.

### Explanation

- **Claim counts develop and are reported consistently over time**, on a stable definition of a claim count, so that the historical count development pattern is a valid guide for the immature years. A change in how claims are counted (e.g. recording claims closed without payment) breaks the frequency estimate.
- **The claim settlement rate and case reserve adequacy are stable**, so the observed severity development reflects the underlying cost of claims rather than a change in the speed of settlement or in reserving strength. A related assumption is that the mix of business by claim type stays homogeneous, so that frequency and severity relationships observed in the past continue into the future.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to describe two key assumptions of the frequency-severity technique.

Common mistakes included giving a generic answer about the availability or accuracy of data, and specifying a particular required definition of claim count — the assumption is that the definition is *consistent*, and several valid definitions exist.
