An article in the February 2019 issue (no. 88) of the Society of Actuaries Health Section's Health Watch on high-deductible health plans (HDHPs) paired with health savings accounts (HSAs) or health reimbursement accounts (HRAs). It examines the history, theory and effectiveness of HDHPs and HSAs, the current research on their impact on health care, and some possible paths forward for both.

> - [[Exam GH-101 (SOA)|Exam GH-101]] — Outcomes 4a; the whole article.

## Brief History of the High-Deductible Health Plan and Health Savings Account
- HDHPs were born in the 1970s and became integrated alongside HSAs and HRAs into U.S. health and tax policy in the early 2000s.
- The history is viewed in two phases: before and after the 2003 Medicare Modernization Act (MMA).
- Phase 1, pre-MMA: cafeteria plans in the mid-1970s, the IRS health version of the flexible spending account, and the Archer MSA pilot for small employers and the self-employed (up to 750,000 allowed, ended in 2005 nationwide except for California); HIPAA defined the cost sharing for plans eligible to have an Archer MSA, formally creating HDHPs.
- Phase 2, post-MMA: the HSA became a permanent feature of the tax code, available to anyone purchasing a qualifying HDHP and fundable by both employer and employee.

## Anatomy of the HDHP/HSA Combination
- What Makes an HDHP?
    - An HDHP has a specific meaning under the IRS code that governs the tax deductibility of the accompanying HSA, with minimum deductibles and maximum out-of-pocket limits set each year (Figure 1 shows 2015 through 2019).
    - A family HDHP whose self-only deductible is below the family minimum must administer an aggregate deductible; with a self-only deductible above it, an embedded deductible approach could be used.
- What Makes an HSA?
    - HSAs let individuals whose only comprehensive medical coverage is an HDHP save for health care expenses on a pretax basis, with deposits up to an annual maximum by both employer and employee (Figure 2).
    - The HSA balance is owned by the enrollee, accrues interest and tax-free investment growth, and is tax-free on withdrawal for eligible medical expenses.
    - HRAs and health FSAs are other tax-advantaged accounts, available only with employer-sponsored health plans; Figure 3 compares the three.

## The Theory of HDHPs
- Consumer Choice and Empowerment
    - A major tenet is that individuals with "skin in the game" take greater responsibility in selecting health services and controlling costs; the article lists examples of consumer behaviour, such as saving for health care services, choosing a more appropriate treatment venue, avoiding unnecessary care, choosing generic drugs, comparing quality ratings and negotiating prices.
    - Consumers with urgent care needs may not have time to shop, and those with higher-cost chronic care needs are more likely to hit their out-of-pocket limit.
- Is "High" Actually High?
    - An HDHP should balance the minimum deductibles against the expected cost; Figure 4 gives the probability that claims exceed the [[Deductible|deductible]].
- Impact of HDHPs and Savings Accounts on Claims
    - The HDHP has an assumed reduction in utilization, offset in part by employer-funded account amounts; Figure 5 outlines the adjustments based on the Milliman Health Cost Guidelines.
- HDHP Prevalence in the Market
    - Enrollment in HSA-qualified HDHPs in the individual market, the small- and large-group markets and Medicaid.

## The Practice of HDHPs
- Claim Costs
    - Several studies found savings between 5 percent and 15 percent for HDHPs; HDHPs have not shown a clear ability to bend the cost curve beyond the initial year of adoption, though savings appear to be retained from year to year.
    - Raw savings are driven by the relative health of individuals selecting plans, the utilization impact of plan design and funding, and increased consumer engagement.
- Consumer Behavior and Health Outcomes
    - A 2016 study measured behaviours such as saving for future health services (40 percent), comparing prices (14 percent), comparing quality (14 percent) and negotiating prices (6 percent); several studies indicated reduced preventive care, and little research has been done on health outcomes.
- Financial Burdens
    - Members with higher-deductible plans pay higher shares of costs as claims begin to be incurred.
- Other Factors That Could Make HDHPs Work Better
    - Cost transparency; discussions between providers and patients; pre-funding of HSAs; allowing more first dollar coverage; lengthened consumerism.
- The Impact of Drug Coupons
    - Coupons go directly to the enrollee, can raise plan costs by incenting higher-cost prescriptions and remove incentives that underlie HDHPs.

## The Future of HSAs
- Proposed changes group into four categories: expanding which plans can be paired with HSAs, expanding the ability of individuals to contribute, allowing HSAs to be used for a broader variety of expenses, and expanding the scope of HSAs beyond major medical health care.
- Expansion of Plans That can be Paired With HSAs
    - HSAs Could be Paired With all ACA Bronze and/or Catastrophic Plans
    - Line up ACA and HDHP Maximum Out-of-pocket Limits
    - Allow Medicare-eligible Individuals to use HSAs
    - Allow Anyone to use an HSA
- Expansion of Contributions
    - Allow Contributions to Match the Out-of-pocket Maximum
    - Allow Spousal Catch-up Contributions in Family HSAs
- Expansion of Major Medical use of HSA Funds
    - Allow use of HSAs for Health Care Premiums
    - Allow use of HSAs for Over-the-counter Health Supplies
    - Allow use of HSAs for Direct Primary Care Arrangements
- Expansion of Nonmajor Medical use of HSA Funds
    - Allow use of HSAs for Fitness Equipment
    - Allow use of HSAs to Pre-fund Long-term Care Needs

## Timeline of the High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) and the Health Savings Account (HSA)
- A sidebar giving the dates from the mid-1980s idea promoted by John Goodman to the current HDHP and HSA formalized in the Medicare Modernization Act and the later modifications by the Health Opportunity Patient Empowerment Act and the Affordable Care Act.

## Conclusion
- The jury is still out on how effective HDHPs and HSAs are in reducing health care expenditures and improving quality and patient outcomes; there are signs the plans reduce costs, but the reduction is at least partly due to avoidance of beneficial services and does not compound year over year.
- More research is needed to assess the value these plans can offer.
