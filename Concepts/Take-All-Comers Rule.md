![[Media/Figures/Take-All-Comers_Rule.svg|340]]

The **Take-All-Comers Rule** requires an insurer licensed to write compulsory automobile insurance in a province to offer coverage, at its filed rates, to any applicant who meets its filed underwriting rules. The insurer may not decline, or quietly discourage, an eligible applicant it would prefer not to write.

The rule exists because auto insurance is compulsory. If the state requires a driver to buy a product, it must ensure the product can actually be bought, or the mandate becomes a prohibition on driving for the risks nobody wants.

In practice the rule forbids an insurer to:

1. Refuse to quote.
2. Quote a deliberately unusable price outside the filed plan.
3. Impose unfiled conditions.
4. Steer an eligible applicant to an affiliate or to the [[Facility Association]] to avoid writing them.

It does not forbid applying filed underwriting rules. An insurer may decline an applicant who fails a rule it has filed and the regulator has accepted. The rule must be written down, approved and applied consistently.

> [!example]- Is each of these practices compliant for an insurer writing compulsory auto in a take-all-comers province? {Example}
> 1. Declining an applicant with three at-fault accidents in three years, per a filed underwriting rule.
> 2. Telling a broker informally to stop submitting applications from new drivers because "we're full."
> 3. Quoting an eligible applicant a premium $40\%$ above the filed rate because the underwriter dislikes the risk.
> 4. Requiring an eligible applicant to also buy a home policy before auto coverage will be issued.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Practice 1 is compliant. The rule is filed, objective and consistently applied, and the applicant will likely reach the [[Facility Association]], which is what the residual market is for.
> > - Practice 2 is not compliant. It is a decline by another name, an unfiled underwriting restriction applied through the distribution channel. It is also the hardest kind to detect, which is why regulators use broker surveys and complaint patterns rather than waiting for filings.
> > - Practice 3 is not compliant, and it is separately a rate violation. Rates must be the filed rates, so charging off-plan deviates from an approved filing whatever the take-all-comers position.
> > - Practice 4 is not compliant. Tied selling conditions the compulsory product on the purchase of a discretionary one, which is a conduct violation in every Canadian jurisdiction.
> > - Only practice 1 is compliant, because only practice 1 is written down and filed.

## The rule and the Facility Association

The rule is the reason the [[Facility Association]] is a residual mechanism of last resort rather than a dumping ground. Risks reach it because no insurer's filed rules accept them, not because insurers chose not to write them.

## Enforcement

Enforcement is a [[Market Conduct Regulation|market conduct]] matter, using mystery shopping, complaint patterns and broker reports, and penalties reach licence conditions.

## Inadequate approved rates

An insurer cannot rely on underwriting selection to fix an inadequate approved rate. If the rate is inadequate for a segment the filed rules accept, the insurer must write that segment at that rate. The only lawful responses are to change the filed rules or to change the filed rate.

> [!trap] Declining on an unfiled rule
> Underwriting judgement that is not in the filed rules cannot be used to turn away an eligible applicant, whether by a refusal, a surcharge or an instruction to brokers. To stop writing a class, the insurer files a rule for it.
