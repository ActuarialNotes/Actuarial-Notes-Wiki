Given the following information about a home's propensity for loss:

| Size of Loss | Loss Distribution |
|---|---|
| $X \le \$400{,}000$ | 50.0% |
| $\$400{,}000 < X \le \$550{,}000$ | 25.0% |
| $\$550{,}000 < X \le \$700{,}000$ | 10.0% |
| $\$700{,}000 < X \le \$850{,}000$ | 10.0% |
| $\$850{,}000 < X \le \$1{,}000{,}000$ | 2.5% |
| $\$1{,}000{,}000 < X \le \$1{,}500{,}000$ | 2.5% |
| Total | 100.0% |

- Expected claim frequency is 2%.
- Expected losses are uniformly distributed within each layer of loss.
- The home is valued at \$1,500,000.

## Part a (1.25 points)

Calculate the rate per \$1,000 of coverage for the home at the following amounts of insurance:

i. \$1,500,000
ii. \$800,000

### Answer

i. \$5.51 per \$1,000 of coverage
ii. \$9.95 per \$1,000 of coverage

### Explanation

**i. AOI = \$1,500,000 (insured to value)**

Losses are uniform within each layer, so each layer's expected severity is its midpoint. In \$000s:

$$E[X] = 0.50(200) + 0.25(475) + 0.10(625) + 0.10(775) + 0.025(925) + 0.025(1{,}250) = 413.125$$

$$\text{Pure premium} = 0.02 \times 413{,}125 = 8{,}262.50$$

$$\text{Rate per \$1,000 AOI} = \frac{8{,}262.50}{1{,}500{,}000 / 1{,}000} = \frac{8{,}262.50}{1{,}500} = \$5.51$$

**ii. AOI = \$800,000**

Now every loss is capped at \$800,000. The \$700K–\$850K layer straddles the cap: two-thirds of it (700–800) is paid in full, averaging \$750K, and one third (800–850) is capped at \$800K:

$$0.10 \times \left[\tfrac{2}{3}(750) + \tfrac{1}{3}(800)\right] = 0.10 \times 766.667$$

Both layers above \$850K are capped at \$800K in full, and together carry 5% of the distribution.

$$E[X \wedge 800] = 0.50(200) + 0.25(475) + 0.10(625) + 0.10(766.667) + 0.05(800) = 397.917$$

$$\text{Rate per \$1,000 AOI} = \frac{0.02 \times 397{,}917}{800} = \frac{7{,}958.33}{800} = \$9.95$$

Note the rate per \$1,000 is far higher at the lower amount of insurance — the first dollars of coverage are the most likely to be used.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to calculate the rate per amount of insurance (AOI) by computing the average severity from the layer midpoints and the given loss distribution, converting to a pure premium with the frequency, and dividing by the AOI in thousands. For part ii they were additionally expected to cap losses at \$800K and adjust the average severity of the \$700K–\$850K layer accordingly.

Common mistakes included using the upper or lower bound of a layer instead of the midpoint, failing to multiply by frequency, failing to divide by the AOI, mishandling the partially-capped \$700K–\$850K layer, and omitting the layers above \$800K entirely rather than capping them.

## Part b (0.5 point)

Briefly discuss a problem associated with underinsurance from the following perspectives:

i. Insured
ii. Insurer

### Explanation

**i. Insured.** On a total or near-total loss the insured recovers only the amount of insurance purchased, which falls short of the cost to rebuild — the shortfall must be funded out of pocket. Where the policy carries a coinsurance clause, the insured is also subject to a coinsurance penalty on *partial* losses, so even a moderate loss is not fully indemnified.

**ii. Insurer.** Rates per \$1,000 of coverage are typically built assuming homes are insured to value. An underinsured home occupies a lower amount of insurance but retains its full propensity for loss in the layers it does buy, so the premium collected is **inadequate** for the expected losses. If the insurer responds by raising rates across the board, the insureds who are properly insured to value end up subsidizing those who are not, and the rates become inequitable.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to describe an issue with underinsurance from the insured's perspective and from the insurer's perspective.

Common mistakes included, for the insured, simply stating that the loss would not be fully paid without specifying that this arises on a total or near-total loss, and giving an insurer issue (inadequate premium) as an insured issue. For the insurer, common mistakes were saying profitability would be "impacted" without a direction, or that rates would be "inaccurate" or "skewed" without specifying that they would be inadequate.

## Part c (1 point)

The home is insured for \$1,000,000 with a coinsurance requirement of 80%. Calculate the indemnity payments and coinsurance penalties for the following losses:

i. \$800,000
ii. \$1,200,000

### Answer

i. Indemnity \$666,667; coinsurance penalty \$133,333
ii. Indemnity \$1,000,000; coinsurance penalty \$0

### Explanation

The coinsurance apportionment ratio is

$$a = \min\left(\frac{\text{Amount of Insurance}}{\text{Coinsurance \%} \times \text{Value}},\ 1\right) = \min\left(\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{0.80 \times 1{,}500{,}000},\ 1\right) = \min(0.8333,\ 1) = 0.8333$$

The insured has bought only 83.3% of the required amount, so partial losses are indemnified at that share, subject to the policy limit.

**i. Loss = \$800,000**

$$I = \min(800{,}000 \times 0.8333,\ 1{,}000{,}000) = 666{,}667$$

$$\text{Coinsurance penalty} = 800{,}000 - 666{,}667 = 133{,}333$$

**ii. Loss = \$1,200,000**

$$I = \min(1{,}200{,}000 \times 0.8333,\ 1{,}000{,}000) = \min(1{,}000{,}000,\ 1{,}000{,}000) = 1{,}000{,}000$$

$$\text{Coinsurance penalty} = \min(1{,}200{,}000,\ 1{,}000{,}000) - 1{,}000{,}000 = 0$$

The apportioned amount reaches the policy limit, so the insured collects the full \$1,000,000 of coverage and there is no coinsurance penalty. The \$200,000 of loss above the limit is uncovered because of the limit, not because of the coinsurance clause.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to calculate the apportionment ratio and apply it to each loss to determine the indemnity payment and the coinsurance penalty.

Common mistakes included using an incorrect apportionment ratio, and including the loss amount in excess of the coverage limit in the coinsurance penalty.
