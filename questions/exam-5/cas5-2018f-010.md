A company writes homeowners insurance in a large state divided in half by a mountain range. The company currently uses two geographic rating territories, one on either side of the mountains, as the range has an effect on weather patterns. Each territory has sufficient exposures for its loss experience to be considered fully credible.

## Part a (0.5 point)

Briefly discuss two disadvantages of the company's current territorial rating approach.

### Explanation

- **The territories are not homogeneous.** Two territories covering half a large state each will contain a wide range of risks — urban and rural, coastal and inland, high and low property values — all charged the same territorial relativity. Rating a heterogeneous group at one average rate overcharges the better risks and undercharges the worse ones.
- **The definition reflects only one peril.** The split was drawn on weather patterns, but homeowners losses also arise from theft, fire, water and liability, whose geographic patterns follow population density rather than the mountain range. Territories built on weather alone will not capture that variation.

Either point supports the further consequence that competitors using finer territory definitions can select against the company: they will quote lower rates to the better risks within each of the company's two territories, leaving it with the worse risks at an average rate.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to understand why a company would not want to use overly broad territorial definitions.

Common mistakes included providing only one disadvantage, and providing advantages of the company's territorial rating approach instead of disadvantages.

## Part b (2 points)

Discuss the process by which an actuary would develop new rating territory definitions for this state. Briefly explain a consideration for each step in the process.

### Explanation

**Step 1 — Define the basic geographic unit.** Divide the state into small units such as postal/zip codes or counties.
*Consideration:* trade off granularity against stability — zip codes are more granular but their boundaries are redefined over time, while counties are stable but coarse.

**Step 2 — Quantify the geographic risk of each basic unit, preferably with a multivariate model.** Fit a GLM including the other rating variables so that the geographic signal is isolated.
*Consideration:* geography is highly correlated with other rating variables (age, amount of insurance, construction), so a multivariate approach is needed to separate the true territorial signal from the effect of those correlated variables — and from noise, since individual units have thin data.

**Step 3 — Smooth the residual geographic effect across units.** Take the residual variation not explained by the model and smooth it spatially across neighbouring units.
*Consideration:* choose the smoothing basis to suit the peril — distance-based smoothing suits weather, while adjacency-based smoothing suits perils that follow contiguous population patterns such as theft.

**Step 4 — Cluster the smoothed basic units into rating territories.** Group units with similar smoothed relativities into a manageable number of territories.
*Consideration:* select the clustering method (quantile-based, giving territories of roughly equal exposure volume, versus similarity-based) and the number of territories, balancing homogeneity within each territory against having enough volume in each for credible experience.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to understand the steps involved in developing new rating territories.

Common mistakes included simply listing the steps with no consideration attached to each, neglecting the final clustering of geographic units into rating territories, and describing criteria for evaluating rating variables instead of the territory-development process.
