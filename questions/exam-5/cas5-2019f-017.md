## Part a (0.75 points)

Identify three changes in an insurance company's internal environment that could distort the paid or reported development patterns.

### Explanation
Three distinct changes internal to the company — that is, within its own control, not external events such as tort reform, inflation or a catastrophe:

1. **A change in case reserve adequacy** — the claims department begins setting case reserves at a higher (or lower) level relative to the ultimate value of the claim.
2. **A change in the rate of claim settlement** — the claims department settles and closes claims faster (or slower) than in prior years, for example after introducing a new claims system.
3. **A change in the mix of business written**, or in the underwriting rules that determine what is written — for example a shift toward higher policy limits or a different deductible.

Other acceptable answers include a change in the claim reporting process (a new call center), a change in the reinsurance program, a change in subrogation procedures, adopting a more aggressive litigation stance, prioritizing large claims over small ones, and starting a fraud detection unit.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to give examples of changes *internal* to the insurer's own practices that can distort paid or reported triangles. Common mistakes included discussing changes external to the company such as tort reform; discussing events outside the company's control such as large losses or catastrophes; and giving two examples too similar to one another, such as "paying claims faster" and "an increase in the settlement rate", which count as one.

## Part b (1.5 points)

Briefly describe how each change identified in part a. above may be observed in a diagnostic triangle.

### Explanation
For each change, the answer needs a *specific* diagnostic triangle and the *direction* in which it moves — down a column (across accident years at the same maturity) or along the latest diagonal.

**1. Change in case reserve adequacy.** Build a triangle of **average case outstanding per open claim** (case outstanding divided by open claim counts). If case adequacy has strengthened, this ratio increases **down the column**: at the same maturity, more recent accident years carry a higher reserve per open claim than older ones did.

A **paid-to-reported claims** triangle gives the same signal from the other direction: strengthening case reserves inflates reported claims without changing paid claims, so the paid-to-reported ratio **decreases down the column**.

**2. Change in the rate of claim settlement.** Build a **disposal rate** triangle — closed claim counts divided by ultimate (or reported) claim counts. A speed-up in settlement shows as the ratio **increasing down the column**: recent accident years have a larger share of claims closed by the same maturity than older years did.

The **paid-to-reported** triangle confirms it, increasing down the column as claims are paid out sooner relative to what has been reported.

**3. Change in the mix of business or underwriting rules.** Build **average severity** triangles — reported claims divided by reported claim counts, or paid claims divided by closed counts. A shift in mix toward larger risks or higher policy limits shows as average severity **increasing down the column** at each maturity. Splitting the triangles by segment and comparing the percentage reported for each confirms whether one part of the book is driving the shift rather than a genuine change in development.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify a development triangle capable of diagnosing the change named in part a, and to describe how it should move — increase or decrease, down the column or along the latest diagonal. Common mistakes included naming the correct diagnostic triangle but giving no explanation of how it would move; identifying a triangle that would not reveal the change in question; giving the wrong direction of change; and simply asserting that the triangle would show "changes" or "distortions" without saying what they would look like.
