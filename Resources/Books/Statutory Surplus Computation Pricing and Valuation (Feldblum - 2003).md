A Casualty Actuarial Society study note (fifth edition, June 2003) that explains the differences between statutory surplus and invested capital and the implications for actuarial pricing and valuations. It traces the computation of statutory surplus and its difference from GAAP equity, contrasts both with invested capital, and adds the capital in the policyholder reserves to the capital required by statutory regulations to determine the capital supporting the insurance policy.

## Introduction
- Insurers now use return on capital models for pricing and performance measurement, and the models differ in whether capital means statutory surplus, GAAP equity, economic surplus or invested capital.
- The idiosyncrasies of statutory accounting complicate the relation between statutory surplus and invested capital: last year's surplus plus this year's net income does not equal this year's surplus, because of adjustments such as the change in non-admitted assets and the change in the provision for reinsurance, which are direct charges or credits to surplus.

## Balance Sheets and Income Statements
- Surplus has two definitions: the balance sheet definition, surplus = assets – liabilities, and the income statement definition, surplus = last year's surplus + current year's income.
- The two are not the same if some balance sheet transactions do not flow through the income statement.

## Non-Admitted Assets
- An illustration of premium more than 90 days past due that is not admitted: the income statement entries are unchanged, while the balance sheet recognizes only the admitted portion of assets.

## The Asset Exhibit
- Exhibit 1, "Analysis of Non-Admitted Assets and Related Items" (page 13 of the Annual Statement), shows the change in non-admitted assets during the year, which is the needed adjustment to income statement surplus.
- An increase in non-admitted assets is a decrease in the admitted portion of the assets, and a direct charge to policyholders' surplus.

## The Statutory Balance Sheet
- The statutory balance sheet uses four columns to reconcile with the income statement: total assets, assets not admitted, net admitted assets, and net admitted assets for the prior year.

## Surplus Adjustments
- The negative entry in column 3 of Exhibit 1, an increase in non-admitted assets, is carried to page 4 and reduces surplus; a positive entry increases it.
- Net unrealized capital gains or losses are a separate adjustment to surplus.

## Office Furniture
- Insurers have two statutory accounting options for non-admitted assets: Method 1 writes the asset off as an expense in the income statement; Method 2 uses GAAP entries for both statements but classifies the asset as non-admitted, with a direct charge to surplus.

## Statutory Liabilities: Provision for Reinsurance
- Any transaction that affects the balance sheet but not the income statement is a direct charge or credit to surplus; the change in the Schedule F provision for reinsurance is a direct charge (credit) to surplus.

## Unrealized Capital Gains
- Unrealized capital gains are direct credits to surplus, and the change in the related deferred tax liability is a direct charge.
