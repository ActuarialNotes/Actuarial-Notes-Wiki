An earthquake model produces the following damage function for a \$1,000,000 home in California:

![Chart of damage ratio (vertical axis, 0% to 100%) against intensity (horizontal axis, 0 to 10). A solid curve labelled A rises from 0% at intensity 0 through about 10% at intensity 2, about 20% at 3.5, about 45% at 6 and about 80% at 8.5, reaching 100% at intensity 10. At intensities 3.5, 6 and 8.5, dashed vertical lines each carry a small dashed bell-shaped distribution, labelled B, straddling the curve. A long, low dashed bell-shaped curve labelled C runs along the bottom of the chart from about intensity 2.5 to 9.5, peaking near intensity 6.](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ActuarialNotes/Actuarial-Notes-Wiki/main/Media/Attachments/cas8-2017-q20-earthquake-damage-function.png)

The probability of an event occurring with a given intensity is:

| Intensity | Probability |
|---|---|
| 3.5 | 10% |
| 6.0 | 5% |
| 8.5 | 2% |

## Part a (0.75 points)

Identify what A, B, and C represent in the above graph.

### Explanation
- **A:** the damage ratio at each intensity (the damage function).
- **B:** the uncertainty in the damage ratio at the given intensities.
- **C:** the uncertainty in the felt intensity given a particular earthquake.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to name and/or describe various curves on a graph.  The graph showed a curve of the damage function (A), distributions describing the uncertainty of the actual damage to the structure (B), and the uncertainty of the forces experienced by the structure during the event (C).
Common mistakes included:

- Referring to curve C as the distribution of all earthquakes out of the model
- Referring to curve C as a combined distribution of total loss after deductible and/or reinsurance recoverable

## Part b (1.75 points)

Determine the premium for this home given the following information:

- The insurer's expense load is 20% of premium
- The risk load is set to 8% of the standard deviation of the loss

### Explanation
Read the damage ratio off curve A at each intensity and apply it to the \$1,000,000 home:

| Intensity | Probability | Expected Loss |
|---|---|---|
| 3.5 | 10% | 200,000 |
| 6.0 | 5% | 450,000 |
| 8.5 | 2% | 800,000 |
| Total expected loss | | 58,500 |

$$SD = \sqrt{0.10 \times 200{,}000^2 + 0.05 \times 450{,}000^2 + 0.02 \times 800{,}000^2 - 58{,}500^2} = 153{,}306$$

$$\text{Risk load} = 0.08 \times 153{,}306 = 12{,}264$$

$$\text{Premium} = \frac{58{,}500 + 12{,}264}{1.0 - 0.2} = 88{,}456$$

Alternatively, reading a damage ratio of 40% for the 6.0 intensity earthquake gives a loss of 400,000 at that intensity and a total expected loss of 56,000:

$$SD = \sqrt{0.10 \times 200{,}000^2 + 0.05 \times 400{,}000^2 + 0.02 \times 800{,}000^2 - 56{,}000^2} = 147{,}187$$

$$\text{Risk load} = 0.08 \times 147{,}187 = 11{,}775 \qquad \text{Premium} = \frac{56{,}000 + 11{,}775}{1.0 - 0.2} = 84{,}719$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the earthquake premium associated with a policy, calculate the expected loss and risk load, and to correctly apply a provision for expenses.
A very small number of candidates assumed that multiple events could occur in the same year, i.e. a 3.5 and a 6.0 intensity earthquake could both occur.  Credit was given when candidates completed the response correctly under this assumption.

Common mistakes included:

- Incorrectly calculating the standard deviation
- Failing to apply the risk load factor
- Applying the expense load by multiplying by (1 + 0.2) instead of dividing by (1 - 0.2)
- Attempting to calculate the expected loss by using the intensities rather than the associated loss amounts
