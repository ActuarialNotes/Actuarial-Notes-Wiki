The following graph shows the results of four techniques for estimating the ultimate claim ratio for accident year 2013, as of December 31 for each year shown.

Accident year 2013 ultimate claim ratio, evaluated as of December 31 of each year (values read from the bar chart):

| Technique | 2013 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paid Development | 55% | 95% | 94% | 125% |
| Reported Development | 120% | 105% | 96% | 95% |
| Paid B-F | 70% | 85% | 88% | 110% |
| Reported B-F | 95% | 90% | 92% | 94% |

## Part a (0.5 points)

The actuary selected an ultimate claim ratio of 100% as of December 31, 2013. Assess the reasonability of this estimate using only information known as of December 31, 2013.

### Explanation
At December 31, 2013 accident year 2013 is only twelve months old, and the four indications are spread across 65 points of claim ratio — paid development 55%, paid B-F 70%, reported B-F 95%, reported development 120%. A defensible assessment has to say *why* the methods disagree and which of them deserves weight at this maturity, not merely note where 100% falls among them.

**A supportable defence of the 100% selection.** The gap between the paid indications and the reported indications says that a great deal has been reported and very little paid. The most likely explanations are a large claim that has been reported and case-reserved but not yet settled, or case reserve strengthening — both of which mean the case reserves carry real information about ultimate cost that the paid data has not yet caught up to. At twelve months the paid development method is the least reliable of the four: the paid claim development factor is enormously leveraged, so a small distortion in the tiny paid figure swings the ultimate wildly. The reported B-F method blends the reported emergence with expected claims and so is not thrown off by a single large reported claim, which makes its 95% the most credible single indication. Selecting 100% sits essentially on top of it while leaning slightly toward the reported development signal, and is reasonable.

**A supportable rejection.** Alternatively, 100% is higher than three of the four indications and is supported only by the reported development method, which at twelve months is the most leveraged and volatile of the set. If the reported figure is inflated by one large claim or by case adequacy that has strengthened relative to the history behind the development factors, the reported development indication is overstated, and a selection nearer the two B-F methods — roughly 80% to 85% — would be better supported by the methods that carry expected claims.

Either conclusion earns credit; the reasoning that ties the observed spread to the mechanics and weaknesses of the specific methods is what is being marked.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge about the principles of basic reserving methods, including the paid and reported claim development methods, B-F method, and the Benktander technique, as well as fundamental issues to be discussed within a company regarding circumstances to consider when evaluating reserve indications.

Candidates were expected to assess the reasonability of a claims ratio selection.

Candidates did not receive credit for supporting their reasonability assessment by merely comparing the positioning of the estimate relative to the indications, thereby restating information given in the question.

A common error was supporting the reasonability assessment to a limited degree by making observations/assumptions of possible scenarios, but not connecting these observations with strengths/weakness nor mechanics of the various methods.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Discuss the relative position of the ultimate claim ratio for the reported Benktander technique versus the other techniques if the reported Benktander technique were added to the graph as of December 31, 2013.

### Answer
Between the reported B-F (95%) and reported development (120%) indications, and closer to the reported B-F — so above all three of the other plotted techniques.

### Explanation
The Benktander technique is a Bornhuetter-Ferguson calculation that replaces the a priori expected claims with the B-F ultimate itself:

$$U_{GB} = C + (1 - p)\,U_{BF}, \qquad p = \frac{1}{\text{CDF}}$$

Since the development ultimate is $U_{LDF} = C/p$, substituting $C = p\,U_{LDF}$ gives

$$U_{GB} = p\,U_{LDF} + (1-p)\,U_{BF}$$

which shows the technique is a **credibility weighting of the development and B-F ultimates**, with weight $p$ — the percentage reported — on the development method. It must therefore fall between the two, so the reported Benktander indication lies between the reported B-F at 95% and the reported development at 120%.

At December 31, 2013 the accident year is twelve months old, so $p$ is small and the weight sits mostly on the B-F ultimate: the Benktander point would plot only modestly above 95%. It would still be above both paid indications (55% and 70%).

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the basic concept underlying the Benktander technique and relate that understanding to its position relative to the B-F and loss development techniques.

A common error was providing the relative position of the Benktander indication as being between the LDF and BF methods but not discussing that this was due to the Benktander technique being a credibility-weighting between the two methods.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Identify two questions that the actuary should ask company management based on the results observed for all four evaluations of accident year 2013.

### Explanation
The pattern to interrogate is the reversal across the four evaluations: the reported indications start high and drift down toward 95%, while the paid indications start very low and end up the highest of the set at 125%. That says claims were reported and case-reserved early but settled unusually late, and that when they did settle they paid for more than the paid history anticipated. Any two distinct questions aimed at that behaviour earn credit, for example:

- Has there been any change in case reserve adequacy — a deliberate strengthening or weakening of case reserves — over this period?
- Have claim settlement or closure practices changed, so that claims are being closed more slowly or more quickly than in the history behind the development factors?
- Was there a large claim in accident year 2013 that was reported early but not paid until 2016?
- Has the claims department shifted its focus toward settling larger claims ahead of smaller ones, changing the mix of what is paid at each maturity?

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify and articulate fundamental principles of company operations which impact claims reserving.

Common errors included:

- Providing two questions that were essentially the same, such as "Has there been a change in settlement rates?" and "Have claim closure rates changed?"
- Providing questions that were not related to the graphical information provided for 2013 but more general in nature, such as "Have there been underwriting changes?"
