An actuary is developing a rating algorithm for a new product covering professional liability for nurses working in hospitals.

Characteristics considered:

- Age of nurse
- Gender of nurse
- Hours worked per week by each nurse
- Number of nurses employed by the hospital
- Specialty of the nurse (Cardiac or General)

Given the following:

- 20% of customers will switch insurers based on price.
- The company's competitor uses specialty of nurse in their rating algorithms and charges the true expected cost.
- At the start of the program the company and the competitor each write 100 policies for Cardiac Nurses and 100 policies for General Nurses.
- There are no underwriting expenses or profit.

| Specialty | True Expected Cost |
|---|---|
| Cardiac | \$500 |
| General | \$200 |

## Part a (0.5 points)

For one of the characteristics, briefly discuss two reasons why it would be an appropriate exposure base.

### Explanation
**Hours worked per week by each nurse.** Any two of the criteria of a good exposure base, applied specifically to this characteristic:

- **Proportional to expected loss.** Professional liability exposure arises from time spent treating patients. The more hours a nurse works, the more patient interactions occur and the more opportunity there is for an error giving rise to a claim, so expected losses scale roughly with hours worked.
- **Practical, objective and verifiable.** Hospitals already record hours worked for payroll, so the data exists, is objectively defined, and can be verified at audit against payroll records. It is not open to manipulation by the insured in the way a subjective measure would be.
- **Historical precedent.** Time-based exposure bases are established in professional and workers-related liability lines, so adopting one causes minimal disruption and allows comparison against existing industry data.

*Number of nurses employed by the hospital* is equally defensible on the same three grounds — more nurses means more opportunity for loss, headcount is clearly defined and verifiable with HR, and counting professionals is common practice in professional liability.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to select an appropriate exposure base and give the rationale for the selection. Common errors included offering age, gender or specialty as an exposure base — these are rating variables, not measures of exposure; listing a criterion for a good exposure base without relating it to the specific characteristic chosen; and commenting on the characteristic's appropriateness as a *rating variable* rather than as an exposure base (for example, "Legal — it should be legal to use this characteristic").

## Part b (0.5 points)

Assess if age of nurse is an appropriate rating variable using two social criteria.

### Explanation
The social criteria are **affordability, causality, controllability and privacy**. Any two, assessed against age:

**Causality — weak.** There is no direct causal link between a nurse's age and the likelihood of a professional error. What plausibly drives error rates is *experience*, and age is only a rough proxy for it: an older nurse may have just entered the profession as a second career, while a young nurse may have trained early and practised for years. A variable that correlates with the true driver but does not cause the loss scores poorly here. (The opposite case can be argued if supported: that inexperience is concentrated among younger nurses, making the relationship intuitive enough to gain public acceptance.)

**Controllability — poor.** A nurse cannot choose or change their age, so the rating variable offers no incentive to reduce risk and no ability to influence the premium charged. Rating variables that the insured can control are generally preferred on social grounds.

**Affordability** also cuts against it: if younger nurses are rated higher for inexperience, the higher premium falls on exactly the group earning the least, making the coverage least affordable for those least able to pay.

**Privacy** is the criterion age scores best on: age is routinely collected and used in insurance rating, so requiring it is unlikely to be seen as an intrusion.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify and explain two **social** criteria and assess the age of a nurse as a rating variable against them. The common mistake was assessing age against criteria that are not social criteria — the statistical, operational or legal criteria — rather than the social ones.

## Part c (0.75 points)

The company decides not to use specialty of nurse in their rating algorithm. Quantify the effect on profitability for the company after one renewal cycle.

### Answer
A loss of \$6,000

### Explanation
**The company's rate.** Ignoring specialty, and with no expenses or profit provision, the company charges every nurse the average cost of its book:

$$\frac{100(500) + 100(200)}{200} = \$350$$

The competitor, which does use specialty, charges the true cost of each: \$500 for Cardiac and \$200 for General.

**Who moves, and which way.** Twenty percent of customers switch on price, and price differs by specialty in opposite directions:

| Specialty | Company charges | Competitor charges | Cheaper | Effect on the company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cardiac | 350 | 500 | Company | **Gains** 20% of the competitor's 100 → $100 + 20 = 120$ |
| General | 350 | 200 | Competitor | **Loses** 20% of its own 100 → $100 - 20 = 80$ |

This is adverse selection in both directions at once: the company attracts precisely the risks it underprices and sheds precisely the risks it overprices.

**Profit after one renewal cycle.** The book is now 120 Cardiac and 80 General, all still paying \$350:

$$\text{Revenue} = 200 \times 350 = \$70{,}000$$

$$\text{Losses} = 120(500) + 80(200) = 60{,}000 + 16{,}000 = \$76{,}000$$

$$\text{Profit} = 70{,}000 - 76{,}000 = -\$6{,}000$$

The company started at break-even — $200 \times 350 = 100(500) + 100(200) = 70{,}000$ — so the whole \$6,000 loss is caused by the shift in mix, not by the rate level.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the impact of anti-selection on the number of insureds and the resulting effect on profit. Common errors included showing a change in only one category of nurse rather than both; using an incorrect true cost; failing to carry the calculation through to the impact on profit; and discussing anti-selection qualitatively without any calculation. Mistakenly using the rate in place of the loss cost was a further common error.
