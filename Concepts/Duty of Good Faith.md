![[Media/Figures/Duty_of_Good_Faith.svg|340]]

The **Duty of Good Faith** is the mutual obligation of insurer and insured to deal with each other honestly and fairly. It runs in both directions: the insured must disclose material facts truthfully, and the insurer must investigate, evaluate and pay claims promptly and fairly, giving the insured's interests equal consideration to its own.

The duty arises because the insurance contract is one of **utmost good faith** (*uberrimae fidei*). The insurer relies on facts only the applicant knows, and the insured relies on a promise only the insurer can perform, years later, when the insured is least able to enforce it.

| Party | What the duty requires |
| :--- | :--- |
| Insured | Disclose material facts on application, report claims honestly and cooperate in the investigation |
| Insurer | Investigate fairly and promptly, interpret the policy reasonably, give reasons for a denial, settle within limits where the insured's exposure warrants it, and not use its superior position to force a discounted settlement from a vulnerable claimant |

The duty is continuing. It applies at application, through the policy term, and throughout claims handling and any litigation that follows.

## Breach of the duty

A breach by the insured, by material misrepresentation or fraud, can void the policy or forfeit the claim.

A claimant who has just suffered a loss usually needs money immediately and cannot fund years of litigation. Canadian courts have repeatedly said that this vulnerability makes the insurer's duty more than a contractual formality. Its breach supports [[Bad Faith Damages]] and, in serious cases, [[Punitive Damages]].

Good faith regulates the process and the motive, not the answer. An insurer may deny a claim it reasonably believes is not covered, with a fair process and reasons. A good-faith denial that turns out to be wrong is a breach of contract, not bad faith.

> [!example]- Which of these insurer actions are legitimate claims handling, and which breach the duty of good faith? {Example}
> 1. Denying a claim after an investigation concluding the loss is excluded, with written reasons.
> 2. Requiring a claimant to submit the same medical documentation four times over eighteen months while paying nothing.
> 3. Offering $\$30{,}000$ on a claim the adjuster's own file values at $\$95{,}000$, knowing the claimant is unable to pay their mortgage.
> 4. Reserving rights while investigating, and defending under a reservation of rights.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Action 1 is legitimate, because the duty requires a fair process and reasons, not a particular outcome.
> > - Action 2 is a breach. Repeated demands for material already provided, with no payment and no decision, use delay as leverage, the classic pattern Canadian courts identify as bad faith.
> > - Action 3 is a breach. The insurer's own valuation contradicts its offer and it is exploiting known financial distress, the fact pattern that produces [[Punitive Damages]].
> > - Action 4 is legitimate, and it is the correct procedure where coverage is uncertain. It preserves the insurer's coverage position while it meets its [[Duty to Defend]], and failing to reserve rights can waive the coverage defence entirely.

## Quebec

In Quebec the same obligation arises from the *Civil Code of Québec*'s general duty of good faith rather than from common-law insurance cases, with substantially similar effect.

> [!trap] Treating a wrong denial as bad faith
> An insurer that denies a claim after a fair investigation, with reasons, has not breached the duty even if a court later finds the loss covered. Bad faith lies in a slow, opaque or coercive process.
