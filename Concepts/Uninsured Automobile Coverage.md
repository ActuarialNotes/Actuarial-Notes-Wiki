**Uninsured Automobile Coverage** protects an insured who is injured or whose vehicle is damaged by a driver who carries no insurance, or who cannot be identified (a hit-and-run). It is compulsory in Canadian auto policies, and it is the mechanism by which the cost of uninsured driving is absorbed by insured drivers rather than left with the victim.

- **Two triggers:** an *uninsured* motorist (identified, but with no valid policy) and an *unidentified* motorist (hit-and-run). The unidentified case usually carries additional evidentiary requirements — corroborating evidence of the other vehicle's involvement — because otherwise a single-vehicle accident could be recharacterised as a hit-and-run.
- **Underinsured motorist** coverage is the adjacent, usually *optional*, protection: the other driver is insured but for a limit below the claimant's damages, and the endorsement tops up to the insured's own limit.
- The coverage responds on a **fault basis** — the insured must show the uninsured driver was legally liable — so it imports tort principles into a first-party coverage. The insurer stands in the shoes of the absent defendant and may contest liability and quantum accordingly.
- **Cost driver:** the uninsured rate in the province. Provinces with high uninsured driving see materially higher costs in this coverage, and enforcement (plate-based insurance verification, penalties) is a public policy lever that directly affects the premium.
- Where an uninsured driver *is* identified, the insurer generally has **subrogation** rights against them personally; recovery rates are low, which is why the coverage is priced as though recovery were nil.
- Some provinces supplement this with a **motor vehicle accident claims fund**, a government fund of last resort for victims with no available coverage at all — a public analogue to a [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty fund]].

> [!example]- Which Coverage Responds? {Example}
> An insured with $\$1$ million third-party liability and an optional underinsured motorist endorsement of $\$1$ million suffers $\$700{,}000$ of damages. Determine the recovery in each case.
>
> 1. The at-fault driver has no insurance.
> 2. The at-fault driver carries the $\$200{,}000$ minimum limit.
> 3. The at-fault vehicle left the scene and cannot be identified; an independent witness saw the collision.
> 4. As in 3, but there is no witness and no physical evidence of another vehicle.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. **Uninsured coverage responds**, up to its limit. The insurer pays and subrogates against the driver personally, with little realistic prospect of recovery.
> > 2. **The other insurer pays $\$200{,}000$; the underinsured endorsement tops up $\$500{,}000$** to the insured's own $\$1$ million limit — subject to the endorsement's own terms, which in most provinces reduce the available limit by amounts recovered elsewhere.
> > 3. **Unidentified-motorist coverage responds.** The independent witness supplies the corroboration the coverage requires.
> > 4. **Likely nothing under this coverage.** Without corroborating evidence the claim fails the unidentified-motorist requirement. The insured's own **collision** coverage, if purchased, responds to the vehicle damage regardless of fault — which is precisely why collision coverage retains value even for a careful driver.
> >
> > Item 4 is the one candidates miss: the corroboration requirement is not a technicality, it is the anti-fraud design of the coverage, and it shifts the loss to a first-party coverage the insured may not have bought.
