Events A and B are mutually exclusive, and at least one of A or B is certain to occur. Events C and D are mutually exclusive, and at least one of C or D is certain to occur.

The following probabilities are known:

i) $P[A] = 0.75$
ii) $P[D] = 0.20$
iii) $P[A \cap C] = 0.55$

Calculate $P[B \cap D]$.

- A) $0.00$
- B) $0.05$
- C) $0.20$
- D) $0.25$
- E) $0.45$

## Explanation

Each pair is mutually exclusive *and* exhaustive, so $\{A, B\}$ partitions the sample space, as does $\{C, D\}$. That means any event can be split across either pair.

**Split $A$ over $C$ and $D$:**

$$
\begin{align*}
P[A] &= P[A \cap C] + P[A \cap D] \\
0.75 &= 0.55 + P[A \cap D] \\
P[A \cap D] &= 0.20
\end{align*}
$$

**Split $D$ over $A$ and $B$:**

$$
\begin{align*}
P[D] &= P[A \cap D] + P[B \cap D] \\
0.20 &= 0.20 + P[B \cap D] \\
P[B \cap D] &= \mathbf{0.00}
\end{align*}
$$

All of $D$ already lies inside $A$, leaving nothing for $B$. A probability of zero is a legitimate answer here, not a sign of an arithmetic slip: the data force $D \subseteq A$.

Choice (D), 0.25, is $P[B] = 1 - 0.75$, computed without intersecting with $D$. The whole question is the observation that a mutually exclusive, exhaustive pair lets you decompose *any* event, which is the two-partition version of the law of total probability.
