**Government and Industry Insurance Programs** are arrangements in which a government, or the insurance industry acting collectively under a legal mandate, provides, backstops or guarantees coverage that the [[Voluntary Private Insurance Market|voluntary private market]] will not supply at an acceptable price, or at all. Each exists to correct a specific market failure: correlated catastrophe risk, [[Adverse Selection|adverse selection]], the availability problem created by compulsory insurance, or insurer insolvency.

Every program answers who pays which dollar of a loss, so the loss is

> $$\text{Loss} = \text{Retained} + \text{Private layer} + \text{Program layer}$$

- $\text{Retained}$ is the insured's retention.
- $\text{Private layer}$ is the part the private insurer pays.
- $\text{Program layer}$ is the part the program pays.

For [[Social Insurance|social insurance]] the program takes the whole loss. For a backstop it takes only the tail. A residual market takes the risks, not a layer.

## Roles a program can play

A program plays one of four roles:

1. **Government as insurer**: [[Workers Compensation Insurance|workers' compensation]] boards in Canada (and monopolistic state funds in a few U.S. states), [[Public Auto Insurance|public auto]] in four provinces, [[Employment Insurance]] and [[Health Care Insurance|health care]], the U.S. National [[Flood Insurance]] Program, federal [[Crop Insurance|crop insurance]] and Canada's [[Agricultural Insurance|AgriInsurance]].
2. **Government as reinsurer or backstop**: [[TRIA]] for terrorism, the [[Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund]], and post-disaster aid such as [[Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements]].
3. **Industry residual markets**: Canada's [[Facility Association]], [[Risk Sharing Pool|risk sharing pools]] and Quebec's [[Plan de Répartition des Risques|PRR]], and the U.S. [[Automobile Plans|automobile plans]], property FAIR plans and workers' compensation pools (the [[Residual Market|residual market]]).
4. **Industry [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty funds]]**: [[PACICC]] in Canada and the state guaranty associations in the U.S., both largely funded by assessments after a failure.

> [!example]- How is this terrorism loss split between the insurer and the federal government under TRIA? {Example}
> An insurer's direct earned premium in TRIA-eligible lines last year was $\$500$ million. A certified act of terrorism causes it $\$400$ million of insured losses, and industry losses exceed the $\$200$ million program trigger. The insurer deductible is $20\%$ of prior-year premium, and the federal share is $80\%$ of losses above it.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The deductible is $0.20 \times 500 = \$100$ million.
> > - The federal share is $0.80 \times (400 - 100) = \$240$ million.
> > - The insurer retains $100 + 0.20 \times 300 = \$160$ million, which is $40\%$ of the loss.
> > - Federal payments may later be recouped through surcharges on policyholders.
> > - By capping the tail, the program lets private insurers keep offering terrorism cover, so it is a backstop to the voluntary market, not a replacement for it.

## Private delivery of public risk

Many programs bear the risk while the private sector supplies distribution and claims handling for a fee. NFIP policies are sold and serviced by private "Write Your Own" insurers, crop policies by private approved insurance providers reinsured by the federal government, and Facility Association policies by servicing carriers.

## Evaluating a program

[[Government Program Evaluation]] asks whether a program meets its objective, whether it is solvent and sustainable without hidden subsidy, whether it is efficient and stable, and how it interacts with the private market (crowd-out, complement or backstop). It also asks how the program manages [[Moral Hazard|moral hazard]]. Subsidised flood premiums, for instance, weaken the incentive not to build in floodplains.

> [!example]- Workplace injury is covered by a compulsory, no-fault program in Canada and in the United States. How do the two delivery models compare? {Example}
> - In Canada each province runs an exclusive board, a monopoly founded on the [[Meredith Principles]].
> - Canadian employers pay assessments set by rate group with [[Experience Rating|experience rating]], and private insurers write no primary workers' compensation. There is no residual market, because there is no voluntary market to fall out of.
> - In most U.S. states private carriers write the coverage in a voluntary market, alongside competitive state funds, self-insurance and a handful of monopolistic state funds.
> - U.S. employers the voluntary market declines go to an assigned-risk residual mechanism whose results are shared among writers.
> - Both keep the no-fault, exclusive-remedy bargain.
> - The Canadian model trades competition for universal availability and low distribution cost, and pushes solvency risk onto the board's funding policy.
> - The U.S. model preserves price competition, but creates a residual market whose size signals rate adequacy, and a solvency exposure to private insurers that guaranty associations must stand behind.
