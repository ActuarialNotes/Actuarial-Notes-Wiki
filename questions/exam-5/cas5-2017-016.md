Given the following information for a private passenger auto insurer as of December 31, 2016:

| Accident Year | Selected Ultimate Claim Counts | Earned Premium (\$000) | Premium On-Level Adjustment Factor to 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,025 | \$132,500 | 1.405 |
| 2013 | 3,070 | \$275,250 | 1.300 |
| 2014 | 2,950 | \$330,750 | 1.070 |
| 2015 | not provided | \$360,825 | 1.050 |

- Annual claim count trend = -2%.
- Annual severity trend = 5%.
- Accident year 2016 selected ultimate severity = \$13,370.
- Accident year 2015 cumulative reported claims as of December 31, 2016 = \$30,880,900.

## Part a (2.25 points)

Estimate the IBNR for accident year 2015 as of December 31, 2016 using a frequency-severity technique.

### Answer
IBNR $\approx$ \$8.5 million.

### Explanation
No claim count is given for 2015, so the ultimate count has to be estimated from a **frequency** selected off the older years — and a frequency is only comparable across years once both its numerator and its denominator have been put on a common level. Here that means trending claim counts and putting premium on level.

**Step 1 — put earned premium on the 2016 rate level.**

$$\text{OLEP}_{2012} = 132{,}500 \times 1.405 = 186{,}162.5 \quad (\$000)$$

| Accident Year | Earned Premium (\$000) | On-level factor | On-level EP (\$000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 132,500 | 1.405 | 186,162.5 |
| 2013 | 275,250 | 1.300 | 357,825.0 |
| 2014 | 330,750 | 1.070 | 353,902.5 |
| 2015 | 360,825 | 1.050 | 378,866.25 |

**Step 2 — trend the ultimate claim counts to the 2016 level** at -2% per year. Accident year midpoints are one year apart, so 2012 is four years from 2016.

| Accident Year | Ultimate counts | Trend factor | Trended counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,025 | $0.98^4 = 0.9224$ | 945.4 |
| 2013 | 3,070 | $0.98^3 = 0.9412$ | 2,889.5 |
| 2014 | 2,950 | $0.98^2 = 0.9604$ | 2,833.2 |

**Step 3 — compute frequency** as trended counts per \$000 of on-level premium. Using on-level premium in the denominator strips out both rate change and growth in the book, so what remains is comparable across years.

| Accident Year | Trended counts | On-level EP (\$000) | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 945.4 | 186,162.5 | 0.00508 |
| 2013 | 2,889.5 | 357,825.0 | 0.00808 |
| 2014 | 2,833.2 | 353,902.5 | 0.00801 |

2012 sits far below the other two years and is treated as an outlier; the 2013–2014 average is 0.00804. **Select 0.0080.**

**Step 4 — apply the frequency to accident year 2015 and de-trend to that year's level.** At the 2016 level, accident year 2015's premium supports

$$0.0080 \times 378{,}866.25 = 3{,}030.9 \text{ counts (2016 level)}$$

The selected frequency is a *2016* frequency, so the count must be brought back one year at the -2% trend:

$$N_{2015} = \frac{3{,}030.9}{0.98} = 3{,}092.8 \text{ ultimate claims}$$

**Step 5 — de-trend the severity.** The given \$13,370 is the accident year 2016 selection, so remove one year of 5% severity trend:

$$S_{2015} = \frac{13{,}370}{1.05} = \$12{,}733$$

Trending both pieces to the same point is what the calculation hinges on: a frequency measured at 2016 level applied to a severity measured at 2016 level, both stepped back one year together.

**Step 6 — ultimate claims and IBNR.**

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2015} = 3{,}092.8 \times 12{,}733 = \$39{,}381{,}000$$

$$\text{IBNR}_{2015} = 39{,}381{,}000 - 30{,}880{,}900 = \mathbf{\$8{,}500{,}000}$$

**Alternatively:** the whole calculation can be run at the 2015 level instead. Divide each on-level factor by 1.050 so premium is on the *2015* level (2012: $186{,}162.5/1.05 = 177{,}298$; 2013: 340,786; 2014: 337,050), trend the counts to 2015 with $0.98^3$, $0.98^2$ and $0.98$, and the frequencies become 0.00544, 0.00865 and 0.00858. Selecting the two-year average of about 0.0086 gives

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2015} = 0.0086 \times 360{,}825 \times 12{,}733 = \$39{,}513{,}000$$

and an IBNR of about \$8.63 million. The two routes differ only in the frequency selection rounding.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate claim frequency on a consistent basis — adjusting claim counts for count trend and putting earned premium on level so that growth in the book is removed — select a frequency, de-trend both that frequency and the given 2016 severity back to accident year 2015, and combine them into an ultimate from which the reported claims are subtracted to give IBNR.

Common errors included omitting the on-level adjustment or the count trend entirely; trending premium and counts to *different* periods, which makes the frequency ratio meaningless; skipping the frequency step and selecting a raw historical claim count instead, which ignores the growth of the book; and failing to de-trend either the selected frequency or the given 2016 severity back to 2015.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Briefly describe one situation where the frequency-severity techniques are useful and one situation where they are not useful.

### Explanation
**Useful — when frequency and severity are moving at different rates.** If claim counts are falling while average claim size inflates, a development method applied to aggregate claims blends the two opposing movements into a single set of factors and cannot be diagnosed. A frequency-severity method separates the pieces, so each can be trended and selected on its own and the actuary can see which one is driving the change.

A second acceptable answer: on a long-tailed line the reported development factors at early maturities are highly leveraged, so a small error in the 12-to-ultimate factor swings the estimate enormously. Claim counts develop faster and more stably than dollars, so projecting counts and severity separately reduces that leverage.

**Not useful — when the claim count definition is not consistent over the experience period.** If the treatment of claims closed without payment changes, or claims reopen frequently and are re-counted, the count triangle no longer measures the same thing year to year. Frequency and severity are then both distorted — in opposite directions — and neither can be trended or selected reliably. Significant partial payments cause the same problem for a paid frequency-severity method, since a claim is neither fully open nor fully settled.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe one situation in which frequency-severity methods are useful and one in which they are not, with enough detail to demonstrate an understanding of *why*.

Common errors were answers that were true but not responsive. Citing that a method assumption holds — "useful when the claim count definition is consistent" — only says the method can be performed, not that it is informative. "Useful for long-tailed lines" and "useful because it incorporates trend" were both too thin on their own: credit required naming the leveraging of early-maturity development factors in the first case, and the ability to handle inflation or separate frequency and severity trends in the second. Generic claims about improving insight into the claims process, with no reference to claim reporting, settlement rates or average claim values, earned nothing.
