## Part a (1 points)

Discuss whether there is a need to explicitly account for the following costs in primary ratemaking:

i. Proportional reinsurance

ii. Non-proportional reinsurance

### Explanation
**i. Proportional reinsurance — no explicit adjustment is needed.** Under a proportional (quota share or surplus share) treaty the reinsurer takes the same fixed percentage of premium and of losses. Ceding, say, 30% of premium and 30% of losses leaves the ratio unchanged:

$$\frac{(1 - 0.30)\,L}{(1 - 0.30)\,P} = \frac{L}{P}$$

Because the net loss ratio equals the direct loss ratio, the indicated rate change is the same gross or net of the treaty, and the cost need not be handled explicitly in a primary rate indication.

**ii. Non-proportional reinsurance — yes, it must be accounted for explicitly.** Under a non-proportional treaty (excess of loss, stop loss, catastrophe cover) the premium ceded bears no fixed relationship to the losses recovered: the reinsurer is paid a premium but only pays on losses piercing the retention. Premium and losses are therefore ceded *disproportionately*, so the net loss ratio differs from the direct loss ratio, and the loss development triangles are distorted as large losses are capped. If the cost of that cover is not brought into the rate level explicitly, the indication will be wrong.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to articulate how reinsurance costs need to be accounted for in primary ratemaking, and to take a position rather than merely describe the two treaty types. In a proportional contract the same percentage of premium and loss is ceded, so the primary loss ratio is unaffected. In a non-proportional contract premium and loss are not ceded proportionally, so the primary loss ratio is distorted if the cover is not considered. Common mistakes included restating what proportional and non-proportional reinsurance are without drawing the ratemaking implication, and mixing up which of the two needs explicit treatment.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Identify two sources of investment income considered in the total profit provision.

### Explanation
Any two distinct sources, for example:

- **Policyholder-supplied funds** — the unearned premium reserve, held between collection of premium and the expiry of the coverage it pays for.
- **Loss reserves** — case reserves and IBNR, held between the occurrence of a claim and its payment.
- **Investor-supplied funds** — the insurer's own capital and surplus.

The income those funds earn (interest, dividends, realized capital gains) is what enters the total profit provision alongside underwriting profit.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify two sources of investment income, and a wide variety of answers were accepted, including several not mentioned in the syllabus text. The two answers had to be genuinely distinct from one another. Common mistakes included naming *collected* premium rather than the unearned premium reserve, and naming underwriting income and investment income as the two sources — those are the two components of total income, not two sources of investment income.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Briefly discuss whether trending is necessary for the following:

i. Variable expenses

ii. Fixed expenses when using the exposure-based projection method

iii. Fixed expenses when using the premium-based projection method

### Explanation
**i. Variable expenses — no trending needed.** Variable expenses are by definition a fixed percentage of premium, so they move automatically with premium. Since premium is itself trended in the indication, the variable expense provision is carried along with it and requires no separate trend.

**ii. Fixed expenses, exposure-based method — trending may be necessary.** Here the fixed expense provision is expressed as an average expense per exposure. Whether it needs trending depends on how the exposure base behaves: if the exposure base is *not* inflation sensitive (car-years, for instance, which do not grow with inflation), then fixed expenses per exposure will drift upward with inflation and must be trended. If the exposure base is inflation sensitive (payroll, receipts), it already absorbs some of that drift — but fixed expenses can still trend at a rate different from the exposure base, in which case trending is again necessary.

**iii. Fixed expenses, premium-based method — trending is necessary unless an explicit assumption is made.** Here fixed expenses are expressed as a ratio to premium. That ratio holds constant only if fixed expenses happen to trend at the same rate as premium. Because that is an *assumption* rather than a definition — unlike variable expenses, which are defined as a percentage of premium — fixed expenses must be trended independently whenever their trend differs from the premium trend.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand when trending is necessary for variable and for fixed expenses. Variable expenses are a percentage of premium and change automatically with it, so no trend is required. Under the exposure-based method fixed expenses can trend at a rate different from exposures and may therefore need trending; candidates did not have to recognize that some exposure bases are already inflation sensitive, and an example either way was accepted. Under the premium-based method fixed expenses need independent trending unless they are assumed to trend at the same rate as premium — an explicit assumption, unlike the definitional relationship for variable expenses. Common mistakes included referring to loss trend rather than fixed expense trend, asserting that no fixed expense trend is needed based on whether the exposure is inflation sensitive, and claiming fixed expenses need no trending under the premium-based method merely because they have been ratioed to premium.
