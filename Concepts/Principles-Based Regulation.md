**Principles-Based Regulation** sets the outcomes a regulated firm must achieve and holds its board and senior management accountable for achieving them, rather than prescribing the rules by which they must do so. It is the dominant Canadian approach: [[OSFI]]'s [[ORSA]], [[FCT]], [[Corporate Governance]] and reinsurance expectations all state what must be achieved and leave the method to the insurer.

It is defined against [[Rules-Based Regulation|rules-based regulation]]:

| | Rules-based | Principles-based |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What it sets | Prescribed formulas, fixed limits, a compliance checklist | Stated outcomes, insurer-designed methods, supervisory judgement |
| Strengths | Predictable, comparable, cheap to supervise, hard to evade in the specifics | Adapts to different business models and to innovation, and cannot be gamed by satisfying the letter of a rule |

## Advantages and disadvantages

Principles-based regulation is proportionate to the firm. It is robust to product innovation, since a new product still has to meet the outcome. It also makes evasion harder, because satisfying a rule technically while defeating its purpose is itself a failure.

It is less predictable, since firms cannot be certain what will satisfy the supervisor. It is more expensive, since each firm builds its own approach. It is harder to enforce, because a breach is a judgement rather than a fact. And it depends on the capacity and consistency of the supervisor.

## The Canadian mixture of rules and principles

The Canadian framework uses both approaches deliberately. The [[MCT]] is rules-based, a prescribed formula producing a comparable ratio. ORSA sits on top of it and asks whether the formula is enough for this insurer. Pure rules would miss firm-specific risk, and pure principles would produce numbers nobody can compare.

[[Solvency II]] is the international expression of the same philosophy. It has three pillars of quantitative requirements, supervisory review and disclosure, and permits internal models subject to approval.

> [!example]- Should the regulator use a rule or a principle for each of these three concerns? {Example}
> A regulator wants to address three concerns:
>
> 1. Ensuring insurers hold enough capital to be comparable across the industry.
> 2. Ensuring insurers manage climate-related risk.
> 3. Ensuring an insurer does not concentrate its reinsurance with a single counterparty.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Capital calls for a rule. Comparability requires a common formula, which is why the [[MCT]] is rules-based: a regulator, an analyst and a rating agency must all be able to read one insurer's ratio against another's.
> > - [[Climate Risk|Climate risk]] calls for a principle. It shows up differently for a coastal property insurer, an auto insurer and a commercial liability writer, and both the science and the exposure are changing, so a prescribed climate capital charge would be wrong for most firms. OSFI's B-15 requires governance, scenario analysis and disclosure rather than a formula.
> > - Reinsurance concentration calls for both. The principle is that the insurer manages counterparty risk within its stated [[Risk Appetite|risk appetite]], because the right concentration depends on the counterparty's strength, the collateral held and the insurer's own capital.
> > - Pure principles here produce drift, so a backstop rule sets a floor beneath the judgement: [[Registered Reinsurance|registration]] requirements, collateral rules for unregistered cessions and the [[Credit Risk Margin|credit risk charge]].
> > - Rules fit where comparability or a hard floor is the objective. Principles fit where the right answer is firm-specific or the subject changes faster than rules can be written.

## Principles-based standards and the actuary

A principles-based standard shifts work to the actuary. Under a prescribed margin, the actuary applies it. Under a principles-based standard, the actuary must select, justify and document the margin. This is why [[IFRS 17]]'s entity-specific [[Risk Adjustment for Non-Financial Risk|risk adjustment]], with a disclosed confidence level, replaced prescribed [[Margin for Adverse Deviations|MfADs]].
