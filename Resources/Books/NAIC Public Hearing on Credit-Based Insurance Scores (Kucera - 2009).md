Remarks delivered on April 30, 2009 at an NAIC public hearing by Jeff Kucera on behalf of the Casualty Practice Council of the American Academy of Actuaries. They address actuarial practice applicable to risk classification and the use of credit-based insurance scores for rating and underwriting, and argue that such scores allow the insurer to better segment insurance risks for the purpose of charging appropriate rates.

## Opening Remarks
- The speaker represents the Casualty Practice Council of the American Academy of Actuaries and will address current economic circumstances, the definition of a credit-based insurance score, how insurers use such scores, and how current economic conditions have affected policyholder premiums related to them.
- Most companies now use [[Credit-Based Insurance Scoring|credit-based insurance scores]] in rating personal lines such as private-passenger automobile or homeowners' insurance; removing them would not lower overall premium but would redistribute it.

## Current Economic Circumstances
- A significant aspect of the economic crisis is the severe tightening of the credit markets, which raises questions about the use of credit rating in insurance, with the greatest impact on private-passenger auto and homeowners' insurance.
- The Academy's Risk Classification Subcommittee presented the NAIC with a November 2002 report, "The Use of Credit History for Personal Lines of Insurance", which is still relevant.

## Definition of What Constitutes a Credit-Based Insurance Score
- An insurance score is a numerical score or ranking assigned to a prospective insured based on the risk's underlying characteristics, giving a relative measure of the expected cost to the insurer.
- A credit-based insurance score uses attributes found in an individual's credit report; there is a strong correlation between such scores and the expected costs of the risk, so they are a statistically reliable tool for segmenting risks.

## Evaluation of How Insurers Use Credit-Based Insurance Scores
- Principle 4 of the CAS Statement of Principles Regarding Property and Casualty Insurance Ratemaking states that a rate is reasonable and not excessive, inadequate or unfairly discriminatory if it is an actuarially sound estimate of the expected value of all future costs associated with an individual risk transfer.
- In a 2001 survey, 90 percent of responding insurers from the top 100 personal lines companies said they used credit data.
- Insurers use scores to decide whether a prospective insured qualifies, as a risk classification factor, or to assign risks to tiers; as a classification or tiering factor, the actuary is guided by ASOP No. 12, Risk Classification.

## Discussion of How Current Economic Conditions Have Affected Policyholder Premiums Related to Credit-Based Insurance Scores
- A shift in the distribution of scores resembles any other distributional shift, and actuaries regularly review the data to decide whether the overall rate level and the rating factors remain appropriate.
- Ascertaining whether an actual shift of any significance has occurred would require a study.
