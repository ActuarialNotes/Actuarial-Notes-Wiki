Given the following information:

| Accident Year | Exposures | Ultimate Claims (\$) |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 10,000 | 1,000,000 |
| 2013 | 10,000 | 1,020,000 |
| 2014 | 10,000 | 1,040,000 |
| 2015 | 10,000 | 1,061,000 |

| Calendar Year | Reported Claims (\$) | Paid Claims (\$) | Paid ULAE (\$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 995,000 | 990,000 | 100,000 |
| 2013 | 1,015,000 | 1,010,000 | 110,000 |
| 2014 | 1,035,000 | 1,030,000 | 121,000 |
| 2015 | 1,056,000 | 1,051,000 | 133,100 |

- Case outstanding as of December 31, 2015 = \$180,000.
- IBNR as of December 31, 2015 = \$50,000.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Using the classical paid-to-paid technique, estimate the unpaid ULAE as of December 31, 2015.

### Answer
\$17,780

### Explanation
**Step 1 — calendar year paid ULAE-to-paid claims ratios.**

| Calendar Year | Paid Claims | Paid ULAE | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 990,000 | 100,000 | 0.101 |
| 2013 | 1,010,000 | 110,000 | 0.109 |
| 2014 | 1,030,000 | 121,000 | 0.117 |
| 2015 | 1,051,000 | 133,100 | 0.127 |

**Step 2 — select a ratio.** The ratio rises in every year, so a straight average of the four would already be stale by the time it is applied to future payments. Select the most recent value, 0.127.

**Step 3 — apply it to the unpaid claims.** The classical technique assumes ULAE is incurred half when a claim is reported and half as it is settled, so claims already reported (case outstanding) need only the remaining 50% of their ULAE, while claims not yet reported (IBNR) need the full amount:

$$\text{Unpaid ULAE} = \text{ratio} \times \left(\text{IBNR} + 0.5 \times \text{case outstanding}\right)$$

$$= 0.127 \times \left(50{,}000 + 0.5 \times 180{,}000\right) = 0.127 \times 140{,}000 = \mathbf{\$17{,}780}$$

*Alternatively*, selecting the four-year average ratio of $(0.101 + 0.109 + 0.117 + 0.127)/4 = 0.114$ gives $0.114 \times 140{,}000 = \$15{,}960$. Either selection was accepted, but the calculation of all four ratios must be shown so the selection is visibly supported.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute the paid ULAE-to-paid claims ratio for each calendar year, make and support a selection, and apply it to IBNR plus half of case outstanding as of December 31, 2015.

Common errors included jumping to the latest year or a weighted average without first computing each year's ratio; using reported rather than paid claims in the denominator; and substituting single-year case outstanding and IBNR figures for the December 31, 2015 balances given.

## Part b (1 points)

Fully discuss how a key assumption of the classical technique is being violated in part a. above.

### Explanation
The classical paid-to-paid technique assumes the book is in a **steady state**, and in particular that ULAE and claims are changing at the same rate, so that the ratio of paid ULAE to paid claims in a calendar year is a fair proxy for the ratio of ultimate ULAE to ultimate claims. That is the assumption the data violates.

**ULAE trend:**

| Calendar Year | Paid ULAE | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 100,000 | |
| 2013 | 110,000 | 10.0% |
| 2014 | 121,000 | 10.0% |
| 2015 | 133,100 | 10.0% |

**Claim trend:**

| Calendar Year | Paid Claims | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 990,000 | |
| 2013 | 1,010,000 | 2.0% |
| 2014 | 1,030,000 | 2.0% |
| 2015 | 1,051,000 | 2.0% |

The same 2% shows up on an accident year basis: exposures are flat at 10,000 in every year, so ultimate loss cost per exposure runs 100.00, 102.00, 104.00, 106.10 — 2% per year.

Claims handling costs are inflating at 10% a year while claim costs inflate at 2%. The paid-to-paid ratio therefore drifts upward every year (0.101, 0.109, 0.117, 0.127) rather than settling at a stable level, and *any* single ratio selected from history will understate the ULAE that will be spent settling the claims still outstanding. The estimate in part a is biased low for this reason.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify the one key assumption of the classical technique that the data actually contradicts — that ULAE and claims are changing at the same rate — and then to demonstrate the violation numerically. Full credit required naming the right assumption and quoting both the ULAE trend and the loss cost (or paid claim, or ultimate claim) trend.

Common errors included failing to name the violated assumption at all, and asserting a difference in trends without computing and displaying the two trends side by side.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Discuss whether or not the Kittel refinement will correct the issue identified in part b. above.

### Explanation
It will not.

The Kittel refinement replaces the paid claims denominator with the average of paid and reported claims:

$$\text{Kittel ratio} = \frac{\text{paid ULAE}}{0.5\left(\text{paid claims} + \text{reported claims}\right)}$$

Its purpose is to fix a *different* problem: in a rapidly growing book, ULAE is being spent setting up newly reported claims that have not yet been paid, so a paid-claims-only denominator understates the volume of claims activity actually generating the expense. Two things follow here.

First, that problem is not present. Exposures are constant at 10,000 in every year, so the book is not growing.

Second, the refinement changes only the denominator's *level*, not the mismatch in trends. Running the data through it:

| Calendar Year | Paid ULAE | Average of paid and reported claims | Kittel ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 100,000 | 992,500 | 0.101 |
| 2013 | 110,000 | 1,012,500 | 0.109 |
| 2014 | 121,000 | 1,032,500 | 0.117 |
| 2015 | 133,100 | 1,053,500 | 0.126 |

The ratio still climbs by roughly 8% a year, because 10% ULAE inflation over a denominator inflating at 2% is unaffected by which mix of paid and reported claims sits in that denominator. Correcting a trend mismatch requires trending the ULAE and claim components to a common cost level, not averaging the base.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know what the Kittel refinement does and to conclude that it does not address the issue identified in part b.

The common error was asserting the conclusion without explaining it — the refinement does not remove the inconsistency between the paid ULAE and paid claims trends, so the upward drift in the paid-to-paid ratio survives it.
