Given the following:

| Week | Company Stock Price | Index Value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$101.88 | \$263.26 |
| 2 | \$101.75 | \$263.27 |
| 3 | \$102.22 | \$277.50 |
| 4 | \$109.68 | \$305.46 |
| 5 | \$117.86 | \$334.75 |

An investor wants to buy the company's stock because the investor expects the stock price to continue to increase at the rate that it has been increasing over the past two weeks.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Identify and describe an information processing bias that may be present in the investor's expectation, and relate it to the information given above.

### Explanation
**Sample size neglect and representativeness:** investors draw conclusions from little information, ignoring the sample size of the results. In this case, the investor predicts increases from the stock prices of the past two weeks alone. This is a very small, volatile sample — it would be better to consider quarterly or yearly changes in stock prices. The changes from week 1 to 2 and week 2 to 3 were much lower, and the weeks before the latest two were stable, so the past two weeks cannot show whether the trend will hold.

Other biases the sample answers identified and related to the data:

- **Forecasting errors:** the investor puts too much weight on recent experience and makes a forecast that is too extreme given the level of uncertainty — here, looking only at the last 2 weeks to determine future prices.
- **Overconfidence:** the investor has too much confidence in his ability to select stocks, and has not noticed that this stock's performance has actually been less than the market's over the last two weeks.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify an information processing bias (IPB) that was relevant to the situation, provide the correct definition for the identified bias and relate the identified bias to the situation in the question.

Common errors include:
- The most common errors were where a candidate identified one IPB but provided the definition of another.
- Neglecting to relate the IPB back to the question.

## Part b (1 point)

Recommend and justify an action for the investor to take using the relative strength technical analysis technique.

### Explanation
Relative strength is the stock price divided by the index value:

| Week | Relative Strength |
|---|---|
| 1 | 101.88 / 263.36 (as printed) = 0.387 |
| 2 | 0.386 |
| 3 | 0.368 |
| 4 | 0.359 |
| 5 | 0.352 |

Interpretations and recommendations:

- There is a downward trend in the relative strength of the stock to the market, which indicates the stock underperformed compared to the market. I suggest the investor does not buy the stock.
- Looking at the ratio of the company's stock price to the index value, the company's stock has actually not kept up with the market, despite its seemingly strong performance. I'd recommend investing in the index instead, since it has outperformed the company and has the added benefit of being well diversified.
- The firm is consistently losing value relative to the entire market. This trend can be exploited by shorting the stock and purchasing the market index, which lets the investor gain from the depreciation of the stock relative to the market.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to correctly calculate relative strength (stock price/market index or vice versa) and show at least 3 points to determine a trend in the results. Candidates were also expected to interpret their calculations correctly and provide a recommendation about whether or not to buy the stock.
Common errors include:
- The most common error was calculating the change or growth rate week over week.
- Another common error was recommending the short sale of the stock. This is invalid as there is no indication that the stock price would decrease in the future. While short sale of the stock by itself would not be an acceptable strategy, short selling the stock and buying the index would still generate a profit under the conclusion of the relative strength, as we expect the index to perform better than the stock.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Describe the purpose of technical analysis in relation to behavioral finance.

### Explanation
Technical analysis studies recurring patterns in historical trading data to exploit opportunities where prices differ from intrinsic value. Irrational behavior causes prices to diverge from intrinsic value, but they converge back slowly, so technical analysis exploits that adjustment period.

Alternatively:

Some technical strategies rely on "momentum", the tendency for a stock price to move in the same direction for a period of time. Momentum can be partially explained by behavioral finance: it arises from investors who buy stocks that have been going up and sell stocks that have been going down, because they assign too much weight to recent results — or from biases such as conservatism (investors slow to react to new information) and the disposition effect (investors who don't want to sell the losers). Technical analysis seeks to identify and capitalize on the mispricings these errors create.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to answer this question in a way that related the two concepts of technical analysis and behavioral finance.
Common errors include:
- Failing to relate technical analysis and behavioral finance.
- Stating that technical analysis helps avoid behavioral mistakes. This is incorrect; it helps take advantage of instances where investors make behavioral mistakes.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Explain one reason why the technical analysis recommendation in part b. above may not be valid.

### Explanation
Any one of the following reasons, explained:

- The prices may already reflect all publicly available trading data (under the weak form EMH), so there are no opportunities to exploit.
- Stock prices follow a random walk and therefore cannot be predicted by technical analysis.
- The attempt to identify patterns and trends may lead analysts to observe trends or patterns that don't actually exist, so their projections wouldn't be valid.
- There is little empirical evidence to support serial correlation in short-term price movements, and the correlations that have been found are typically too small to trade on profitably.
- [Relation to a moving-average technique in part b.] The stock price might already be at a high point, so when the moving average catches up, it is because the stock price has dropped.
- [Relation to assuming the stock price will return to its average relative strength] \$118 could be the highest price, a ceiling, that investors are willing to pay for the company's stock, so it will never appreciate past it — no profit if bought at \$117.86.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to provide a reason why technical analysis may not be valid and elaborate on this reason.
Common errors include:
- Giving a reason the technical analysis may not be profitable, rather than a reason technical analysis is invalid. These responses include referencing limits to arbitrage, referencing another IPB, or providing a reason without explanation.
