A **Risk Taxonomy** is a structured classification of the risks an organisation faces, a common language that lets each risk be identified, owned, measured and aggregated without gaps or double counting. For a P&C insurer the usual categories are underwriting, reserve, catastrophe, market, credit, operational and strategic (business) risk.

A good taxonomy is **exhaustive and non-overlapping**, so the change in surplus over the horizon, $\Delta S$, is

> $$\Delta S = \sum_{k} \Delta S_k$$

- $\Delta S_k$ is the part of the change attributable to category $k$.

The variance of the change in surplus is

> $$\text{Var}(\Delta S) = \sum_{k} \sigma_k^2 + 2\sum_{j<k} \rho_{jk}\,\sigma_j\,\sigma_k$$

- $\sigma_k$ is the standard deviation of $\Delta S_k$.
- $\rho_{jk}$ is the correlation between categories $j$ and $k$.

The correlation terms are why the categories cannot be managed in isolation.

> [!example]- What is this insurer's total standard deviation, and how much does one correlation assumption move it? {Example}
> The standard deviations of the one-year change in surplus are $\$60$M for underwriting, $\$80$M for reserve and $\$50$M for market risk. The correlations are $0.5$ between underwriting and reserve, $0$ between underwriting and market, and $0.25$ between reserve and market. An inflation-shock view raises the reserve–market correlation to $0.75$.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The variance is $60^2 + 80^2 + 50^2 + 2[0.5(60)(80) + 0.25(80)(50)] = 19{,}300$, so $\sigma = 138.9$.
> > - The standalone sum is $190$, so diversification is worth $190 - 138.9 = \$51.1$M, or $27\%$.
> > - With a reserve–market correlation of $0.75$, the variance is $12{,}500 + 2[2{,}400 + 3{,}000] = 23{,}300$, so $\sigma = 152.6$.
> > - One correlation assumption moves the requirement by $\$13.7$M and cuts the diversification benefit to $\$37.4$M.

## Categories of insurer risk

Each category is defined by the *source* of loss:

| Category | Source of loss |
| :--- | :--- |
| [[Insurance Risk]] | *Underwriting* (premium) risk on current and future business, *reserve* risk on past business, and *catastrophe* risk from events that strike many policies together |
| Market risk | Interest rates, equity prices, credit spreads, currency and inflation, acting on assets and liabilities alike |
| [[Credit Risk]] | Default or downgrade of bond issuers and, above all for an insurer, of reinsurers ([[Reinsurance Credit Risk]]) |
| [[Operational Risk]] | Failed processes, people and systems, and external events |
| Strategic or business risk ([[Business Risk]]) | Competition, the underwriting cycle, regulation, reputation, the wrong strategy |

Market risk, which includes [[Interest Rate Risk|interest rate risk]], makes up [[Financial Risk|financial risk]] with liquidity risk.

Frameworks slice the categories differently. The CAS ERM framework uses four types, hazard, financial, operational and strategic, and Brehm et al. use the insurer's version, with insurance hazard in place of hazard. The NAIC [[Risk-Based Capital|RBC]] formula charges separately for asset, credit, reserve, premium and catastrophe risk. [[Solvency II]] has modules for non-life underwriting, market and counterparty default risk, among others. Frameworks also place some risks differently, such as reputation as strategic or operational. The exact slotting matters less than covering every material risk exactly once.

## Selecting a model for each category

A taxonomy is the first step in choosing a [[Risk Measure|risk measure]] and a model for each category, the "diverse risks" that [[Risk Modeling]] must integrate. A first internal model might use:

- for underwriting risk, frequency–severity models by line ([[Frequency-Severity Models]]), with [[Parameter Risk|parameter risk]] layered on as a common multiplier, since otherwise volume appears to diversify everything,
- for reserve risk, a stochastic reserving method giving a distribution of unpaid claims ([[Stochastic Reserving]]), on the same horizon as the rest of the model,
- for catastrophe risk, vendor [[Catastrophe Modelling|catastrophe model]] event-loss output, ideally compared across more than one model,
- for market risk, an economic scenario generator producing interest rates, equity returns and inflation consistently, with the inflation path also feeding claim severity,
- for credit risk, default and [[Credit Rating Migration|rating-migration]] probabilities for bonds, with reinsurer default made *conditional* on the catastrophe scenarios,
- for operational risk, often a factor or a few calibrated scenarios, supported by loss data and key risk indicators,
- for strategic risk, scenario planning rather than a probability distribution.

Proportionality: the sophistication should follow materiality. Most of the effort belongs where the capital is, usually catastrophe and reserves, and in the **dependencies**: the shared inflation driver, and the link between catastrophes and reinsurer failure. A category-by-category build is most likely to miss them.

## The four types of enterprise risk

The CAS's 2003 ERM overview sorts the risks of any enterprise, not only an insurer, into four types.

1. **Hazard risks** come from fire and other property damage, windstorm and other natural perils, theft and other crime, personal injury, business interruption, disease and disability (including work-related injury) and liability claims.
2. **Financial risks** come from prices (asset values, interest rates, foreign exchange, commodities), liquidity, credit, inflation and hedging or basis risk.
3. **Operational risks** come from business operations (human resources, product development, capacity, supply chain, product failure), empowerment and leadership, information technology, and information and business reporting.
4. **Strategic risks** come from reputational damage, competition, customer wants, demographic and social trends, technological innovation, capital availability, and regulatory and political trends.

## Pure and speculative risk

A pure risk offers only a chance of loss or no loss, such as fire, flood or a liability claim. A speculative risk offers a chance of gain as well as loss, such as an investment, a new product or a change in exchange rates. Traditional risk management and insurance deal with pure risk ([[Insurable Risk]]); [[Enterprise Risk Management|ERM]] considers both. Hazard risks are pure, but operational risks can be pure too, and some risks can be slotted either way depending on how narrowly they are defined.

## Diversifiable and nondiversifiable risk

A diversifiable, or idiosyncratic, risk is particular to one firm or exposure, so holding many independent exposures makes the average loss predictable, as with fires at homes in different places. A nondiversifiable, or systemic, risk is shared by every exposure, such as an economy-wide recession or inflation, and adding more exposures does not reduce it.

A fundamental risk, such as a flood or hurricane striking many homes in one area, affects a large number of exposures at once and is generally systemic. Private insurers often cannot take it on alone. A particular risk, such as a single house fire, affects one exposure accidentally and is usually insurable.

> [!example]- How does each of this restaurant chain's risks classify? {Example}
> A restaurant chain lists five risks, each to be classified by the four types, as pure or speculative, and as diversifiable or not:
>
> - (a) a kitchen fire,
> - (b) a rise in interest rates on its floating-rate loan,
> - (c) a point-of-sale system outage,
> - (d) a food critic's viral review that drives customers away,
> - (e) a recession that cuts restaurant spending everywhere.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - (a) is hazard, pure and diversifiable: a fire can only cause a loss, and fires at different restaurants are largely independent.
> > - (b) is financial, speculative and nondiversifiable: rates can fall as well as rise, and they move for every borrower at once.
> > - (c) is operational, pure as defined here, and diversifiable: an outage causes only loss and is particular to the chain's systems.
> > - (d) is strategic, speculative and diversifiable: reputation is particular to the firm, and a review can help as well as hurt.
> > - (e) is strategic, speculative and nondiversifiable: a recession affects every business, and spending can recover as well as fall.
> > - Only (a), and (c) to a degree, would be covered by traditional insurance.

## Why insurers fail

A.M. Best's study of property/casualty financial impairments from 1969 to 2009 names as primary causes deficient loss reserves (inadequate pricing), rapid growth, alleged fraud, investment problems (overstated assets), significant change in business, reinsurance failure, catastrophe losses and affiliate problems. Deficient reserves and inadequate pricing, then rapid growth, are the major causes. Where several causes are listed, it is not clear that any one is independent of the others. Mismanagement, often cited, can show itself in many ways, including deficient reserves and inadequate pricing.

> [!trap] Calling a widespread peril diversifiable
> A risk is diversifiable when losses are independent across exposures. A rare, severe event that strikes many exposures together, such as a hurricane, is not diversified away by writing more of them.
