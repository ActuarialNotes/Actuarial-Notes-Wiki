The following graph shows pure premium relativities produced by a generalized linear model (GLM). The variable indicates whether the risk has had a claim in the most recent prior year or not.

**Prior Claim History** — a combination chart with two vertical axes. The horizontal axis is "Claim in the Prior Year" with two levels, **No** and **Yes**. The left axis is "Number of Policies" (0 to 140,000) and is read by the grey bars; the right axis is "Factor" (0.9 to 1.5) and is read by seven plotted series, one for each model year 2012 through 2018.

Exposure volume behind each level (grey bars, read off the left axis):

| Claim in the Prior Year | Number of Policies (approximate) |
|---|---|
| No | ~120,000 |
| Yes | ~45,000 |

Fitted relativities (seven series, read off the right axis):

| Series | Factor at "No" | Factor at "Yes" |
|---|---|---|
| Year (2012), (2013), (2014), (2015), (2016), (2017), (2018) | all seven series sit on top of one another at approximately 1.00 | a tight band spread over roughly 1.28 to 1.38 |

Every one of the seven year-lines slopes upward from "No" to "Yes", and the seven lines are nearly parallel — the individual year values at "Yes" cannot be read off the graph more finely than the band above.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Describe the type of test for which the above graph is used.

### Answer
The consistency test

### Explanation
This is the **consistency test** (sometimes called the year-over-year or time-consistency test) used when validating a rating variable in a GLM. Rather than fitting one model on all of the data, the same model is fit separately to each individual year of experience and the resulting relativities for the variable are plotted side by side.

The question the test answers is whether the *signal* the variable carries is real and stable, or whether it is an artifact of a particular year's data. A variable whose fitted relativities keep the same shape and roughly the same slope from year to year is measuring something persistent about the risk; a variable whose relativities jump around, reverse direction, or show a slope only in some years is picking up noise and should not be relied on in the rating plan.

The grey bars are part of the diagnostic too — they show how much exposure sits behind each level, so the reader can tell whether an erratic-looking level is genuinely unstable or simply thinly populated.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the GLM consistency test graph, what it represents, and two other tests used in assessing whether to include a variable in the GLM or not.

Candidates were expected to know that the graph is used for the consistency test and that it reflects a stable variable to use in the GLM.

Common mistakes included:

- Not knowing what the graph represents and what it is used for
- Stating that the graph was evaluating whether a variable should be included, without any mention of consistency over years

## Part b (0.25 points)

Briefly state the conclusion that can be drawn from the above graph, using the test described in part a.

### Explanation
The variable **passes** the consistency test, so having a claim in the most recent prior year is a stable, reliable predictor and is reasonable to include in the model.

All seven year-lines run in the same direction — upward from "No" to about 1.3 at "Yes" — with essentially the same shape and slope, and no year contradicts the others. The relativity for a prior claim is therefore consistently in the neighbourhood of 1.3 rather than being a one-year phenomenon: risks with a claim in the prior year genuinely run about 30% higher pure premium, and the variable can be carried into the rating plan.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to assess what specific graph reflected – consistency of the shape/slope.

Common mistakes included:

- Not knowing the use of the graph
- Stating that the pattern was not consistent/stable because the slopes were not relatively similar

## Part c (1 point)

Describe two other tests to consider when evaluating the inclusion of this variable in the model.

### Explanation
Any two of the following tests of an *individual variable's* appropriateness (note that measures of the model as a whole are not what is being asked for):

- **Statistical significance test.** Test the null hypothesis that the variable's parameters are zero using the model output — a chi-square test on the deviance of the model with and without the variable, or the equivalent $F$-test, or a $t$-test on the individual parameter estimate. A p-value below the chosen threshold (commonly 5%) rejects the null hypothesis, indicating the variable is predictive and should be retained; a large p-value says the data does not support keeping it.

- **Standard error test.** Look at the standard error attached to each fitted parameter, or equivalently the confidence interval around each relativity. If the interval around an estimate is wide — particularly if it spans 1.00 — the level is not distinguishable from the base and the variable is not reliably estimated. Tight intervals around the estimates support including the variable.

- **Judgment / common-sense test.** Check that the fitted relativities are intuitive and explainable: the direction and rough magnitude should match what is known about the risk (here, that a recent claim signals higher expected loss), and the variable must also be practical to collect and acceptable from a regulatory and social standpoint.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge of two other tests used in evaluating whether a variable is appropriate to include in the GLM or not.

Common mistakes included:

- Listing tests that are not used in evaluating a variable's appropriateness
- Listing tests generally used for assessing the appropriateness of the GLM as a whole (not a specific variable)
- Not providing the correct explanation for a specific test they listed
- Mixing the name of a type of test with the explanation for a different type of test
