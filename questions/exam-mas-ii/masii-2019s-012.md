Fitting a Linear Mixed Model requires estimating the fixed-effect parameters, $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, and the covariance parameters for the $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{R}_i$ matrices, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Consider the following statements regarding maximum likelihood (ML) estimation and restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation.

I. If $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is known, ML produces the best linear unbiased estimator of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.
II. REML accounts for the degrees of freedom used to estimate $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ in order to produce an unbiased estimate of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
III. ML and REML estimation both use the same formulas to estimate $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.

Determine which of the preceding statements are true.

- A) I only
- B) II only
- C) III only
- D) I, II, and III
- E) The correct answer is not given by (A), (B), (C), or (D)

## Explanation

**I is true.** With $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ known, the marginal covariance $\mathbf{V}$ is known and the ML estimator collapses to the generalized least squares estimator

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \left(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{y},
$$

which by the Gauss-Markov result is the best linear unbiased estimator of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.

**II is true.** ML variance-component estimates are biased downward because they ignore the degrees of freedom spent estimating the $p$ fixed effects. REML maximizes the likelihood of a set of error contrasts that are free of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, restoring unbiasedness in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

**III is true.** Once each method has produced its own $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, both plug it into the same GLS expression above to obtain $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$. The estimates differ numerically only because $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ differs, not because the formula differs.

All three statements are true, so the answer is **D**.
