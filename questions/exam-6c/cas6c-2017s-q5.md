The Supreme Court of Canada's decisions in *Andrews v. Grand & Toy Alberta Ltd.*, *Teno v. Arnold*, and *Thornton v. Prince George School District No.57* are commonly referred to as the 1978 Trilogy.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Describe the outcome of the 1978 Trilogy.

### Explanation
Sample answers include the following:

- The Judge put a rough upper limit on non-economic loss of \$100K to protect the stability and predictiveness of legal system.
- Established a cap of non-pecuniary general damages.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to discuss the issues, outcome, rationale and implications of decisions on the Canadian cap for non-pecuniary general damages.

Candidates were expected to describe the outcome of the Trilogy of Supreme Court of Canada decisions.
A common error was confusing punitive damages with non-pecuniary damages, for example:

- The trilogy established a rough upper limit of \$100K for punitive damages.

## Part b (1 point)

In the case of *Fenn v. City of Peterborough* in 1979, the Ontario Court of Appeal determined that the appropriate figure for general damages was \$125,000. This decision was not appealed. Fully describe the likely outcome if the case were subsequently appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada.

### Explanation
Sample answers include the following:

- If the case were appealed to Supreme Court, the Judge may adjust the award to \$100K with index of inflation. The original amount of \$125K considers that the plaintiff is more severely injured than victims in Trilogy and Trilogy was 1.5 years old. However, the award is not compensatory hence should not consider the level of injury. The goal of the cap is to make life better and ensure the affordability of P&C insurance as well as stability of legal system.
- In Lindal v. Lindal, Judge Dickson ruled that it is not proper to compare the extent of injuries of victims since non-pecuniary damages are not meant to compensate. However, it is appropriate to increase the cap with inflation. As a result, the award would likely to be lowered to 100K in 1978 dollars. (Since Fenn v. Peterborough occurred in 1979, it is unlikely that the inflation rate would yield an award of \$125K.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to apply the rationale of the Canadian cap for non-pecuniary general damages to a real case.
A common error was not understanding how the level of injury and inflation should be taken into account, for example:

- General damages allowed are below the non-pecuniary cap, so this is not a concern with this case
- Supreme Court will likely allow the general damage awarded to be at \$125K because 1) the insured suffer worse injuries than the 3 injured from the Trilogy. 2) \$125K is only slightly higher than \$100K, will not be a social burden.

## Part c (0.25 points)

In the case of *Lee v. Dawson*, the Supreme Court of Canada dismissed the plaintiff's application for Leave to Appeal. Briefly discuss the implication of the Supreme Court's refusal to revisit this issue.

### Explanation
Sample answers include the following:

- They dismissed the leave to appeal which means that they probably still support the limit on non-pecuniary damages for personal injury.
- The Supreme Court refuses to look at this case probably because the court was still recognizing the rationale for the cap.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe the current state of cap.

A common error was not understanding the cap on non-pecuniary general damages became a rule of law as a result of the decisions on Trilogy while the Lee v. Dawson decision implied the cap remained a rule of law. For example:

- It implies the cap on non-pecuniary general damages becomes a rule of law.
