Given the following information:

| Calendar Year | Average Earned Premium at Current Rate Level | Average Written Premium at Current Rate Level |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | \$210 | \$212 |
| 2015 | \$220 | \$224 |
| 2016 | \$235 | \$240 |

- The projected annual premium trend = -2%.
- Fourth quarter 2016 average earned premium at current rate level = \$236.
- Fourth quarter 2016 average written premium at current rate level = \$242.
- The company uses a calendar-accident year aggregation of data for indications.
- All policies are annual.
- Rates are in effect for one year.
- The rate revision is planned to be effective January 1, 2018.

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the premium trend factor for each year using two-step trending.

### Answer
2014: 1.115; 2015: 1.064; 2016: 0.997

### Explanation
Two-step trending splits the projection into a **historical** step, measured from the data itself, and a **projected** step, using the selected trend rate.

**Step 1 — the historical trend that has already happened.** Bring each calendar year's average *earned* premium at current rate level up to the latest data point available, which is the fourth quarter 2016 average *written* premium at current rate level:

$$\text{Step 1 factor} = \frac{\text{4Q2016 average written premium at CRL}}{\text{CY average earned premium at CRL}} = \frac{242}{\text{AEP}}$$

The numerator is a *written* figure and the denominator an *earned* figure by design: the ratio carries the experience period from its average earned date up to the average written date of the most recent quarter, which is where step 2 picks up.

| CY | Step 1 |
|---|---|
| 2014 | $242/210 = 1.1524$ |
| 2015 | $242/220 = 1.1000$ |
| 2016 | $242/235 = 1.0298$ |

**Step 2 — project forward at the selected trend.** The period runs from the average written date of the fourth quarter 2016 data to the average written date of the policies that will be written under the new rates:

- 4Q2016 spans 10/1/2016 – 12/31/2016, so its average written date is **11/15/2016**.
- The new rates are effective 1/1/2018 for one year on annual policies, so policies are written 1/1/2018 – 12/31/2018 and the average written date is **7/1/2018**.

$$\text{Trend period} = 11/15/2016 \longrightarrow 7/1/2018 = 1 \text{ year} + 7.5 \text{ months} = 1.625 \text{ years}$$

$$\text{Step 2 factor} = (1 - 0.02)^{1.625} = 0.98^{1.625} = 0.9677$$

Note the projected trend is *negative*, so this factor is below 1.0 and partly offsets the historical increase.

**Combined trend factors:**

| CY | Step 1 | $\times$ Step 2 | Trend Factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 1.1524 | 0.9677 | **1.115** |
| 2015 | 1.1000 | 0.9677 | **1.064** |
| 2016 | 1.0298 | 0.9677 | **0.997** |

Other selections were accepted provided the two steps were internally consistent — for instance trending in step 1 to the average written date of calendar year 2016 (7/1/2016) using the \$240 CY2016 average written premium, which then requires a 2.0-year step 2 period from 7/1/2016 to 7/1/2018 and gives 1.098 / 1.048 / 0.981. What matters is that the date step 1 ends on is the date step 2 begins from.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate premium trend factors for each year using two-step trending. Several approaches to the current trend factor were accepted given the data provided, and candidates were expected to derive the projected trend period consistent with the approach they chose.

Common errors included using written premium in the *denominator* of the first step trend factor; projecting to an average earned date when the first step had trended to an average written date, or the reverse; selecting a historical annual premium trend far too high or too low relative to the data when using a selected trend for step 1; calculating the trend factor for only one year; and calculating the projected premium instead of the premium trend factor.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Identify two scenarios that could lead to a negative premium trend when analyzing average premium at current rate level.

### Explanation
Because the premium is already at current rate level, rate changes are excluded by construction — a negative trend must come from a change in the *characteristics of the business being written*. Any two of:

- **A shift in the mix of business toward lower-premium classes or territories.** If growth is concentrated in geographic regions or classes that carry lower rates, the average premium falls even though no rate decreased.
- **Insureds selecting higher deductibles or lower policy limits.** Buying less coverage lowers the premium per policy, dragging the average down.
- **Deflation with an inflation-sensitive exposure base.** Where the exposure base is payroll or receipts, actually falling price levels reduce the exposure per policy and therefore the average premium.
- **An underwriting shift toward better risks**, which typically attract lower rates and so lower the average premium as the mix changes.
- **An aging book of insureds** moving into lower age-based rating factors.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to give two distinct, reasonable explanations for why premium at current rate level might trend negatively. Common errors included insufficient detail — "change in limits" or "mix of business shift" without saying which direction the shift runs; citing negative rate changes, whether in the experience period or the future, when premium trend is analyzed at current rate level precisely to remove them; citing a shrinking or growing book without reference to *average* premium; giving explanations for negative loss cost or expense trend instead; and stating that a decrease in the inflation *rate* causes negative premium trend — a slower rate of inflation is not the same as deflation, which is the valid version of that answer when the exposure base is inflation-sensitive.
