Given the following information:

Reported Claims Only (\$) as of (months)

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 6,000 | 9,000 | 10,500 |
| 2014 | 7,500 | 11,250 | |
| 2015 | 9,000 | | |

Reported ALAE (\$) as of (months)

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 150 | 900 | 1,575 |
| 2014 | 300 | 1,125 | |
| 2015 | 525 | | |

- The 36 to ultimate development factor for reported claims only is 1.143.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Use the reported development technique to calculate ultimate claims only for all accident years.

### Answer
AY 2013: \$12,000. AY 2014: \$15,000. AY 2015: \$18,000.

### Explanation
Develop the **claims-only** triangle; the ALAE triangle plays no part in this section.

**Age-to-age factors:**

| Accident Year | 12-24 | 24-36 |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | $9{,}000/6{,}000 = 1.500$ | $10{,}500/9{,}000 = 1.167$ |
| 2014 | $11{,}250/7{,}500 = 1.500$ | |
| Selected | 1.500 | 1.167 |

The 12-24 factor is 1.500 in both years, so the selection is unambiguous; only one 24-36 observation is available.

**Cumulative factors,** using the given 36-to-ultimate factor of 1.143:

$$\text{CDF}_{36} = 1.143$$
$$\text{CDF}_{24} = 1.167 \times 1.143 = 1.334$$
$$\text{CDF}_{12} = 1.500 \times 1.334 = 2.000$$

**Ultimate claims only:**

| Accident Year | Reported | CDF | Ultimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 10,500 | 1.143 | \$12,000 |
| 2014 | 11,250 | 1.334 | \$15,000 |
| 2015 | 9,000 | 2.000 | \$18,000 |

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute age-to-age factors from the reported claims-only triangle, cumulate them with the given tail into age-to-ultimate factors, and apply them to each accident year's latest reported value.

Common errors included adding the claims and ALAE triangles together and developing the combined amounts, which is not what part a asks for, and developing only the most recent accident year instead of all three.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Evaluate the reasonableness of combining the reported claims only and reported ALAE provided above to estimate total unpaid liabilities.

### Explanation
Combining claims and ALAE into one triangle is reasonable only if ALAE develops on a pattern close to that of claims, or if ALAE is small enough relative to claims that a difference in pattern would not distort the combined development. Test both.

**ALAE development on its own:**

| Accident Year | 12-24 | 24-36 |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | $900/150 = 6.000$ | $1{,}575/900 = 1.750$ |
| 2014 | $1{,}125/300 = 3.750$ | |

Against 1.500 and 1.167 for claims, ALAE emerges far more slowly and far more erratically.

**ALAE as a ratio of claims:**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 2.5% | 10.0% | 15.0% |
| 2014 | 4.0% | 10.0% | |
| 2015 | 5.8% | | |

The ratio is unsettled at 12 months (2.5%, 4.0%, 5.8% — rising sharply) but identical at 24 months across both available years.

**Conclusion.** Either recommendation can be defended on this data provided it is argued from the exhibits:

- *Against combining:* the two triangles develop on visibly different patterns, and the 12-month ALAE-to-claims ratio is rising year over year, so one blended factor would misstate at least one of the two components.
- *For combining:* ALAE runs at only about 10-15% of claims, so a separate ALAE analysis would rest on very thin, volatile data; folding it into the far larger claims triangle borrows the credibility of the claims development without materially distorting it, and the ratio is stable from 24 months on.

The answer must end in an explicit conclusion either way.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to evaluate combining claims and ALAE by comparing the two sets of development factors, the consistency of the ALAE-to-claims ratios, and the size of ALAE relative to claims. Either conclusion earned credit as long as it rested on an actuarially sound argument built from the data given.

Common errors included analyzing the exhibits but never stating a conclusion, and arguing that ULAE information would be needed, which is not relevant to whether these two reported triangles should be developed together.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Assess the appropriateness of applying the development technique to the reported ALAE data provided above.

### Explanation
The question here is narrower than part b: not whether ALAE should ride along with claims, but whether the chain ladder can be applied to the ALAE triangle **on its own**.

The ALAE age-to-age factors are 6.000 and 3.750 at 12-24 and 1.750 at 24-36. Two observations that differ by a factor of nearly two, on a base of only \$150 to \$300 at 12 months, make the 12-24 selection close to arbitrary — a \$50 movement in a single cell swings the indicated ultimate substantially. There is also only one 24-36 observation and no information at all about the ALAE tail, which cannot be assumed to match the 1.143 given for claims since ALAE is clearly reporting more slowly.

Compounding the leverage problem, the ALAE-to-claims ratio at 12 months has risen from 2.5% to 4.0% to 5.8%. Something has changed — most plausibly the claims department is setting up ALAE reserves earlier than it used to — so the historical ALAE development factors describe a reporting pattern that is no longer in force and would over-develop the recent years.

The development technique applied directly to reported ALAE is therefore not appropriate here. A ratio-to-claims approach, developing the ALAE-to-claims ratio and applying it to developed ultimate claims, is more stable, though it still requires an explicit assumption about the ALAE tail.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to assess whether the development method can be applied to the reported ALAE triangle by itself, using the ALAE data given — pointing to the highly leveraged and inconsistent age-to-age factors, the small dollar amounts behind them, and the shifting ALAE-to-claims relationship.

The common error was misreading the question and simply repeating the part b answer about combining claims and ALAE.
