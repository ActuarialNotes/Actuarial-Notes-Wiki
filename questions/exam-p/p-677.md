In a certain group of medical insurance policyholders, the length of a hospitalization is a random variable modeled with density function

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases}
0.4\left(e^{-0.6x} + e^{-1.2x}\right), & x > 0 \\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

Calculate the median length of hospitalizations.

- A) $0.537$
- B) $0.905$
- C) $0.963$
- D) $1.252$
- E) $1.389$

## Explanation

Integrate the density to the median $m$ and set it equal to 0.5:

$$
0.5 = \int_0^m 0.4\left(e^{-0.6x} + e^{-1.2x}\right)dx = 0.4\left(2.5 - \frac{e^{-0.6m}}{0.6} - \frac{e^{-1.2m}}{1.2}\right)
$$

The substitution $y = e^{-0.6m}$ turns this into a quadratic, since $e^{-1.2m} = y^2$:

$$
\begin{align*}
1.25 &= 2.5 - \frac{y}{0.6} - \frac{y^2}{1.2} \\
1.5 &= 3 - 2y - y^2 \\
y^2 + 2y - 1.5 &= 0
\end{align*}
$$

Only the positive root can be an exponential:

$$
y = \frac{-2 + \sqrt{4 + 6}}{2} = 0.58114
$$

and therefore

$$
m = \frac{-\ln(0.58114)}{0.6} = \mathbf{0.9046}
$$

Choice (C), 0.963, treats the density as a mixture — it is $\frac{2}{3}$ of an exponential with rate 0.6 plus $\frac{1}{3}$ of one with rate 1.2 — and then averages the two components' medians: $\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\ln 2}{0.6}\right) + \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\ln 2}{1.2}\right) = 0.963$. The mixture identity is correct, but the median of a mixture is not the mixture of the medians; only the *cdf* can be averaged that way, which is what the integral above does.
