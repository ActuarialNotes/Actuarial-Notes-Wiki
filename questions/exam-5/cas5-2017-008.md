A company's current rating plan for fire coverage for personal property insurance only includes territory. The following GLM outputs and experience are from a recent analysis of pure premium:

**Exhibit 1 — "Number of Occupants GLM Relativities."** A combination chart over the four occupancy levels 1-2, 3-4, 5-8 and >8. Grey bars show earned exposures against a right-hand axis running 0 to 2,500; a solid line with round markers shows the indicated relativity against a left-hand axis running 0 to 2; two dotted lines with triangular markers show the indicated relativity plus and minus two standard errors. Exposures fall away sharply after the 3-4 level, and the two standard-error lines fan out as they do — narrow and nearly on top of the indicated relativity at 1-2 and 3-4, but very wide at >8, where the band spans roughly 0.83 to 1.86.

| Number of Occupants | 1-2 | 3-4 | 5-8 | >8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exposures (approx., from the bars) | 1,600 | 2,200 | 350 | 150 |
| Indicated Relativity | 0.83 | 1.00 | 1.34 | 1.28 |
| +2 Std Errors (approx.) | 0.87 | 1.13 | 1.52 | 1.86 |
| -2 Std Errors (approx.) | 0.78 | 0.88 | 1.14 | 0.83 |

**Exhibit 2 — "Number of Occupants GLM Relativities by Year."** The same four occupancy levels on the horizontal axis, with one line per accident year 2013 through 2016 showing that year's fitted relativity, on a left-hand axis running 0 to 2. The four years lie almost on top of one another at 1-2, 3-4 and 5-8, then diverge violently at >8, where the 2013 line rises to about 1.72 while the 2016 line falls to about 0.90.

| Year | 1-2 | 3-4 | 5-8 | >8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 (approx.) | 0.81 | 0.99 | 1.21 | 1.72 |
| 2014 (approx.) | 0.90 | 1.00 | 1.30 | 1.09 |
| 2015 (approx.) | 0.87 | 1.04 | 1.45 | 1.40 |
| 2016 (approx.) | 0.82 | 0.99 | 1.32 | 0.90 |

- Number of occupants chi-squared percentage (entire variable) = 3.2%

## Part a (0.75 points)

Fully justify whether number of occupants would be an appropriate addition to the rating classification plan.

### Answer
Yes — number of occupants should be added, ideally with the 5-8 and >8 levels grouped together.

### Explanation
Three separate pieces of evidence have to be read off the exhibits, and a recommendation stated.

**1. The indicated relativities show a real, monotonic effect with tight standard errors where the data is.** Relativity rises 0.83 → 1.00 → 1.34 across the first three levels. At 1-2 and 3-4 the plus/minus two standard error band is narrow — roughly 0.78 to 0.87 and 0.88 to 1.13 — and the two bands do not overlap, so those levels are statistically distinguishable from one another rather than noise around a common mean. A slope this steep across levels that are individually significant is exactly what a useful rating variable looks like. The relationship is also intuitive: more people in a dwelling means more cooking, heating and ignition sources, so more fire exposure.

**2. The relativities are stable over time.** In Exhibit 2 the 2013-2016 lines are nearly coincident at 1-2, 3-4 and 5-8. A variable whose fitted effect reproduces itself in four independent years is measuring a persistent characteristic of the risk, not a one-off feature of a single year's experience — this is the check that guards against overfitting.

**3. The chi-squared percentage is below the 5% threshold.** At 3.2% for the variable as a whole, the null hypothesis that number of occupants adds nothing to the model is rejected at the conventional significance level, so the variable is statistically significant overall.

**The >8 level is the one caveat, and it is a credibility problem, not a reason to reject the variable.** That level carries only about 150 exposures — the smallest bar by far — which explains both its very wide standard error band (0.83 to 1.86, wide enough to contain 1.00) and its wild year-to-year swing in Exhibit 2 (1.72 in 2013 down to 0.90 in 2016). Its point estimate of 1.28 also breaks the otherwise monotonic pattern by falling below the 1.34 at 5-8, which is itself a symptom of thin data rather than evidence that very large households are safer. The fix is to **combine 5-8 and >8 into a single "5 or more" level**: the merged level has enough exposure to be estimated stably and preserves the increasing relationship.

Finally, the variable is objective and easy to verify, so it is administrable and unlikely to raise the practical or social-acceptability objections that sink some rating variables — though as with any new variable, its use would need to be checked against the applicable regulations before filing.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to read the GLM output and reach a supported recommendation, resting on three things: an increasing trend in the indicated relativities with fairly narrow standard errors, consistency of the relativities across years, and a chi-squared statistic below the 5% threshold.

Common errors included calling the variable "statistically significant" with no interpretation of the exhibits to back it; recommending implementation on the strength of the narrow standard errors alone, without mentioning the slope of the indicated relativities; rejecting the variable outright because of the volatility of the >8 level, when the low exposure count there argues for grouping rather than abandonment; and doing the analysis but never stating whether the variable should be implemented.

## Part b (1 points)

Identify and briefly describe two types of insurance environments which may discourage use of multivariate methods.

### Explanation
Two environments, each identified and then explained:

**1. A heavily regulated market that mandates a different method.** Where the regulator prescribes the ratemaking approach — the standard example is California private passenger auto, where the process is built on sequential univariate factor selection and the adjusted pure premium method — the insurer must use the prescribed method whatever its own analysis shows, so there is no scope for a multivariate model. Note the distinction: a regulator limiting *which variables* may be used is not by itself a reason to abandon multivariate methods, since the technique can still be applied to whatever variables remain permissible.

**2. A market with too little or too heterogeneous data to support a model.** Entering a brand-new line or a new territory leaves the insurer with a data volume far short of what a GLM needs to estimate parameters with usable standard errors. The same problem arises at the other extreme, in large commercial lines — large-deductible and retrospectively rated accounts — where each policy is individually underwritten with unique terms, so the risks are not homogeneous enough to be pooled into a modeling dataset. In both cases judgment, univariate analysis or individual risk rating are used instead.

Two further environments would also have received credit:

- **Operational constraints.** If the insurer lacks the systems, data infrastructure or actuarial expertise to build and maintain multivariate models, and the cost of acquiring them is not justified by the expected gain, the investment will not be made.
- **Little competitive pressure.** In a monopolistic or otherwise uncompetitive market there is no rival refining its rates, so the insurer faces no adverse selection threat and no commercial reason to bear the cost and complexity of multivariate ratemaking.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify two genuinely distinct environments and briefly explain why each discourages multivariate methods.

Two answers were specifically rejected. Regulatory restrictions on *allowable rating variables* do not by themselves discourage multivariate methods, since the methods still apply to the permitted variables — the regulatory argument only works where a different method is mandated. An environment in which the variables are known to be uncorrelated was also not accepted: multivariate methods exist to account for possible correlation, including correlation that is not intuitive, so the analyst cannot assume it away.

Common errors included listing only one environment; offering two environments that were effectively the same point restated; citing regulatory limits on rating variables; and arguing that a single non-intuitive model factor is grounds for abandoning the approach.
