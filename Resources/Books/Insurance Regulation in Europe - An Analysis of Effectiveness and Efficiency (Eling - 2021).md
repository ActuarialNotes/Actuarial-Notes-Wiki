A Journal of Insurance Regulation article analyzing the current development of insurance regulation in Europe with respect to its effectiveness and efficiency. Martin Eling illustrates the increasing amount, cost and complexity of insurance regulation, analyzes some unintended consequences of that cost and complexity, discusses how effective and efficient insurance regulation might look, and ends with three suggestions for policymakers: reduce complexity, increase transparency, and conduct systematic cost-benefit analyses before and after regulation is implemented.

## 1 Introduction
- Financial regulation has grown more complex since the early 1990s, and several industry studies report that regulation is viewed as the greatest risk to the insurance industry, a gap between the goals of regulation and the industry's perception of it.
- The article makes no novel theoretical or empirical contribution but collects existing results on the effectiveness and efficiency of regulation to draw policy implications; it is not an argument for less intensive regulation.

## 2 Analysis of the Current Situation
- 2.1 Increasing Complexity of Regulation
    - A mid-size Swiss insurer active in five European countries must satisfy local GAAP, the Swiss Solvency Test, Solvency I and [[Solvency II]], a rating agency, [[IFRS]] (IFRS 4, preparing for [[IFRS 17]] and IFRS 9) and MCEV; Table 1 shows how a decline in interest rates affects assets, reserves and equity under each of the eight models managers use to view the company's financial situation, which can show an increase, a decrease or no impact on equity.
- 2.2 Increasing Amount of Regulation
    - Table 2 lists the laws, regulations and ordinances for insurers in Germany, Austria and Switzerland; recent EU regulatory projects fall into financial stability, supervision, taxes, consumer protection and other regulation.
- 2.3 Increasing Costs of Regulation
    - There is no holistic representation of the costs and benefits of regulation for insurance; the costs and staff of the German-speaking countries' financial supervisory authorities have grown, costs by 6.5% to 12.1% a year (Table 3).

## 3 Unintended Consequences
- Guarantee funds and price regulation are classic examples of regulation with unintended side effects; Solvency II's complexity may make it less comprehensible, less transparent and so less effective.
- 3.1 Investment Incentives
    - Solvency II encourages relatively undiversified holdings of government bonds, whose capital requirement is very low and based on political rather than risk considerations, which may lead smaller non-rated insurers in particular to invest heavily in riskier government bonds.
- 3.2 Reduction of Product Availability
    - Insurers may respond to regulatory requirements by transferring risks to customers (classic versus unit-linked life insurance), and regulatory fixed costs may push small regional insurers out of the market and drive consolidation.
- 3.3 Regulatory Arbitrage
    - Costly, complex regulation raises concern about regulatory arbitrage — shadow reinsurance, banks versus insurers, SIFIs versus non-SIFIs, European versus rest-of-the-world insurers; a principle-based approach requiring consideration of all relevant risks might help avoid it.

## 4 How Might Effective and Efficient Insurance Regulation Look?
- 4.1 Effectiveness of Insurance Regulation
    - Klein (1995) divides regulatory activities into solvency; prices and policy forms; market practices with respect to distribution and underwriting; and any other function, such as improving customer information.
    - Solvency: simple measures are as good as or better than complex ones for identifying companies in financial distress.
    - Prices and policy forms: research is quite clear that price and policy-form regulation is not effective.
    - Market practices: whether the Insurance Mediation Directive's documentation and disclosure requirements give customers better information is questioned, and the unisex Gender Directive may cause adverse selection and higher prices.
    - Any other function: evidence on improving customer information is mixed, since too much information leads to information overload; key information documents seem useful.
    - Skipper and Klein (2000) recommend that regulation be adequate, impartial and minimally intrusive, and that the regulatory process be transparent.
- 4.2 Efficiency of Insurance Regulation
    - There is no accepted method of analyzing the costs and benefits of insurance regulation; the OECD's 1995 checklist of ten questions for regulatory decision-making (Table 4) is one useful tool.

## 5 Policy Implications
- 5.1 Reduce Complexity
    - Simple regulation is better regulation: harmonizing and standardizing modeling approaches, simpler risk capital calculations and [[Principles-Based Regulation|principle-based regulation]] with risk-based supervision.
- 5.2 Increase Transparency
    - Publishing ratings, solvency capital ratios and key information documents, possibly through voluntary disclosure, could improve market discipline and foster competition.
- 5.3 Conduct Systematic Cost-Benefit Analyses
    - New regulation should be enacted only when its total benefits outweigh its total costs, and re-evaluated a few years after implementation; cost-benefit analyses are limited by lobbying and regulatory capture.

## 6 Conclusion
- The complexity of regulation makes insurance regulation less comprehensible, less transparent and less effective; simplifying Solvency II and harmonizing it with IFRS, publishing solvency ratios and key information documents, and using the OECD checklist might give the same effectiveness at lower cost.
