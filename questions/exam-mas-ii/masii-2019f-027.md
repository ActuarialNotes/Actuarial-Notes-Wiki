When modeling claim frequency, an actuary chooses to use the following Bayesian model:

$$
\begin{align*}
Y &\sim \text{Poisson}(\mu) \\
\mu &\sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha = 1,\ \theta = 0.1)
\end{align*}
$$

Suppose the actuary observed 10 claims from 250 policies in the past year.

Calculate the mean of the posterior predictive distribution for 250 policies.

- A) Less than 5
- B) Greater than or equal to 5 but less than 10
- C) Greater than or equal to 10 but less than 15
- D) Greater than or equal to 15 but less than 20
- E) Greater than or equal to 20

## Explanation

The gamma is conjugate to the Poisson. With $\theta$ as the scale parameter the prior mean frequency is $\alpha\theta = 0.1$ claims per policy, and the update over $n = 250$ policies with $\sum y_i = 10$ claims is

$$
\begin{align*}
\alpha^* &= \alpha + \sum y_i = 1 + 10 = 11 \\
\frac{1}{\theta^*} &= \frac{1}{\theta} + n = 10 + 250 = 260
\end{align*}
$$

The posterior mean frequency per policy is

$$
\begin{align*}
E[\mu \mid \text{data}] &= \alpha^* \theta^* \\
                        &= \frac{11}{260} \\
                        &= 0.04231
\end{align*}
$$

Note how the credibility works out: the observed frequency is $10/250 = 0.04$ and the prior mean is 0.10, and the posterior sits at 0.0423 — very close to the data, because 250 policies swamp a prior worth only 10 policies of exposure.

The posterior predictive mean for another 250 policies is

$$
\begin{align*}
250 \times 0.04231 &= 10.58 \text{ claims}
\end{align*}
$$

This falls in $[10,\, 15)$, so the answer is **C**.
