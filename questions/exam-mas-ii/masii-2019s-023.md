A process is assumed to follow a Binomial distribution with probability of success equal to $\pi$. The process is observed for 10 outcomes and all 10 of the observed outcomes were successes. A model is fit to this observed data and the prior distribution for $\pi$ in this model is given below:

$$
\pi^{-1}(1-\pi)^{-1}; \quad \pi \in [0,1]
$$

Calculate the posterior mean of $\pi$.

- A) Less than or equal to 0.60
- B) Greater than 0.60 but less than or equal to 0.75
- C) Greater than 0.75 but less than or equal to 0.90
- D) Greater than 0.90
- E) No posterior mean exists. The posterior distribution is improper.

## Explanation

The prior is the Haldane prior — the $\text{Beta}(0, 0)$ kernel. It is **improper**: $\int_0^1 \pi^{-1}(1-\pi)^{-1}d\pi$ diverges at both endpoints. An improper prior is allowed, but only if the data pull the posterior back into a proper distribution.

The likelihood for 10 successes in 10 trials is

$$
L(\pi) \propto \pi^{10}(1-\pi)^{0}.
$$

Multiplying:

$$
\begin{align*}
p(\pi \mid \mathbf{y}) &\propto \pi^{10}(1-\pi)^{0} \cdot \pi^{-1}(1-\pi)^{-1} \\
                       &= \pi^{9}(1-\pi)^{-1}
\end{align*}
$$

This is the $\text{Beta}(10, 0)$ kernel. A beta density requires both shape parameters to be strictly positive, and here the second is 0 — the integral

$$
\int_0^1 \pi^{9}(1-\pi)^{-1}\,d\pi
$$

diverges at $\pi = 1$ because $(1-\pi)^{-1}$ is not integrable there. With no successes-complement in the data, nothing tames the singularity: the posterior cannot be normalized.

Because the posterior is improper, no posterior mean exists, so the answer is **E**.
