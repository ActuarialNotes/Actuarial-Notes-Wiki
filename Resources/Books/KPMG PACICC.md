KPMG's March 2015 report for **[[PACICC]]** on what actuaries do — and could do — to keep P&C insurers solvent. Read for [[Exam 6C (CAS)|Exam 6C]] objectives A1 and C5. The syllabus assigns **Parts 1, 2, 5 and 7 (pp. 43–47 only)**.

> [!note]- Access
> The CAS cannot redistribute this report; the link on the syllabus resolves to a CAS page carrying the citation and pointing candidates to PACICC.

## The argument

- **The actuary as a line of defence.** Solvency regulation in Canada relies on the [[Appointed Actuary|appointed actuary]] as an internal check that sits between management and the regulator: independent enough to say no, close enough to see the business. The report examines how well that has worked in practice.
- **Insolvency in the Canadian P&C industry.** PACICC's own record of failures is the evidence base — what went wrong, and how much of it was visible in the actuarial work beforehand. Under-reserving and reliance on optimistic assumptions recur; so does rapid growth into an unfamiliar line.
- **The actuary's tools.** The [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion|opinion]] on [[Insurance Contract Liabilities|policy liabilities]], the [[Appointed Actuary's Report|Appointed Actuary's Report]], [[FCT|financial condition testing]], and the [[Duty to Report|duty to report]] to the board and to [[OSFI]] — the escalation chain, and where it breaks.
- **[[Peer Review|Peer review]]** as the check on the checker (see [[OSFI AA]] §4).
- **Recommendations** on strengthening the actuary's role in the solvency framework.

## Why it is on the syllabus

It is the reading that ties the professional-responsibility material to the guaranty-fund material: [[PACICC]] pays when an insurer fails, so PACICC has a direct interest in whether the actuarial safeguards work. Expect the exam to ask what the appointed actuary can do that a regulator or auditor cannot, and where the role's independence is weakest.
