**Premises Liability** is the liability of a person who owns or occupies land for injuries that people suffer because of the condition of the land or the activities carried on there. It is a branch of [[Negligence]] and the exposure insured by the premises part of a [[Commercial General Liability|CGL]] policy and by Section II of a [[Homeowners Policy|homeowners policy]].

Under the traditional common law, the care the occupier owes depends on why the injured person was on the land: an *invitee* is owed the most, a *licensee* less, and a *trespasser* the least.

| Entrant | Why on the land | Duty the occupier owes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invitee | A purpose connected with the occupier's business, or land held open to the public | Inspect, repair or warn |
| Licensee | With permission, for the licensee's own purposes | Warn of known dangers the licensee is unlikely to discover |
| Trespasser | Without permission | Not to injure willfully or wantonly |

The duty is set by the entrant's status at the moment and place of the injury. Under comparative negligence, the damages are then reduced by the entrant's own share of fault.

> [!example]- Which kind of entrant is each of these four people, and what duty did the store owe each? {Example}
> A hardware store has a cracked step on the stairs to its basement stockroom, which the manager noticed a week ago and neither repaired nor marked. The stairs are behind a door marked "Staff Only". Four people fall on the step:
>
> 1. A customer who opened the door looking for a restroom.
> 2. A sales representative from a supplier, whom the manager took downstairs to look at stock.
> 3. The owner's friend, who came by after hours to borrow a tool and was sent down to get it.
> 4. A burglar who broke in at night.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. The "Staff Only" door marked the limit of the customer's invitation, so on the stairs the customer is a licensee or, in a strict reading, a trespasser. As a licensee, the customer was owed a warning of the known crack, which the store didn't give, so the store is likely liable, subject to the customer's comparative fault for ignoring the sign.
> > 2. The representative's visit served the store's business, in an area the manager brought him into, so he is a business invitee. The store owed reasonable care to repair or warn of the crack it knew about, and breached it.
> > 3. A social visitor is a licensee even with permission. The crack was known to the store and not obvious on the stairs, so the store owed a warning and is liable for failing to give one.
> > 4. The burglar was a trespasser, owed only freedom from willful or wanton injury. An unrepaired step is not a trap set for intruders, so the store owes him nothing.

## Invitees

An invitee is on the land for a purpose connected with the occupier's business (a *business invitee*, such as a store customer or a delivery driver) or on land held open to the public (a *public invitee*, such as a library patron). The occupier must use reasonable care to keep the premises safe, which includes inspecting to discover hazards and then repairing them or warning of them. An invitee who wanders beyond the area of the invitation, such as into a stockroom marked "Employees Only", can lose that status.

## Licensees

A licensee is on the land with the occupier's permission but for the licensee's own purposes. Social guests are licensees, even when invited to dinner, because the visit serves no business purpose of the host. The occupier must warn of dangerous conditions it knows about and the licensee is unlikely to discover, and must not injure the licensee through active negligence, but has no duty to inspect for unknown hazards.

> [!trap] Treating a social guest as an invitee
> An invited dinner guest feels like an invitee but is a licensee under the traditional rules, because the visit has no business purpose for the host. The host owes a warning of known hidden dangers but no inspection.

## Trespassers

A trespasser enters without permission, and the occupier owes only a duty not to injure the trespasser willfully or wantonly. An occupier may not set a spring gun or similar device to injure intruders. In *Katko v. Briney* (Iowa 1971) a landowner was held liable for wounding a burglar with a spring gun set to guard an unoccupied farmhouse, because such force is justified only against a trespasser committing a violent felony or endangering human life.

Many states owe more to a trespasser the occupier knows about or should expect at a particular place, such as a path across a field that people regularly use. That duty includes a warning of known artificial conditions that could cause death or serious injury.

## Attractive nuisance

Children who trespass are protected by the attractive nuisance doctrine, stated in Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 339. The occupier is liable for an artificial condition, such as a swimming pool or an abandoned refrigerator, when every one of these holds:

1. The occupier knows children are likely to trespass where the condition is.
2. The condition poses an unreasonable risk of death or serious harm.
3. The children are too young to appreciate the risk.
4. The burden of removing the danger is slight compared with the risk.

A natural pond, an older child who understands the risk, or a danger that would be very costly to remove usually falls outside the doctrine.

## Abolishing the categories

Some states, starting with California's *Rowland v. Christian* (1968), have replaced the three categories with a single duty of reasonable care to everyone lawfully present or even to all entrants, and others have merged invitees and licensees. A question that names the traditional categories expects the traditional duties.

## Landlords and tenants

A tenant, as the occupier, generally owes the duties inside the leased premises. The landlord remains liable for common areas it controls (stairways, lobbies, parking lots), for latent defects it knew about and didn't disclose, for repairs it undertook and performed negligently, for premises leased for public admission, and in many states for violating housing codes. Leases usually shift some of this exposure with indemnity clauses and a requirement that the tenant name the landlord as an additional insured.

> [!example]- Who owes what for each of this guest's two falls? {Example}
> A tenant's guest falls twice in one visit. The state uses pure comparative negligence.
>
> | Fall | Damages | Guest's share of fault |
> | :--- | ---: | ---: |
> | Loose stair tread in the building's common stairway, reported to the landlord a month earlier | \$60,000 | 20% |
> | Loose rug the tenant left inside the apartment | \$8,000 | 0% |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The common stairway is under the landlord's control, and the landlord knew of the hazard, so the landlord is liable for the first fall.
> > - Her award is reduced by her share of fault: $(1 - 0.20) \times \$60{,}000 = \$48{,}000$.
> > - The landlord's own liability coverage, typically a CGL written for the building, pays the \$48,000, because the injury arose from a part of the premises the landlord controls.
> > - Inside the apartment the tenant is the occupier, and the guest, a social visitor, is a licensee.
> > - The tenant owes the \$8,000 only if the loose rug was a danger the tenant knew of and the guest was unlikely to notice. If so, the tenant's renters policy pays under its personal liability coverage.

## Insurance

On a CGL form, injury arising from the premises falls under [[Premises and Operations Liability|premises and operations]] rather than the products-completed operations hazard. [[CGL Coverage C|Medical payments]] coverage pays a member of the public's medical expenses after an accident on the premises without anyone having to establish the occupier's duty or breach, which can settle small claims before they become negligence suits.
