![[Media/Figures/Peer_Review.svg|340]]

**Peer Review** is the independent examination of an actuary's work by another qualified actuary. In Canadian insurance it most often means review of the [[Appointed Actuary]]'s valuation and reports, sometimes required by [[OSFI]] and sometimes adopted voluntarily by a board seeking assurance that the work is sound.

A peer review provides:

1. Independent challenge of methods, assumptions and selections.
2. Identification of errors and of omitted considerations.
3. Confirmation that the work meets the [[Standards of Practice]].
4. Comfort to the board and the regulator that someone with no stake in the answer has tested the valuation.

Independence is the essential feature. A reviewer who reports to the same management, or whose firm depends on the relationship, provides less assurance. The reviewer's access to data and staff, and the reporting line for findings, are settled in advance.

OSFI may require a peer review where it has concerns, such as after adverse development, on a complex or newly acquired book, or where the actuarial function's resources are in question. A required review is a supervisory signal in its own right.

## Scope of a review

The scope varies widely:

- A full re-performance of the valuation.
- A review of methods and assumptions without re-computation.
- A targeted review of specified lines or issues.

The scope must be stated in the report, because a limited review provides limited assurance and readers routinely assume more was done.

> [!example]- How should this board scope the peer review, and what should the reviewer look for? {Example}
> After three consecutive years of adverse development averaging $6\%$ of opening reserves, an insurer's board commissions a peer review of the Appointed Actuary's valuation.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The review should be a full independent re-estimate. Three years of one-directional development suggest an approach that looks reasonable but gives biased answers, and only an estimate built without anchoring on the incumbent's selections tests that.
> > - If each year were equally likely to develop up or down, three adverse years in a row would happen by chance about $1$ time in $8$, and a consistent size makes chance less likely still.
> > - The reviewer should find where the deviation concentrates. Bias in one long-tail line points at development factors or a tail assumption, and bias spread evenly points at a trend or discount assumption.
> > - The reviewer should check whether the process changed, such as claims handling, case reserve adequacy, mix or a legal development, and whether the valuation reflected it.
> > - Comparing the actuary's indicated range with the carried amount over the three years shows whether selections drifted toward management's preference.
> > - Findings go to the board's audit or risk committee and not only to management, since pressure from management is one possibility.

## Responsibility and follow-up

A peer review does not transfer responsibility. The [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion]] remains the Appointed Actuary's. A reviewer's concurrence is no defence for an opinion that turns out to be unsupportable, and the standards do not permit reliance on a reviewer in place of the actuary's own judgement. If the incumbent disagrees with the reviewer and signs the original figure, the opinion is still theirs, and the board has a documented disagreement to resolve.

The findings must be acted on and documented. The Appointed Actuary responds in writing, and the [[Appointed Actuary's Report]] records what the review found and what was done about it.

> [!trap] Leaving a finding unaddressed
> An unaddressed finding sitting in a file is worse than no review, because it shows the problem was known.
