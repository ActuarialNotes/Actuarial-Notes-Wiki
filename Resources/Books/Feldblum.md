Sholom Feldblum's CAS study note (3 October 2011) on **[[Rating Agency|rating agencies]]** and the insurer financial strength rating. Read for [[Exam 6C (CAS)|Exam 6C]] objectives C2–C4. The syllabus assigns **pp. 1–7, including Appendix A**; candidates are **not** responsible for Section 4, Appendices B–D, the formulae, or the endnotes.

## Why ratings matter to an insurer

An insurance policy is a promise to pay in the future, and the buyer cannot verify the insurer's ability to keep it. A rating is the substitute for that verification, and it is often a *condition of doing business*: a bank taking a mortgage requires homeowners cover from a rated insurer; a [[Structured Settlement|structured settlement]] annuity is bought from a rated life insurer because the payments run for decades. A downgrade can therefore remove access to a market outright — a harder constraint than a capital requirement, and one that binds well above the regulatory minimum.

## The ratings process (Section 2)

Five steps:

1. **Background research** by the ratings analyst, and submission of proprietary data by the insurer.
2. **Interactive meetings** between the analysts and the insurer's senior managers.
3. **Preparation of a ratings proposal** by the lead analyst, with additional data from the insurer.
4. **Decision by the ratings committee**, after the lead analyst's presentation.
5. **Publication** of the rating, and provision of the analysis to fee-paying subscribers.

**Public versus interactive ratings** is the distinction to hold: a *public* rating uses public data only, with no input from the insurer; an *interactive* rating adds the insurer's proprietary data and management access. The agency does not disclose its impressions from public data alone — which is what gives the insurer an incentive to participate and to disclose adverse information itself rather than have it inferred.

The assigned sections also cover the **meetings with senior managers** and the topics discussed (Section 4 is excluded), and **rating agency capital standards** (Section 5) — risk measures, stochastic models, and how an agency's capital model differs from a regulator's.

## Capital adjustments the exam asks about

Appendix A works through items an agency treats differently from the balance sheet: **goodwill** from a recent acquisition (an asset on statutory and GAAP balance sheets, but worthless in a run-off), **deferred tax assets** (which assume a going concern with future taxable income), **surplus relief from quota share reinsurance**, **holding company debt appearing as equity**, and **catastrophe bonds**. The general principle: an agency asks what the capital is worth *in the scenario it is needed*, which is not the scenario the accounting assumes.

## Rating agencies versus regulators

Both assess capital adequacy, and the exam asks how they differ:

| | Regulator ([[OSFI]]) | Rating agency |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Protect policyholders; minimise loss on failure | Inform investors and buyers about relative financial strength |
| Standard | A pass/fail minimum plus a [[Supervisory Target Capital Ratio\|supervisory target]] | A graded scale, with capital one factor among several |
| Also weighs | Risk management quality, governance | **Operating performance**, business profile, management, market position |
| Powers | Statutory intervention | None — but the market's reaction is the sanction |

A. M. Best, founded in 1899, is the agency whose surveys are most widely reviewed in the insurance industry; agencies also rate short-term debt on simpler scales, and letter rating changes should show **negative serial correlation** if the scale is used consistently.
