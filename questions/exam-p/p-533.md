This year, each of the ten employees of a company has probability 0.2 of having at least one accident. The occurrences of accidents among different employees are mutually independent.

Of the ten employees, six are in department A. The other four are in department B.

Exactly three of the ten employees each have at least one accident.

Calculate the probability that at least one of these accidents occurs to an employee in department B.

- A) $0.201$
- B) $0.500$
- C) $0.590$
- D) $0.784$
- E) $0.833$

## Explanation

The 0.2 accident probability is never needed. Because every employee has the *same* probability, conditioning on exactly three having accidents makes those three a simple random sample of size 3 from the ten — every triple is equally likely.

"At least one in department B" is easiest as the complement of "all three in department A":

$$
\begin{align*}
P[\text{all three in A}] &= \frac{\binom{6}{3}}{\binom{10}{3}} \\
                         &= \frac{20}{120} = \frac{1}{6}
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{align*}
P[\text{at least one in B}] &= 1 - \frac{1}{6} \\
                            &= \mathbf{0.8333}
\end{align*}
$$

Choice (A), 0.201, is $\binom{10}{3}(0.2)^3(0.8)^7 = 0.2013$ — the unconditional probability that exactly three employees have an accident, which the question gives rather than asks for. Choice (D), 0.784, is $1 - (0.6)^3$, which treats the three accident employees as independent draws with replacement; with only ten employees the without-replacement structure matters. Recognising that the common accident probability drops out is what makes this a one-line hypergeometric calculation.
