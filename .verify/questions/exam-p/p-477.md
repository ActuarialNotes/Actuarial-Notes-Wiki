## [F-001] Closing remark misstates how an exponential lifetime behaves
- entry_type: finding
- author: agent:validate-v1
- run_id: 2026-09-27T19:59Z/bfa2
- date: 2026-09-28
- severity: major
- status: open
- locus: Explanation, final paragraph
- claim: Because the density rises with t, a pole that has already lasted five years is *more* likely to be replaced in any given later window than a new one — so the conditional probability exceeds the unconditional, the opposite of what an exponential lifetime would give.
- evidence: Since {5 < T <= 10} lies inside {T > 5}, P[T <= 10 | T > 5] = P[5 < T <= 10]/P[T > 5] >= P[5 < T <= 10] for every lifetime distribution, so the conditional exceeding the unconditional (0.20 vs 75/400 = 0.1875) is not a consequence of the rising density, and an exponential lifetime gives the same direction, not the opposite: e.g. mean 10 gives conditional 1 - e^(-0.5) = 0.393 vs unconditional e^(-0.5) - e^(-1) = 0.239. The comparison where the rising density matters is with a new pole over its first five years: P[T <= 5] = 25/400 = 0.0625 < 0.20 here, whereas a memoryless (exponential) lifetime makes those two equal, not reversed. The keyed answer and the computation above the remark (75/375 = 0.20, matching SOA solutions PDF p.133) are right.
- source_rank: 5
- proposed_action: Delete the inference 'so the conditional probability exceeds the unconditional, the opposite of what an exponential lifetime would give', or restate the contrast correctly: against a new pole's P[T <= 5] = 0.0625 the five-year-old pole is more likely to be replaced in the next five years, where an exponential lifetime would make the two equal.
