Data are collected on 10 players on a baseball team. For each individual player, $i$, let the number of hits be denoted as $hits_i$ and the number of at-bats be denoted $n_i$. The batting average is the ratio of hits to at-bats ($hits_i/n_i$).

The following three models are proposed:

**Model X**

$$
\begin{align*}
hits_i &\sim \text{Binomial}(n_i, p_i) \\
\text{logit}(p_i) &= \alpha_i \\
\alpha_i &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 25)
\end{align*}
$$

**Model Y**

$$
\begin{align*}
hits_i &\sim \text{Binomial}(n_i, p_i) \\
\text{logit}(p_i) &= \alpha_i \\
\alpha_i &\sim \text{Normal}(\alpha, \sigma^2) \\
\alpha &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 1) \\
\sigma &\sim \text{HalfCauchy}(0, 1)
\end{align*}
$$

**Model Z**

$$
\begin{align*}
hits_i &\sim \text{Binomial}(n_i, p_i) \\
\text{logit}(p_i) &= \alpha \\
\alpha &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 25)
\end{align*}
$$

The models are fit and the posterior means of batting average for each player are plotted below:

![Combined bar and line chart over players 1 to 10: bars give each player's at-bats, rising from about 6 for player 1 to roughly 50 for player 10. Output 1 (dashed, triangles) swings widely between 0.13 and 0.50; Output 2 (dotted, circles) is flat at 0.30 for every player; Output 3 (solid, squares) tracks Output 1 but with smaller swings, staying between 0.22 and 0.45](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ActuarialNotes/Actuarial-Notes-Wiki/main/Media/Attachments/masii-2019s-q22-batting-outputs.png)

Determine which of the following are true.

- A) Model X produced Output 1, Model Y produced Output 2, and Model Z produced Output 3.
- B) Model X produced Output 2, Model Y produced Output 1, and Model Z produced Output 3.
- C) Model X produced Output 1, Model Y produced Output 3, and Model Z produced Output 2.
- D) Model X produced Output 3, Model Y produced Output 1, and Model Z produced Output 2.
- E) None of the above statements are true.

## Explanation

The three models are the three classic pooling regimes, and each leaves a distinct signature on the plot.

**Model Z — complete pooling.** A single $\alpha$ governs every player, so every player gets the *same* posterior batting average. That is the flat line at 0.30: **Output 2**.

**Model X — no pooling.** Each player has his own $\alpha_i$ with a very diffuse $\text{Normal}(0, 25)$ prior, so the prior barely constrains anything and each posterior mean sits essentially at that player's raw batting average. That is the most extreme, widest-swinging series — including the low values for the players with very few at-bats: **Output 1**.

**Model Y — partial pooling.** The $\alpha_i$ are drawn from a common $\text{Normal}(\alpha, \sigma^2)$ whose parameters are themselves estimated, so each player's estimate is shrunk toward the team mean, with the shrinkage strongest for players with the fewest at-bats. That is the series that follows the shape of Output 1 but with the swings pulled in toward 0.30: **Output 3**.

This is the credibility idea in Bayesian dress — no pooling is $Z = 1$, complete pooling is $Z = 0$, and the hierarchical model estimates $Z$ from the data.

Model X → Output 1, Model Y → Output 3, Model Z → Output 2, so the answer is **C**.
