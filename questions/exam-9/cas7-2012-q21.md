## Part a (1 point)

Describe two costs of financial distress that apply specifically to insurance companies.

### Explanation
1. **Rise in the cost of insuring policyholders.** If an insurer is deemed risky, policyholders demand a greater discount for buying a policy, reducing premium without reducing the risk assumed. The discount demanded is often greater than the expected loss from the increased risk.
2. **Regulatory capital.** State regulators require sufficient capital for the insurer to operate. If the regulator requires more capital to be held when the company is in distress, that capital is more expensive to acquire, making matters even worse.

Alternatively:

- The insurer may have to write less business, possibly forgoing future profitable business.
- The insurer may lose policyholders, who expect a discount for assuming the increased risk of being with a distressed company.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Briefly describe three other potential costs of financial distress that might apply to a firm in any industry.

### Explanation
1. Debt will be issued at a higher yield, since debt holders are less likely to be paid back.
2. Stock is sold at a discount, since growth prospects are diminished.
3. Assets are sold below market value, since the seller can't wait for the optimal price and buyers know it.

Alternatively:

1. Agency problems — the agent may not act for the mutual benefit of agent and principal.
2. Regulation and taxation — stable outcomes may minimize taxes; there could be a regulatory requirement for capital.
3. Relationships with stakeholders — they prefer stable and predictable outcomes.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Explain why firms with higher market-to-book ratios or higher research and development expenditures may gain more value from risk management than other firms.

### Explanation
R&D is very capital intensive, with long times to payout, and it doesn't produce stable cash flow, so capital is needed to keep it going. Firms that need capital benefit the most from risk management, since they need to be in solid financial shape when they go to the markets to raise it, and don't want those capital costs to be too high.

Alternatively:

These firms anticipate more growth opportunities, and so have more to lose. They are also lightly capitalized with few tangible assets, making financial distress even more costly; being riskier, raising capital is more expensive.
