An actuary is preparing a homeowners overall rate level indication by the loss ratio method from five accident years of experience, with house-years as the exposure base. Each finding below describes a distortion in the historical data. Match each finding to the adjustment that corrects it. An option may be used more than once or not at all.

## Part a (1 point)
Average earned premium per house-year is noticeably lower in the earliest experience year than in later years, although the mix of homes has not changed; a filed rate increase took effect partway through the experience period.

- A) Loss trend
- B) Loss development
- C) Premium on-leveling
- D) Premium trend
- E) Replacing actual catastrophe losses with a long-term catastrophe provision

### Answer
C

### Explanation
Premium written before the rate increase was charged at the old rate level. On-leveling (for example by the parallelogram method or extension of exposures) restates historical premium to the current rate level, so the loss ratios reflect the rates that will actually be charged. **D** corrects for changes in average premium at a *constant* rate level, such as shifts in coverage amounts; here the mix is unchanged and the difference comes from a rate change.

## Part b (1 point)
Wind losses in one accident year are four times those of any other year, almost all from a single storm.

- A) Loss trend
- B) Loss development
- C) Premium on-leveling
- D) Premium trend
- E) Replacing actual catastrophe losses with a long-term catastrophe provision

### Answer
E

### Explanation
Whether a five-year experience period happens to contain a storm says nothing about the expected annual catastrophe cost. The storm's losses are removed from the experience and replaced by a provision built from a long-term history or a catastrophe model, so the indication neither overcharges after a storm year nor undercharges after a quiet one. **A** and **B** adjust the level and maturity of ordinary losses; neither removes the volatility of a single event.

## Part c (1 point)
The most recent accident year is evaluated at 12 months, while the older years are at 36 months or later, and its reported losses are expected to keep increasing as claims are reported and settled.

- A) Loss trend
- B) Loss development
- C) Premium on-leveling
- D) Premium trend
- E) Replacing actual catastrophe losses with a long-term catastrophe provision

### Answer
B

### Explanation
Immature losses are projected to their ultimate value with loss development factors, so each accident year contributes its full expected cost. **A** is tempting because both adjustments increase losses, but trend adjusts for changes in cost *level* between the experience period and the future policy period, not for claims that are not yet fully reported or settled.

## Part d (1 point)
Average premium per house-year at current rates has risen about 3% a year because homeowners are insuring their homes for higher amounts as construction costs rise.

- A) Loss trend
- B) Loss development
- C) Premium on-leveling
- D) Premium trend
- E) Replacing actual catastrophe losses with a long-term catastrophe provision

### Answer
D

### Explanation
With house-years as the exposure base, higher amounts of insurance raise the average premium per exposure even with no rate change. Premium trend projects that drift in average premium at current rate level to the future policy period. **A** is the tempting wrong answer because construction cost inflation also drives loss trend, which is applied to the losses; the loss ratio method needs both, and this finding is about the premium. **C** handles filed rate changes, not distributional shifts at a constant rate level.
