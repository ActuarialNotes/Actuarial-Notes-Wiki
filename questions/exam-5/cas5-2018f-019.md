One year ago, an automobile insurer implemented a mobile claims reporting platform. The company anticipated that this would lead to shorter reporting patterns.

Since the mobile platform was implemented, the company has identified a shift in mix of business towards younger drivers, with younger drivers having a higher loss cost than older drivers.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Evaluate the effect on the IBNR estimated using each of the following techniques if no adjustments are made:

i. Development Technique
ii. Expected Claims Technique
iii. Cape Cod Technique

### Explanation

Two changes are in play: a **speed-up in reporting** (the mobile platform) and a **shift in mix toward higher loss cost insureds** (younger drivers). Assuming both classes are charged adequate premium, the effects differ by technique.

**i. Development technique — IBNR overstated.**

The selected development factors come from history that predates the mobile platform, so they are too high for the current reporting pattern. Applying stale, high LDFs to the latest accident years develops the reported claims too far, overstating ultimate claims and therefore IBNR. The mix shift compounds the error: reported claims in the latest years are themselves higher because of the younger drivers, so the excessive LDFs are applied to a larger base.

**ii. Expected claims technique — little or no effect.**

The expected claims technique does not use the development pattern at all, so the reporting speed-up is irrelevant to it. The mix shift raises loss costs but also raises premium, since the younger drivers are priced accurately — the expected claims ratio is unchanged and the estimate remains reasonable. (If the premium charged to younger drivers were *inadequate*, the expected claims ratio would be too low and this technique would understate IBNR.)

**iii. Cape Cod technique — IBNR overstated, but by less than the development technique.**

Cape Cod uses the development pattern to compute used-up premium. Overstated CDFs understate used-up premium, which raises the expected claims ratio, and they simultaneously overstate the percentage unreported. Both effects push the estimated unreported claims up, so IBNR is overstated. Because the estimate is anchored partly on premium rather than solely on the developed reported claims, the distortion is smaller than under the development technique. The mix shift itself has no separate effect, since accurately priced business leaves the expected claims ratio unchanged.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to recognize that two changes are taking place — a change in the reporting pattern and a shift in the mix of business — and to address the impact on IBNR for each of the three techniques if no adjustment is made, giving a directional impact (or the lack of one) with support.

Common mistakes included stating a direction with no support, identifying the wrong direction, simply saying IBNR "would be impacted" or that a method "would not account for the changes appropriately", and focusing on one of the two changes while ignoring the other. For the expected claims technique, a common mistake was failing to distinguish the loss cost impact from the loss ratio impact, ignoring the premium side of the technique. For the Cape Cod technique, a common mistake was describing it merely as a weighted average of the development and expected claims techniques without demonstrating knowledge of the method, giving a direction, or supporting it.
