The following information is available for an insurance company:

| Age (Month) | Cumulative Paid Development Factors to Ultimate | Cumulative Reported Development Factors to Ultimate |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 2.44 | 1.69 |
| 15 | 2.00 | 1.46 |
| 18 | 1.65 | 1.38 |
| 21 | 1.49 | 1.30 |
| 24 | 1.38 | 1.22 |

**Accident year 2017 as of March 31, 2018:**

| | |
|---|---|
| 2,400 | Reported claims (\$) |
| 1,820 | Paid claims (\$) |
| 3,300 | Selected ultimate claims (\$) |

**Accident year 2017 as of May 31, 2018:**

| | |
|---|---|
| 2,750 | Reported claims (\$) |
| 2,050 | Paid claims (\$) |

- Loss emergence between evaluation points is linear.

## Part a (1.25 points)

Considering the data through March 31, 2018, compare the cumulative expected reported claims to the actual reported claims as of May 31, 2018 for accident year 2017.

### Answer

Expected \$2,476 versus actual \$2,750 — actual reported claims are about \$274 higher than expected.

### Explanation

Accident year 2017 is at **15 months** as of March 31, 2018 and at **17 months** as of May 31, 2018, so the reporting pattern must be interpolated between the 15- and 18-month evaluations. Interpolate the **percent reported**, not the development factors:

$$\%\text{rep}_{15} = \frac{1}{1.46} = 68.49\%, \qquad \%\text{rep}_{18} = \frac{1}{1.38} = 72.46\%$$

$$\%\text{rep}_{17} = 68.49\% + \tfrac{2}{3}(72.46\% - 68.49\%) = 71.14\%$$

The expected emergence over the two months is that share of the claims still *unreported* at 15 months:

$$\text{Expected incremental} = (3{,}300 - 2{,}400) \times \frac{71.14\% - 68.49\%}{1 - 68.49\%} = 900 \times 0.0840 = 75.6$$

$$\text{Cumulative expected reported} = 2{,}400 + 75.6 = 2{,}475.6$$

Actual reported claims are \$2,750 — **\$274 more than expected**, a substantially unfavourable emergence.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to calculate expected cumulative reported claims for the accident year and compare them to actual, which required interpolating the reporting pattern over a two-month period and adding the expected incremental emergence to the inception-to-date reported amount.

Common mistakes included failing to recognize the need to interpolate between quarters, interpolating the cumulative development factors rather than the percent reported, applying the expected emergence percentage to the selected ultimate rather than to the unreported amount, leaving the answer as an incremental figure instead of a cumulative one, and failing to actually compare actual with expected.

## Part b (1.25 points)

Considering the data through March 31, 2018, compare the cumulative expected paid claims to the actual paid claims as of May 31, 2018 for accident year 2017.

### Answer

Expected \$2,029 versus actual \$2,050 — actual paid claims are about \$21 higher, essentially in line with expectation.

### Explanation

The same interpolation on the payment pattern:

$$\%\text{paid}_{15} = \frac{1}{2.00} = 50.00\%, \qquad \%\text{paid}_{18} = \frac{1}{1.65} = 60.61\%$$

$$\%\text{paid}_{17} = 50.00\% + \tfrac{2}{3}(60.61\% - 50.00\%) = 57.07\%$$

$$\text{Expected incremental} = (3{,}300 - 1{,}820) \times \frac{57.07\% - 50.00\%}{1 - 50.00\%} = 1{,}480 \times 0.1414 = 209.3$$

$$\text{Cumulative expected paid} = 1{,}820 + 209.3 = 2{,}029.3$$

Actual paid claims of \$2,050 exceed this by only about \$21 — a very close match.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to calculate expected cumulative paid claims and compare them to actual, interpolating the payment pattern over the two-month period and adding the expected incremental payment to the inception-to-date paid amount.

Common mistakes mirrored those in Part a: not interpolating, interpolating the cumulative development factors rather than the percent paid, applying the emergence percentage to the selected ultimate rather than to the unpaid amount, leaving the figure incremental, and not comparing actual to expected.

## Part c (0.5 point)

Describe a situation in which the actuary **would** revise the March 31, 2018 estimate of ultimate claims given the results calculated in parts a. and b. above.

### Explanation

Paid claims emerged as expected, but reported claims came in \$274 above expectation — so the excess is in case reserves rather than payments. The actuary **would** revise the ultimate upward if that excess reflects genuinely more claim dollars: for example, if a **large loss or catastrophe claim** occurred during April–May 2018 that was reserved at a level beyond what the existing IBNR provision anticipated. That is real, unanticipated loss activity, and the selected ultimate of \$3,300 should be increased to reflect it — even if the event is a one-off that would be excluded when setting expectations for future accident years.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to describe a situation in which the actuary would revise the estimate of ultimate claims given the actual versus expected results.

A common mistake was arguing that worse-than-expected emergence *caused by organizational changes* — case reserve strengthening or a speed-up in payments — is grounds for increasing the ultimate. Those changes affect the timing of case revisions and payments, not necessarily the ultimate claims.

## Part d (0.5 point)

Describe a situation in which the actuary **would not** revise the March 31, 2018 estimate of ultimate claims given the results calculated in parts a. and b. above.

### Explanation

The actuary **would not** revise the estimate if the unfavourable reported emergence is explained by a recent **strengthening of case reserve adequacy**. That interpretation fits the pattern seen here: paid claims track expectation almost exactly while reported claims run well ahead, which is what happens when the claims department raises case reserves on known claims without any change in the underlying claim costs. The reported claims are simply arriving at their ultimate level sooner, so the timing of the emergence has shifted but the ultimate has not — the existing \$3,300 selection stands, though the reporting pattern used going forward would need to be revisited.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to describe a situation in which the actuary would not revise the estimate of ultimate claims.

A common mistake was arguing that worse-than-expected emergence caused by a large loss would not require an increase to the ultimate. While a one-time shock loss may reasonably be excluded when setting ultimates for *future* accident years, the question concerns the selected ultimate for accident year 2017 itself, so the large loss should be reflected through an increase to that selection.
