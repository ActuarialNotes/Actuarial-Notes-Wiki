An article in the NAIC's Journal of Insurance Regulation (Fall/Winter 2009, Vol. 28, No. 1) by the NAIC's chief executive, Therese M. Vaughan. It summarizes theories often used to explain regulatory failure and examines the unique structure of U.S. insurance regulation within the context of those theories, in light of the recent financial turmoil.

## Abstract
- Policymakers and scholars are asking what role regulation played in creating the recent financial turmoil and how the structure of financial services regulation should change to prevent similar crises.
- Policymakers argue the U.S. needs an increased focus on systemic risk and a resolution authority for systemically risky institutions.

## Introduction
- Before recent years, the main criticism of the U.S. regulatory system was the cost of dealing with multiple states; critics argued that a single federal regulator would increase national uniformity and avoid duplicative and overlapping regulation.
- Recent events are a reminder that efficiency is not the only important aspect of financial regulation: financial regulators have admitted mistakes in supervising institutions and markets, and the number of failures and bailouts in the insurance sector appears minimal in comparison.
- The main test of regulation's success is its effectiveness in achieving its objectives in protecting policyholders, depositors, investors and the economy generally.

## Why Regulation Fails
- The simplest explanation for regulatory failure is that regulators are human and make mistakes.
- Regulatory Forbearance
    - Regulatory forbearance is the failure to take prompt and stringent action in the face of a potentially troubled firm, that is, to delay regulatory intervention.
    - A failure to act in a timely manner can increase the ultimate size of the deficit when the institution does fail, because a firm in danger of failing tends to "bet the farm" by increasing its risk-taking.
- Regulatory Capture
    - Regulatory capture is the tendency for regulators to take the mindset of an interest group, either because of the interest group's influence or because of political interference; the typical example is capture by the regulated industry.
- A system that relies on a single omniscient regulator will ultimately fail; in U.S. insurance regulation the answer to "Who watches the regulators?" is that the regulators watch each other.

## The Structure of Insurance Regulation in the United States
- Insurance regulation is a state-based system: each state has a chief insurance regulator charged with enforcing that state's regulatory laws, and in 1871 the state regulators established the NAIC as a national forum for cooperative activity.
- Through the NAIC's Financial Regulation and Solvency Accreditation Program, solvency regulation is largely uniform in the U.S. and states undergo regular on-site reviews.
- A key strength of the system is a set of checks and balances that limit regulatory failures: duplication, peer pressure and diversity of perspective.
- Duplication
    - The Martin Frankel insurance fraud of the 1990s was uncovered because of the duplication in the system, and no policyholders lost any funds; the SEC's failure to detect the Madoff fraud let it grow to over $10 billion.
    - The NAIC serves as the coordinating role in U.S. insurance regulation, and a lead state regulator is designated for every U.S. insurance group.
- Peer Review and Peer Pressure
    - The NAIC's Financial Analysis Division performs ongoing financial analysis of all nationally significant insurers, referring unusual findings to the Financial Analysis Working Group (FAWG), a group of 16 of the most experienced financial regulators.
    - The accreditation program, established in 1990, subjects each state to an on-site evaluation by an NAIC accreditation team every five years, with off-site reviews annually.
    - Peer pressure acts as a counterweight to the pressures on a regulator not to act, countering regulatory forbearance and regulatory capture.
- Diversity of Perspective and a Search for Compromise
    - Because of the multitude of diverse perspectives in the U.S. regulatory system, it is less likely to tend toward the extreme, and a dogmatic move toward excessive deregulation or over-regulation is highly unlikely in the state-based system.
- Market Discipline and Moral Hazard
    - An often unnoticed advantage of a state regulatory system is the difficulty it has in accessing federal government funds, which decreases the likelihood of bailouts and the [[Moral Hazard|moral hazard]] problems that accompany a market expectation of bailouts.
    - The state [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty fund]] system provides a level of protection for individuals and small businesses on insurer insolvency but is not designed to satisfy the needs of large policyholders.

## Lessons from State Insurance Regulation
- Building an effective system requires four elements, all aimed at emphasizing coordination and cooperation: regulators' confidence in each other, free sharing of information among supervisors, the ability to take action if dissatisfied with another supervisor's actions, and a credible mechanism for resolving situations where assets are insufficient to fund all of the claims.

## Lessons for State Insurance Regulation
- Targeted improvements identified by U.S. regulators include revisiting reliance on rating agencies in the risk-based capital system, new reporting requirements around securities lending, and more attention to the impact of unregulated affiliates on insurance companies.
- The downside of the checks and balances is the additional cost of multiple regulators with potentially different standards and processes.
- In February 2009 the NAIC adopted five principles for a nationwide system of insurance regulation that promotes uniformity and reciprocity.
