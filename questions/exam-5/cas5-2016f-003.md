Given the following data for an insured:

| Report Year | Lag 0 | Lag 1 | Lag 2 | Lag 3 | Lag 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | \$75,300 | \$84,000 | \$62,400 | \$59,000 | \$39,800 |
| 2012 | \$65,000 | \$63,200 | \$84,000 | \$80,200 | \$62,100 |
| 2013 | \$82,100 | \$49,900 | \$55,000 | \$60,600 | \$72,300 |
| 2014 | \$90,000 | \$77,000 | \$104,300 | \$45,000 | \$88,300 |
| 2015 | \$71,800 | \$89,000 | \$62,000 | \$91,500 | \$46,600 |

(Table header in the booklet: "Reported Loss (\$) by Report Year Lag", with lag columns 0 through 4.)

- Policies run from January 1 through December 31.
- The insured's coverage changed from occurrence to claims-made on January 1, 2013 with a retroactive date of January 1, 2013.

## Part a (0.25 points)

Calculate the reported losses for the 2012 occurrence policy as of December 31, 2015.

### Answer
\$310,700

### Explanation
The lag is the number of years between the year the loss **occurred** and the year it was **reported**, so a cell in report year $R$ at lag $L$ holds losses that occurred in accident year $R - L$.

An occurrence policy covers every loss *occurring* during its term, whenever reported. The 2012 occurrence policy therefore picks up all cells with $R - L = 2012$ — the diagonal running down and to the right — restricted to report years through 2015, since the valuation is as of December 31, 2015:

| Report year | Lag | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 0 | \$65,000 |
| 2013 | 1 | \$49,900 |
| 2014 | 2 | \$104,300 |
| 2015 | 3 | \$91,500 |

$$65{,}000 + 49{,}900 + 104{,}300 + 91{,}500 = \mathbf{\$310{,}700}$$

Losses occurring in 2012 but reported in 2016 or later (lag 4 and beyond) have not yet emerged at the valuation date and are excluded.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know that an occurrence policy responds to losses by accident year, and so to sum the diagonal of the report-year/lag triangle rather than a row.

A common error was summing across the 2012 row — that is, summing by report year rather than by accident year.

## Part b (0.25 points)

Calculate the reported losses for the 2014 claims-made policy as of December 31, 2015.

### Answer
\$167,000

### Explanation
A claims-made policy covers claims **reported** during its term, so it responds to report year 2014 — the 2014 row. The **retroactive date of January 1, 2013** then restricts coverage to claims whose occurrence date is on or after that date, which cuts the row off at lag 1:

| Report year | Lag | Accident year | Covered? | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 0 | 2014 | Yes | \$90,000 |
| 2014 | 1 | 2013 | Yes | \$77,000 |
| 2014 | 2 | 2012 | No — before retroactive date | — |
| 2014 | 3 | 2011 | No — before retroactive date | — |
| 2014 | 4 | 2010 | No — before retroactive date | — |

$$90{,}000 + 77{,}000 = \mathbf{\$167{,}000}$$

Because the policy is written on a report-year basis, its losses are fully reported at the end of the policy period — there is no further reporting-lag emergence to wait for, which is why the December 31, 2015 valuation does not truncate anything here.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know that a claims-made policy responds by report year and that a retroactive date excludes claims occurring before it.

A common error was ignoring the retroactive date and summing the entire 2014 row across all report lags.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Describe how a switch from occurrence to claims-made coverage could affect an insurer's loss reserve risk.

### Explanation
The switch **reduces** loss reserve risk.

Under an occurrence policy the insurer must hold IBNR for claims that have already occurred but have not yet been reported — an estimate that reaches years past the end of the policy period and depends on assumptions about the reporting pattern. A claims-made policy, by contrast, carries essentially **no pure IBNR**: every claim it will ever cover has already been reported by the time the policy expires, so the only remaining uncertainty is development on known claims.

Removing the reporting-lag component does two things to the reserve estimate. It **shortens the tail**, so the estimate projects a shorter distance into the future and is less exposed to changes in inflation, legal environment or claim handling over that horizon. And it strips out one of the two sources of estimation error entirely: an occurrence reserve carries both report-lag risk and development risk, while a claims-made reserve carries development risk only. Both effects reduce the variability of the estimate, i.e. reserve risk.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify the primary reserving difference between claims-made and occurrence coverage — the absence of IBNR and the shorter tail — and then explicitly connect that difference to reserve *risk*.

Common errors included describing the difference without ever stating the effect on reserve risk, and discussing the size of the loss reserve in dollars rather than the risk (uncertainty) attaching to it.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Describe how a switch from occurrence to claims-made coverage could affect the target underwriting profit provision.

### Explanation
Either direction is defensible, provided the reason is stated and the direction follows from it. The two standard arguments:

**Investment income falls, so the target underwriting profit provision must rise.** A claims-made policy has a much shorter interval between collecting premium and paying losses, so the insurer holds the funds for less time and earns less investment income on them. Because the target underwriting profit provision moves in the **opposite** direction to expected investment income — total return is what is being targeted — less investment income requires a higher underwriting profit provision to hit the same overall return.

**Risk falls, so the target underwriting profit provision can fall.** The target provision also compensates the insurer for bearing risk. Claims-made coverage removes reporting-lag uncertainty from both pricing and reserving, so less capital is needed to support the business and a lower risk load is required.

Whichever argument is used, the answer must name the direction of the movement and the reason behind it.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that the shorter claims-made tail either reduces investment income or reduces risk, and then to state both the direction the target underwriting profit provision moves and the corresponding reason.

Common errors included failing to recognize that the target underwriting profit provision moves opposite to investment income, and discussing actual realized profits rather than the target provision built into the rates.
