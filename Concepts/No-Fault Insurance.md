**No-Fault Insurance** in automobile insurance means that an injured person recovers defined benefits from **their own insurer**, regardless of who caused the collision. It trades the right to sue for certainty and speed of payment. Canadian schemes are almost all **partial** ("threshold" or "add-on") no-fault: first-party [[Statutory Accident Benefits|accident benefits]] are payable immediately, and the right to sue survives only above a [[Tort Threshold and Deductible|threshold]].

- **Pure no-fault** abolishes the tort action entirely — Quebec's public bodily injury scheme and Manitoba's are the Canadian examples. **Partial no-fault** keeps tort for serious injury (Ontario, the Atlantic provinces, Alberta in modified form). **Add-on** provides first-party benefits with no restriction on suing at all.
- **The arguments for:** benefits paid within weeks rather than after years of litigation; every injured person is compensated, including those at fault; transaction costs fall sharply because legal and adjusting expense on contested liability disappears; premiums are more stable because severity is capped by a benefit schedule rather than by juries.
- **The arguments against:** the negligent driver bears no financial consequence, weakening deterrence ([[Moral Hazard]]); benefit schedules under-compensate the seriously injured relative to full tort damages; the right to sue is a legal right being removed; and disputes migrate from liability to **entitlement** — whether an injury meets a definition — so litigation is reduced rather than eliminated.
- **First-party structure changes the incentives on the insurer too.** Under tort, the insurer defends against a stranger's claim; under no-fault it adjudicates its own policyholder's claim, which is a [[Duty of Good Faith|good faith]] and [[Market Conduct Regulation|conduct]] relationship rather than an adversarial one.
- Actuarially, no-fault shifts cost from **severity-driven and volatile** (jury awards, tail development) to **frequency-driven and schedule-bounded**, which shortens the tail and improves reserve predictability — the reason no-fault provinces have less reserve development risk in auto.

> [!example]- Same Accident, Three Systems {Example}
> A driver is injured by another driver's negligence: $\$18{,}000$ of medical and rehabilitation cost, $\$25{,}000$ of lost income, and pain and suffering a court would value at $\$45{,}000$. Describe the recovery under (a) pure tort, (b) pure no-fault with a benefit schedule paying medical costs in full and $80\%$ of income loss, and (c) threshold no-fault where the injury does not meet the threshold.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > **(a) Pure tort.** The driver sues and, if liability is established, recovers all three heads:
> >
> > $$\$18{,}000 + \$25{,}000 + \$45{,}000 = \$88{,}000$$
> >
> > less any contributory negligence, after two to four years, net of legal costs.
> >
> > **(b) Pure no-fault.** Paid by the driver's own insurer, promptly, with no litigation:
> >
> > $$\$18{,}000 + 0.80(\$25{,}000) = \$38{,}000$$
> >
> > There is no pain-and-suffering award, and the $20\%$ income gap is uncompensated. The driver is $\$50{,}000$ worse off but was paid within weeks and would have been paid even if at fault.
> >
> > **(c) Threshold no-fault, below threshold.** The accident benefits of $\$38{,}000$ are payable; the tort action for the $\$45{,}000$ is barred because the injury does not meet the threshold. Total $\$38{,}000$.
> >
> > The comparison is the whole no-fault debate in one table: the system that pays most pays slowest and only to the not-at-fault, and the system that pays fastest and most broadly pays least to the seriously injured. Which is preferable is a policy judgement; the actuary's contribution is that (b) and (c) are far cheaper and far more predictable to reserve.
