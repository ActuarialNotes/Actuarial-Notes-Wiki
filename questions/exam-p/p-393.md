A homeowner purchases a policy from an insurance company covering losses from hurricanes and fires. Under the policy, the insurance company pays 1000 for each loss.

In each year, the number of hurricanes is Poisson distributed, with a common mean for all years. Similarly, for each year, the number of fires is also Poisson distributed, with a common mean for all years. A hurricane occurs on average once every 10 years, while a fire occurs on average once every 50 years. The numbers of hurricanes and numbers of fires in different years are all mutually independent.

Let $T$ be a random variable representing the total payments made by the insurance company to the homeowner over the next 40 years.

Calculate the mode of $T$.

- A) $2000$
- B) $3000$
- C) $4000$
- D) $4800$
- E) $5000$

## Explanation

Independent Poisson variables add to a Poisson variable whose mean is the sum of the means. Per year the two perils give

$$
\lambda_{\text{year}} = 0.1 + 0.02 = 0.12
$$

and over 40 years,

$$
\lambda = 40(0.12) = 4.8
$$

so the total number of losses $N$ is Poisson with mean 4.8. Since $T = 1000N$, the mode of $T$ is 1000 times the mode of $N$.

To locate the mode, compare consecutive probabilities:

$$
\begin{align*}
p(n) &= \frac{e^{-4.8}4.8^n}{n!} \\
     &= \frac{4.8}{n}\cdot\frac{e^{-4.8}4.8^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} \\
     &= \frac{4.8}{n}p(n-1)
\end{align*}
$$

The ratio $4.8/n$ exceeds 1 while $n < 4.8$ and falls below 1 after, so the probabilities rise up to $n = 4$ and decline from $n = 5$ on. Concretely $p(4) = 1.2\,p(3)$ while $p(5) = 0.96\,p(4)$, so the mode of $N$ is 4 and

$$
\text{mode}(T) = 1000(4) = \mathbf{4000}
$$

Choice (D), 4800, is $1000\lambda$ — the *mean* of $T$ rather than its mode. For a Poisson variable with non-integer mean the mode is the integer part of the mean, so the two differ here by design.
