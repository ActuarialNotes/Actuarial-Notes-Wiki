**Social Insurance** is a compulsory, government-administered insurance program covering a risk the private market serves badly or not at all — [[Employment Insurance]], [[Health Care Insurance]], [[Workers Compensation Insurance]], public pensions. Participation is universal or near-universal, contributions are usually a function of earnings rather than of individual risk, and benefits are set by statute.

- **The five features** that distinguish social insurance from private insurance: compulsory participation; benefits prescribed by legislation rather than contract; contributions not fully risk-based, so **cross-subsidy is intentional**; a social adequacy objective alongside individual equity; and financing that may be pay-as-you-go rather than fully funded.
- **The economic justification** is [[Adverse Selection]]: a voluntary program covering unemployment, or health care for the sick, attracts only the high risks and collapses. Compulsion eliminates selection, which is what makes universal coverage affordable.
- **Individual equity versus social adequacy** is the trade-off to be able to state on demand. Private insurance aims for equity — each pays their own expected cost. Social insurance deliberately sacrifices some of it so that benefits are adequate for those who need them, and accepts the resulting [[Moral Hazard]] and cross-subsidy as the price.
- **Evaluation criteria** the syllabus asks candidates to apply to any government program: does it meet its stated objective; is it financially sustainable ([[Solvency|solvency]] and funding); is it efficient (administrative cost, incentive effects); is it equitable; and how does it interact with the private market — does it crowd it out, complement it, or leave a gap?
- **Funding is the recurring weakness.** Pay-as-you-go financing works while demographics are favourable and fails when they are not, and benefits set politically tend to be increased in good times faster than contributions are.
- The private market is not absent: it often provides the **top-up** layer above the social floor — supplementary health, disability and life coverage — so program design determines the size of the private market beside it.

> [!example]- Evaluating a Government Program {Example}
> A province proposes a compulsory public earthquake insurance program funded by a flat levy on every property, replacing private earthquake coverage. Apply the standard evaluation framework.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > **Objective.** Universal coverage against a catastrophic peril with very low take-up privately (earthquake take-up in high-risk Canadian regions is well below the level required to avoid a solvency crisis after a major event). Compulsion solves the [[Adverse Selection]] and under-insurance problems at once. So far the case is strong.
> >
> > **Sustainability.** Weak as designed. Earthquake is the archetypal low-frequency, extreme-severity peril: the program will accumulate a fund for decades and then face a claim far exceeding it. Without reinsurance, a capital markets facility or an explicit government backstop, "the fund" is a promise the treasury will honour later — and that should be stated in the proposal rather than discovered after the event.
> >
> > **Efficiency.** A flat levy is administratively cheap but destroys the price signal. Building in a high-hazard zone costs no more than building on bedrock, which discourages mitigation and encourages exposure growth exactly where it is most dangerous — [[Moral Hazard]] at the land-use level, and the same criticism made of [[Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements|disaster assistance]].
> >
> > **Equity.** A flat levy transfers from low-hazard to high-hazard property owners. Whether that is fair is a policy judgement; that it is a subsidy is not, and the actuary should quantify its size.
> >
> > **Interaction with the private market.** Replacing private earthquake coverage removes the insurers who currently price, model and reinsure this risk, and with them the expertise and the global reinsurance capacity. A layered design — public coverage above a private layer, or public reinsurance behind private policies — usually dominates full replacement.
> >
> > **Recommendation.** The coverage gap is real, but risk-based levies with a mitigation credit, and retention of private participation, address it at lower cost. That structure keeps the price signal while still achieving universality.
