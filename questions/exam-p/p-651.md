A dental patient buys insurance that reimburses 100% of dental losses in Year 1 and 60% of dental losses in Year 2. The table below shows the joint probability function of the patient's losses in both years.

| Year 2 \ Year 1 | 0 | 2 | 5 | 10 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | 0.4 | 0.23 | 0.12 | 0.05 | 0.8 |
| **2** | 0.05 | 0.03 | 0.012 | 0.008 | 0.1 |
| **5** | 0.03 | 0.025 | 0.004 | 0.001 | 0.06 |
| **10** | 0.02 | 0.015 | 0.004 | 0.001 | 0.04 |
| **Total** | 0.5 | 0.3 | 0.14 | 0.06 |  |

Calculate the patient's expected unreimbursed loss in this two-year period.

- A) $0.36$
- B) $0.76$
- C) $0.90$
- D) $1.70$
- E) $2.80$

## Explanation

Year 1 losses are reimbursed in full, so they contribute nothing unreimbursed — the whole Year 1 dimension of the table drops out and only the Year 2 margin matters. Let $Y$ be the Year 2 loss, read off the row totals:

$$
p(y) = \begin{cases}
0.80, & y = 0 \\
0.10, & y = 2 \\
0.06, & y = 5 \\
0.04, & y = 10
\end{cases}
$$

Year 2 reimburses 60%, leaving 40% unreimbursed:

$$
\begin{align*}
E[\text{Unreimbursed Loss}] &= E[0.4Y] = 0.4\,E[Y] \\
                            &= 0.4\left[0(0.80) + 2(0.10) + 5(0.06) + 10(0.04)\right] \\
                            &= 0.4(0.9) \\
                            &= \mathbf{0.36}
\end{align*}
$$

Choice (C), 0.90, is $E[Y]$ — the full expected Year 2 loss, with the 60% reimbursement never applied. Note that the joint structure of the table is a red herring here: since only $Y$ carries an unreimbursed share, its marginal distribution is all the information the calculation needs.
