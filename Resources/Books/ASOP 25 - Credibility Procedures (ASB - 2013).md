![[ASOP 25 - Credibility Procedures (ASB - 2013) - Cover.svg]]

The Actuarial Standards Board's guidance to actuaries selecting or developing credibility procedures and applying those procedures to sets of data. This revised edition (Doc. No. 174) was developed by the Credibility Task Force of the ASB's General Committee and adopted in December 2013; it widens the original 1996 standard, which was limited to accident and health, group term life, and property/casualty coverages. It is effective for professional services with respect to credibility procedures performed on or after May 1, 2014.

> [!info] On the syllabus
> - [[Exam 8 (CAS)|Exam 8]] — objectives A2–A3; the whole standard, "Credibility Procedures," December 2013.

## Transmittal Memorandum
- The original standard, adopted in 1996, was a product of the ASB's Health and Casualty Committees. In 2011 the ASB asked whether its scope should be expanded, and a task force of life, health, pension and property/casualty actuaries drafted the revision under the General Committee.
- The first exposure draft (September 2012) drew 20 comment letters. The most significant change it prompted was to section 1.2, Scope, clarifying the situations in which the standard applies, together with the purpose and use of credibility procedures and the continued need for professional judgment.
- The second exposure draft (June 2013) drew nine comment letters and only clarifying changes, summarised in appendix 2; the ASB adopted the standard in December 2013.

## 1 Purpose, Scope, Cross References, and Effective Date
- 1.1 Purpose
    - To guide actuaries performing professional services with respect to selecting or developing credibility procedures and applying them to sets of data.
- 1.2 Scope
    - Applies when applicable law requires the actuary to evaluate credibility; when the actuary chooses to evaluate the credibility of subject experience, or states that credibility has been evaluated in accordance with the ASOP; when the actuary blends subject experience with other experience; and when the actuary represents the data used as statistically or mathematically credible.
    - Where it conflicts with ASOP No. 35 (pension demographic assumptions), ASOP No. 35 governs; a departure to comply with applicable law, or for any other reason, is dealt with under section 4.
- 1.3 Cross References
- 1.4 Effective Date
    - Professional services with respect to credibility procedures performed on or after May 1, 2014.

## 2 Definitions
- 2.1 Credibility
    - [[Credibility]]: a measure of the predictive value in a given application that the actuary attaches to a particular set of data — predictive in the statistical sense, not in the sense of predicting the future.
- 2.2 Credibility Procedure
    - A process that either evaluates subject experience for potential use in setting assumptions without reference to other data, or identifies relevant experience and selects and implements a method for blending it with the subject experience.
- 2.3 Full Credibility
    - [[Full Credibility Standard|Full credibility]]: the level at which the subject experience is assigned full predictive value, often based on a selected confidence interval.
- 2.4 Relevant Experience
    - [[Complement of Credibility|Relevant experience]]: sets of data, including data other than the subject experience, that in the actuary's judgment are predictive of the parameter under study (loss ratios, claims, mortality, payment patterns, persistency or expenses); it may include the subject experience as a subset.
- 2.5 Risk Characteristics
    - Measurable or observable factors used to assign each risk to one of the risk classes of a risk classification system — [[Rating Variable|risk characteristics]].
- 2.6 Risk Classification System
    - A [[Classification Ratemaking|risk classification system]] assigns risks to groups based upon the expected cost or benefit of the coverage or services provided.
- 2.7 Subject Experience
    - A specific set of data drawn from the experience under consideration for the purpose of predicting the parameter under study.

## 3 Analysis of Issues and Recommended Practices
- 3.1 Purpose and Use of Credibility Procedures
    - Credibility procedures have two purposes: to evaluate subject experience for potential use in setting assumptions without reference to other data, and to improve the estimate of the parameter under study. They serve tasks such as pricing, ratemaking, prospective [[Experience Rating|experience rating]] and reserving.
- 3.2 Selection or Development of Credibility Procedure
    - The actuary should use an appropriate procedure when deciding whether the subject experience has full credibility or when blending it with relevant experience; procedures may differ by practice area and application.
    - The criteria are whether the procedure is expected to produce reasonable results, is appropriate for the intended use and purpose, and is practical to implement given its cost and benefit.
    - The procedure should consider the characteristics of both the subject and the relevant experience, and the predictive value of more recent experience compared with experience from earlier time periods.
- 3.3 Selection of Relevant Experience
    - Relevant experience should have characteristics similar to the subject experience — demographics, coverages, frequency, severity or other determinable risk characteristics. Experience that does not meet, and cannot be adjusted to meet, such criteria should not be used.
    - Where the subject experience is a material part of the relevant experience, professional judgment decides whether and how to use it; where no relevant experience is available, the actuary uses professional judgment, considering the available subject experience.
- 3.4 Professional Judgment
    - The use of credibility procedures is not always a precise mathematical process: an acceptable procedure may assign full, [[Partial Credibility|partial]] or zero credibility to the subject experience without a rigorous mathematical model.
- 3.5 Homogeneity of Data
    - The actuary should consider the [[Homogeneity|homogeneity]] of both the subject and the relevant experience. Segments not representative of the whole may be treated separately to enhance predictive value, balancing the homogeneity of the data against the size of the data set.

## 4 Communications and Disclosures
- 4.1 Disclosure
    - When appropriate, the actuary should disclose the credibility procedures used and any material changes from prior procedures, together with the ASOP No. 41 disclosures (sections 4.2–4.4) for a method prescribed by law, reliance on other sources, or a material deviation from the standard.

## Appendix 1 Background and Current Practices
- Background
    - Credibility has been part of actuarial practice since the beginning of the profession, balancing responsiveness against stability. The earliest recorded paper is Albert H. Mowbray's "How Extensive a Payroll Exposure Is Necessary to Give a Dependable Pure Premium" (Proceedings of the Casualty Actuarial and Statistical Society, Volume I, 1914); later writers extended credibility to claim severity and total losses, including Bayesian procedures.
- Current Practices
    - Classical credibility procedures assume a form for the underlying distribution and compute the volume of claims, premium or other measure — the [[Full Credibility Standard|full credibility standard]] — at which the probability that the subject experience is within a specified percentage $r$ of the expected value equals a specified $p$. [[Limited Fluctuation Credibility]] assumes normally distributed claims and bases partial credibility on the square root of the ratio of actual claims to the full credibility standard.
    - Empirical credibility procedures measure the statistical relationships of the subject experience to its mean and to comparable experience of prior periods, without reference to the underlying distribution.
    - [[Bayesian Credibility|Bayesian credibility procedures]] merge a prior distribution from the relevant experience with the subject experience into a posterior, and give a least squares approximation to its mean. Greatest Accuracy Credibility — linear Bayesian or [[Bühlmann Credibility|Bühlmann credibility]] — assigns credibility of the form $n/(n+k)$, where $n$ is the volume of subject experience and $k$ a parameter derived from variances.
    - Emerging practice builds credibility into [[Generalized Linear Model|generalized linear models]] and other multivariate techniques — [[Generalized Linear Mixed Model|generalized linear mixed models]], hierarchical and mixed-effects models — estimating it from statistical significance, performance on a [[Holdout Sample|holdout data set]], or the consistency of either over time.
    - The most common credibility bases are numbers or amounts of claims, losses, premiums and exposures. For ratemaking the full-credibility sample size may rest on an assumed frequency distribution adjusted for variation in claim size; prospective experience rating may split the subject experience into components, such as primary and excess losses, with different credibility for each.

## Appendix 2 Comments on the Second Exposure Draft and Responses
- Nine comment letters were received on the second exposure draft; the task force, the General Committee and the ASB reviewed them.
- The definitions of risk characteristics and risk classification system were kept unchanged as consistent with ASOP No. 12, sections 2.8 and 2.10.
- The standard addresses traditional credibility theory: predictive modeling is not explicitly referenced, but the standard applies to the extent credibility procedures within its scope are used in it.
- The reviewers declined to require relevant experience to be fully credible, since fully credible experience does not always exist, and kept zero credibility in the standard. They replaced "valuation" with "reserving" in section 3.1 and added the balance between data size and homogeneity to section 3.5.
- "Greatest accuracy credibility" was kept as the primary name for the Bühlmann approach, as in the American Academy of Actuaries' July 2008 Credibility Practice Note; the appendix heading on generalized linear models became "Emerging Practice Involving Statistical Models."
