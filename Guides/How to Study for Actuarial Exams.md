Becoming an actuary is a **course of study, not a degree**: a sequence of exams and requirements set by a professional body, sat while working, over roughly seven to ten years. Nobody schedules it for you. This page is the map — what the exams are, how a sitting works, and how to spend the months in between.

## The two societies

Two bodies credential actuaries in North America, and the choice between them is a choice of industry, not of difficulty.

- The **Casualty Actuarial Society (CAS)** credentials actuaries in **property and casualty** (general) insurance — auto, home, commercial liability, workers compensation, reinsurance. Its designations are [[Associate of the Casualty Actuarial Society (ACAS)|ACAS]] and then [[Fellow of the Casualty Actuarial Society (FCAS)|FCAS]].
- The **Society of Actuaries (SOA)** credentials actuaries in **life, health, pensions and investments**. Its designations are [[Associate of the Society of Actuaries (ASA)|ASA]] and then [[Fellow of the Society of Actuaries (FSA)|FSA]].

The first two exams — [[Exam P-1 (SOA)|Probability]] and [[Exam FM-2 (SOA)|Financial Mathematics]] — are **jointly administered**. The CAS calls them Exams 1 and 2, the SOA calls them P and FM, and they are the same papers. That is worth knowing early: you can start studying before you have decided which society you are aiming at, and most candidates do.

Each credential is also more than exams. Both pathways include university coursework validated in advance (**VEE**), online courses or modules, and a **professionalism course** — and it is membership of the society, not the letters, that carries the professional obligations. The designation pages above list the current requirements in full.

## How a sitting works

A sitting is a date, a seat at a test centre, and a fee, booked months ahead:

- **Registration closes weeks before the exam.** Deadlines are the part candidates actually miss; the fee is usually non-refundable after them.
- **The preliminary exams are multiple choice**, computer-based at a test centre, and scored the same way whether you got there by insight or by elimination — nothing is subtracted for a wrong answer, so never leave one blank.
- **The upper exams are written-answer.** Partial credit is real and graders award it for the method, so the work is shown on purpose, not as a courtesy.
- **Results take weeks**, and the pass mark is not a fixed percentage: the exams are scaled, and the published pass rates sit around 30–50%. A failure is a common outcome for a well-prepared candidate, not a verdict.
- **Calculators are restricted** to a short approved list. Buy one of them early and do every practice problem on it, so the keystrokes are automatic by exam day.

Each of this wiki's exam study guides opens with the exam's own format — the length, the number of questions, and what that works out to per question.

## How to actually study

**Work backwards from the sitting date.** Pick the exam date first, then count the weeks. A preliminary exam takes most candidates something like 300 hours spread over three to four months; the upper exams take more. Fifteen hours a week for fourteen weeks is a plan. "When I have time" is not.

**Learn the syllabus, not the textbook.** Every exam publishes learning objectives with **weights** — the percentage of the paper each section is worth. This wiki's exam pages list them, weights included. Time is allocated in proportion to those weights, and a 5% topic does not get a fortnight.

**Do problems from the first week.** Actuarial exams test whether you can produce an answer under a clock, and reading is not practice. Work problems from day one — badly at first — and treat every one you get wrong as the thing you actually learned that day. Keep a list of them.

**Spread the practice out.** The **spacing effect** is the most reliable finding in the study literature: the same hours, spread over more days, produce a more durable memory than the same hours stacked together. Short daily sessions beat weekend marathons, and revisiting a topic *after* you have started to forget it is exactly when the review does the most work.

**Test yourself instead of re-reading.** Retrieving an answer from memory strengthens it in a way that recognising it on the page does not. Close the notes and reproduce the formula, then check. This wiki's flashcards and quizzes exist for that; so does covering the solution and redoing a worked example from scratch.

**Sit past papers under exam conditions.** Whole papers, whole clock, approved calculator, no notes — starting about a month out. This is where pacing is learned, and pacing is what fails candidates who knew the material.

**Then read the examiner's report.** Published past papers come with reports on what candidates actually got wrong. They are the closest thing to being told what the graders are looking for, and they are consistently under-read.

## A sitting order that works

Order matters, because the exams build. [[Exam P-1 (SOA)|Probability]] first: everything statistical downstream conditions on it. [[Exam FM-2 (SOA)|Financial Mathematics]] next, and it can overlap with a job hunt — the two preliminary exams are what make a candidate employable, and most of the rest of the pathway is sat while employed, with an employer's study programme paying the fees and giving back some of the hours.

After that, follow the pathway of the society you have chosen, and check the transition rules before you plan a long sequence — both bodies restructure their syllabi periodically, and a requirement can change under you.
