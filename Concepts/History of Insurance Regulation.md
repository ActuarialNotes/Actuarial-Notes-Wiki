**The History of Insurance Regulation** in the United States is the story of how a business the Supreme Court once held was *not* commerce came to be regulated by the states under a federal statute — and of the repeated federal pressure (1944, 1990, 1999, 2010) that has reshaped, but never replaced, that settlement.

> $$\begin{gathered}\text{1869: insurance is not commerce} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{1944: insurance is interstate commerce} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{1945: states regulate; federal law defers}\end{gathered}$$

- **State beginnings.** New Hampshire appointed the first state insurance commissioner in 1851. In *Paul v. Virginia* (1869) the Court held that "issuing a policy of insurance is not a transaction of commerce", leaving regulation and taxation to the states. Their commissioners met in 1871 as the National Insurance Convention — known since 1935 as the **NAIC** — to handle what crossed state lines, beginning with uniform financial reporting.
- **Bureau rating.** Fire insurers priced collectively through rating bureaus. New York's Merritt Committee (1910–11) accepted cooperative rating as preferable to destructive price-cutting, provided the state supervised it — the model for regulated bureau pricing.
- **SEUA (1944).** In *United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association* the Justice Department prosecuted a fire-insurance rating bureau for price-fixing and boycotting; the Court held that insurance conducted across state lines **is** interstate commerce, subject to the Sherman Act. State rating laws built on bureau pricing, and state premium taxes, were suddenly exposed.
- **McCarran-Ferguson (1945).** Congress declared continued state regulation and taxation to be in the public interest: no federal act supersedes state insurance law unless it specifically relates to insurance, and the federal antitrust laws apply only to the extent the business is not regulated by state law — boycott, coercion and intimidation excepted ([[Antitrust Law]]). The states answered with the 1946 **All-Industry** model rating laws: rates *not excessive, inadequate or unfairly discriminatory*, the standard still applied to [[Emerging Regulatory Issues|new pricing tools]].
- **Competition versus control.** From the 1960s many states moved from prior approval toward file-and-use and open competition. California, long an open-rating state, went the other way with Proposition 103 (1988): prior approval, a rate rollback and an elected commissioner.
- **Solvency (1980s–90s).** A wave of insolvencies and the congressional report *Failed Promises* (1990) produced NAIC accreditation and RBC — see [[History of Solvency Regulation]].
- **Financial modernization.** Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999) allowed banks, securities firms and insurers to affiliate, reaffirmed McCarran-Ferguson, adopted **functional regulation** (each activity supervised by its own regulator), and threatened a federal producer-licensing body unless states achieved licensing reciprocity. Optional federal charter bills followed; none passed.
- **After 2008.** AIG's near-failure — driven mainly by a derivatives unit that state insurance regulators did not supervise — led to [[Dodd-Frank]] (2010): the Federal Insurance Office, FSOC, and surplus lines and reinsurance reform. The NAIC's own response, the Solvency Modernization Initiative, produced ORSA and governance disclosure ([[Insurance Governance]]).

> [!example]- Why SEUA Mattered {Example}
> Explain why the 1944 SEUA decision threatened the state system, and how McCarran-Ferguson resolved each threat.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Once insurance was interstate commerce, three things were exposed:
> >
> > 1. **Bureau rating** — insurers agreeing on common rates — was price-fixing under Sherman Act section 1. McCarran-Ferguson applied federal antitrust law only where the business is *not regulated by state law*, so states quickly passed rating laws bringing bureau activity under supervision.
> > 2. **Every state insurance law** was open to displacement by federal statutes and by Commerce Clause challenge, including premium taxes that favoured domestic insurers. McCarran-Ferguson declared state regulation and taxation to be in the public interest and barred displacement by any federal act not specifically about insurance.
> > 3. **Regulatory vacuum.** Congress had no insurance regulator ready to step in. A moratorium on antitrust enforcement (to 1948) gave the states time to legislate.
> >
> > The price of the settlement was the **boycott exception**: collective refusals to deal remained federally actionable whatever the state did.

> [!example]- Federal Pressure, State Reform {Example}
> Match each federal event to the problem it responded to and to the state-level change that followed: SEUA (1944), *Failed Promises* (1990), Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999), Dodd-Frank (2010).
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - **SEUA** — collusive pricing → state rating laws under McCarran-Ferguson.
> > - ***Failed Promises*** — insolvencies and weak state supervision → NAIC accreditation, RBC and risk-focused surveillance.
> > - **Gramm-Leach-Bliley** — convergence of banking and insurance → functional regulation and producer-licensing reciprocity.
> > - **Dodd-Frank** — systemic risk and group-level gaps exposed by AIG → FIO and FSOC federally; ORSA, Form F and group supervision at the states.
> >
> > The pattern is the point: each wave of federal attention has been answered by **state** reform strong enough to keep the primary authority where McCarran-Ferguson put it.
