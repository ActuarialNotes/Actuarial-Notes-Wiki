The **Professional Responsibilities of the Actuary** are the obligations an actuary owes to the principal, to the users of the work, to regulators and to the public when producing work others rely on. In [[Financial Reporting|financial reporting]] they fall above all on the actuary who values an insurer's claim liabilities and opines on them.

They are set by four sources, each ranking above the next:

1. **Law and regulation.** Law prevails: an actuary required by law or regulation to use a method or assumption complies and discloses it. The standards do not override it.
2. **The code of conduct**, which governs integrity, competence, qualification and conflicts.
3. **Standards of practice**, which govern how the work is done and what is disclosed.
4. **Guidance**, such as CIA [[Educational Note|educational notes]] and U.S. practice notes, which is non-binding but in practice expected.

In both countries the actuary must be qualified for the work, exercise independent judgement even when employed by the company, and disclose reliance on others, deviations from standards and material limitations. When the numbers are wrong, the actuary escalates rather than resigning quietly.

## Canada

The [[Insurance Companies Act]] (and provincial Insurance Acts) require the board to appoint an [[Appointed Actuary]] who values the liabilities in accordance with [[Accepted Actuarial Practice]], reports to the directors, performs [[FCT]] and carries the [[Duty to Report]]. The CIA's Rules of Professional Conduct and [[Standards of Practice]] govern the work: [[Materiality]], [[Subsequent Events]], [[Model Risk|models]], [[Runoff|run-off and wind-up valuations]], the [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion|opinion]] and the [[Appointed Actuary's Report|report]]. OSFI's Guideline E-15 adds qualification and [[Peer Review|peer review]] expectations, and the [[Actuary and Auditor Relationship]] and [[Corporate Governance]] fix whom the actuary works with and answers to.

## United States

The Code of Professional Conduct, adopted by the U.S.-based actuarial organizations including the CAS, requires qualification under the [[American Academy of Actuaries|Academy]]'s U.S. Qualification Standards and compliance with the [[Actuarial Standards of Practice]]. The Actuarial Board for Counseling and Discipline enforces it.

The NAIC instructions add the statutory layer ([[Appointed Actuary Responsibilities]]): appointment by the board, a defined "Qualified Actuary", an annual report to the board on the opinion's scope, an SAO consistent with ASOPs 23, 36, 41 and 43, the [[Actuarial Opinion Summary]] and [[Actuarial Report]], and a duty to notify when an opinion proves to have been in error.

> [!example]- What must happen after this Appointed Actuary finds the year-end opinion relied on incomplete data? {Example}
> In June, the Appointed Actuary learns that the claim data used for the year-end SAO omitted a block of open claims that existed at December 31. With the correct data, the carried reserve would have been below the actuary's range.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - This is an error under the NAIC instructions, because the opinion relied on data that was factually incorrect as of the balance sheet date and would have been materially different with the correct data.
> > - The actuary notifies the board of directors or audit committee in writing within five business days of determining the error.
> > - The insurer forwards the amended opinion to the domiciliary commissioner within five business days and gives the actuary a copy. If the actuary does not receive it in that time, the actuary notifies the commissioner.
> > - Had the effect not been clear at once, the company would have had ten business days to supply corrected data and support before the actuary went to the board and the commissioner directly.
> > - Statements made in a good-faith effort to comply carry immunity.
> > - If the reserve had merely developed adversely because of events after December 31, the opinion would not be in error, because it is judged on what was true at the valuation date.

> [!example]- What are the actuary's responsibilities for this deficiency in Canada and in the United States? {Example}
> Carried net claim liabilities are $\$470$ million. The actuary's range of reasonable estimates is $\$480$ million to $\$540$ million. Surplus is $\$150$ million.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The shortfall against the low end is $480 - 470 = \$10$ million, about $6.7\%$ of surplus.
> > - In Canada, the amount carried does not make appropriate provision, so the opinion is adverse and must quantify the deficiency.
> > - The matter affects financial condition, so the [[Duty to Report]] escalation applies: the CEO and CFO, then the directors, then [[OSFI]] if it is not addressed.
> > - In the United States, the SAO is a Determination of Deficient or Inadequate Provision, and must disclose the minimum amount the actuary considers reasonable, $\$480$ million.
> > - The risk-of-material-adverse-deviation comments state the materiality standard in dollars, the board hears the actuary's findings, and the regulator sees the deficiency in the Annual Statement filing.
> > - In neither country may the actuary sign a clean opinion on a number outside the range, or resolve the disagreement by resigning.

## Data modeling

The same Code applies to a U.S. actuary building or using a predictive model:

- Precept 1 requires skill and care.
- Precept 2 requires work only within the actuary's qualification.
- Precept 3 requires compliance with the standards of practice.
- Precept 4 requires communications that are clear and appropriate to their audience.
- Precept 8 requires reasonable steps so the work is not used to mislead.
- Precept 9 requires protection of confidential information.

The standards most often engaged are:

- **ASOP 23** (*Data Quality*): select appropriate data, review it for questionable values unless that is not necessary or practical, and disclose its limitations.
- **ASOP 12** (*Risk Classification*): risk characteristics must be related to expected outcomes, though a cause-and-effect relationship need not be shown, and applicable law can limit the choice.
- **ASOP 56** (*Modeling*): the model must meet its intended purpose, model risk must be evaluated and mitigated, and it defines hold-out data and overfitting.

The law around these is [[Insurance Data Regulation]].

> [!trap] Requiring cause and effect for a rating variable
> ASOP 12 requires a demonstrated relationship between a risk characteristic and expected outcomes, not proof of cause and effect. Law may still forbid a characteristic that predicts well.
