![[Media/Figures/Insurance_Regulation.svg|340]]

**Insurance Regulation** in Canada is the supervision of insurers by government, split between a federal solvency regulator, [[OSFI]], and provincial regulators who license insurers and oversee [[Market Conduct Regulation|market conduct]] and [[Rate Regulation|rates]]. No single body regulates an insurer end to end. The same company answers to OSFI for its capital and to each province it writes in for how it sells and prices.

The work divides into two parts:

| Part | Regulator | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solvency | Mostly federal ([[OSFI]]) | Incorporating and supervising federally registered insurers, and their capital |
| Market conduct | Provincial | The insurance contract, licensing of insurers and agents, premium rates, and the treatment of policyholders |

The split comes from the [[Federal-Provincial Jurisdiction|division of powers]]. The federal government incorporates and supervises federally registered insurers under the [[Insurance Companies Act]]. The provinces' authority over "property and civil rights in the province" gives each province authority over the contract itself, the licensing of insurers and agents, and premium rates.

The many provincial regulators coordinate through the [[Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators]], and coordinate prudential matters with OSFI through information-sharing agreements. Coordination is not consolidation, and the cost of complying in many jurisdictions is a standing industry complaint.

> [!example]- Which regulator addresses each of these four questions? {Example}
> A federally incorporated insurer writes personal automobile insurance in Ontario and Alberta and commercial property across Canada. The questions are:
>
> 1. Whether the company holds enough capital.
> 2. Whether its Ontario auto rates may increase $6\%$.
> 3. Whether a claims-handling practice is unfair to policyholders.
> 4. Whether the company may write business at all.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Capital is federal solvency supervision, so **OSFI** addresses it, through the [[MCT]] and its capital ratio targets.
> > - Rates are provincial [[Rate Regulation|rate regulation]]. Ontario private passenger auto is file-and-approve, so **FSRA** must approve the increase. Alberta's rate board decides separately for Alberta.
> > - Market conduct is provincial, so **the provincial regulator** in the province where the conduct occurred addresses it.
> > - Writing business needs **both**. OSFI issues the federal order to commence and carry on business, and each province issues a separate licence for that province.

## Rationales for insurance regulation

The three classic rationales for regulating insurance are:

1. **Solvency.** Policyholders buy a promise to pay later and cannot assess an insurer's balance sheet.
2. **Information asymmetry.** The contract is complex and drafted by the insurer.
3. **Availability and affordability.** The public is compelled to buy some coverage, which is why [[Automobile Insurance Reform|auto]] is the most heavily regulated line.

> [!example]- A commentator argues that competition alone protects insurance consumers, so solvency regulation is unnecessary. What is the actuarial response? {Example}
> - Competition disciplines price, but insurance is a promise to pay later.
> - Assessing whether an insurer will be solvent in ten years needs reserve and capital analysis the buyer cannot do, so competition on price alone rewards the insurer that under-reserves.
> - An insolvency imposes losses on claimants who are not the insurer's customers, such as injured third parties, and on the industry through [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty fund]] assessments ([[PACICC]]).
> - Drivers are legally required to buy auto insurance, so not buying it is not an option. This justifies [[Rate Regulation|rate]] and availability regulation on top of solvency regulation.
> - Competition alone therefore does not protect the consumer, and solvency regulation is needed.

## Principles-based regulation

Regulation is increasingly [[Principles-Based Regulation|principles-based]]. OSFI sets outcomes, such as its [[ORSA]], [[FCT]] and [[Corporate Governance]] expectations, and holds the board and senior management responsible for them, rather than prescribing every calculation.

The [[Appointed Actuary]] is a regulatory creation. The [[Insurance Companies Act]] requires the appointment and gives the actuary duties owed to the company, with reporting obligations the regulator relies on.

> [!trap] Naming only one regulator
> There is no single regulator. An answer that names only OSFI for a market-conduct question, or only the province for a capital question, has missed the split.
