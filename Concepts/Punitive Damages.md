**Punitive Damages** (also *exemplary damages*) are awarded not to compensate the plaintiff but to punish and deter conduct that is malicious, oppressive, or a marked departure from ordinary standards of decent behaviour. In Canadian insurance they arise almost exclusively from an insurer's breach of the [[Duty of Good Faith]] in claims handling.

The Canadian standard comes from *Whiten v. Pilot Insurance* (SCC, 2002). Punitive damages:

1. Are exceptional.
2. Require an independent actionable wrong beyond the breach of contract. In insurance cases, the breach of the duty of good faith supplies it.
3. Must be rationally proportionate to the objective of retribution, deterrence and denunciation, and no more than necessary to accomplish it.

> [!example]- Would punitive damages be awarded in each of these three cases? {Example}
> Assess each scenario against the *Whiten* framework.
>
> 1. An insurer denies a claim on a coverage interpretation later held to be wrong, having obtained a legal opinion supporting its position.
> 2. An insurer instructs adjusters to offer no more than $60\%$ of assessed value on any claim where the claimant is unrepresented, on the basis that most will accept.
> 3. An insurer takes eleven months to decide a claim because of a backlog, then pays in full with interest.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Case 1: no. The insurer investigated, took advice and formed a defensible view, so being wrong is not reprehensible. It is a breach of contract, remedied by paying the claim with interest.
> > - Case 2: yes, and it is the strongest of the three. The conduct is planned, deliberate and sustained, it targets claimants because they are vulnerable, and it is profitable. The award would be sized to exceed the profit from the practice, and a book-wide practice is a natural [[Class Action|class action]].
> > - Case 3: probably not. Delay from incompetence rather than strategy usually attracts interest and possibly aggravated damages for distress, not punishment. If the backlog was a deliberate cost-saving choice made knowing its effect on claimants, it becomes case 2.
> > - Courts punish an insurer that chose to behave badly, not one that got the answer wrong, so the deciding question is motive and system rather than outcome.

## The size of the award

Courts weigh these factors in setting the amount:

1. How reprehensible the conduct was.
2. The vulnerability of the plaintiff.
3. The harm caused or intended.
4. Whether the conduct was planned and deliberate.
5. Its duration.
6. Whether the defendant concealed it.
7. The profit made from it.
8. The adequacy of other penalties already imposed.

Canadian awards are modest by American standards and follow no fixed multiplier, but they are not trivial. The $\$1$ million in *Whiten* was on a claim of roughly $\$345{,}000$, and the Court expressly considered whether a lower award would simply be a cost of doing business.

## Punitive damages and the insurer

An insurer generally cannot insure a policyholder against their own punitive damages, as a rule of policy, since doing so would defeat the deterrent. Whether that holds in a given province and wording is a live coverage question and matters for liability reserving.

For the insurer as defendant, punitive damages are the uncapped tail of its [[Bad Faith Damages|bad faith damages]] exposure. For the insurer as liability carrier, the question is whether the policy responds at all to punitive damages awarded against the insured, and treaties frequently address it separately.

> [!trap] Treating a wrong claim decision as bad faith
> A denial later held to be wrong is not, on its own, grounds for punitive damages. The award needs reprehensible conduct, such as a deliberate practice of underpaying, and an honest, advised coverage position is not that.
