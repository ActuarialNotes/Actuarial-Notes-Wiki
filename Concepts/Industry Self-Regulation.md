**Industry Self-Regulation** is the setting and enforcing of conduct standards by the insurance industry itself, through trade associations, industry-run bodies and professional organisations, rather than by a statute enforced by a government regulator. In Canadian P&C insurance its centrepiece is the [[Insurance Bureau of Canada]]'s [[Code of Consumer Rights and Responsibilities]].

Self-regulation sits on a spectrum. Moving down the table, enforceability rises and industry control falls:

| Tier | Who sets and enforces the rules | Canadian P&C examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Voluntary code | The industry writes, adopts and polices it. Participation rests on membership, and the ultimate sanction is reputational. | The IBC Code; the General Insurance OmbudService, industry-funded but independent, whose recommendations are not binding; IBC's anti-fraud investigation and the vehicle loss data behind its CLEAR vehicle rating system |
| Statutory self-regulation | An industry or professional body holds powers granted by law. | The Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario (RIBO), which licenses and disciplines Ontario brokers under its own Act; the [[Canadian Institute of Actuaries (CIA)|CIA]], whose standards gain legal force because insurance legislation requires [[Accepted Actuarial Practice]]; the [[Facility Association]] and [[PACICC]], whose membership the law makes compulsory |
| Direct regulation | A statute enforced by a [[Superintendent of Insurance]] or [[OSFI]]. | Rate approval, solvency supervision |

> [!example]- Which tier governs each of these Ontario situations, and what enforcement is available? {Example}
> 1. A broker tells a client a policy covers sewer backup when it does not.
> 2. An insurer denies a home claim without giving written reasons.
> 3. An insurer wants to raise private passenger auto rates by $6\%$.
> 4. A driver whom no insurer's filed underwriting rules will accept needs coverage.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The broker's misstatement falls under statutory self-regulation. RIBO can investigate and discipline the broker, up to revoking the licence, and the client can also sue in negligence for the coverage gap.
> > - The denial without reasons falls under a voluntary code backed by direct regulation. The IBC Code promises reasons but creates no cause of action, so the route is the insurer's complaint process, then the General Insurance OmbudService, and, for a pattern of such denials, the regulator's market-conduct powers.
> > - The rate increase is direct regulation: [[Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario|FSRA]] must approve the rate before it is used, and no industry body has a role.
> > - The uninsurable driver falls under statutory self-regulation. The Facility Association writes the risk through a servicing carrier at its filed rates.
> > - Consumer-facing service standards are largely self-regulated, while price and solvency are directly regulated.

## The case for self-regulation

Self-regulation brings industry expertise, and it is fast and flexible: a code can change in months, while legislation takes years. Its cost falls on the industry, not the taxpayer, and its standards can exceed the legal minimum. A shared reputation gives every member a reason to police the worst performers.

## The case against self-regulation

The rules are drafted by those they govern, which is a conflict of interest. Non-members and free riders are untouched, and standards drift toward what the least willing member will accept. Enforcement is weak and gives the individual consumer no remedy. A code can also be offered to head off regulation rather than to improve conduct.

> [!example]- An industry association proposes replacing a province's statutory claims-handling rules with its voluntary code, as its members already comply. Should the province agree? {Example}
> - For the proposal, the code is more detailed and easier to update than regulation, and duplicate regulatory reporting buys little if compliance is high.
> - Against it, the code binds members only, so an insurer that leaves the association, or never joined, is unregulated.
> - Nothing in the code compensates the consumer or penalises the insurer, and the statute does both.
> - High compliance today is evidence that the statutory floor works, because the incentive to comply partly comes from the statute behind it.
> - The province should keep the statutory floor and let the code sit above it as co-regulation, with the regulator treating code breaches as evidence in conduct reviews.

## Outcomes set by regulators

Regulators increasingly set expected outcomes themselves, such as the [[Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators|CCIR]] and CISRO fair-treatment-of-customers guidance, enforced through [[Market Conduct Regulation|market conduct regulation]]. Voluntary codes now feed supervision rather than replace it ([[Principles-Based Regulation]]).
