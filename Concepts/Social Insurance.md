**Social Insurance** is a compulsory, government-administered insurance program covering a risk the private market serves badly or not at all, such as [[Employment Insurance]], [[Health Care Insurance]], [[Workers Compensation Insurance]] and public pensions. Participation is universal or near-universal, contributions are usually a function of earnings rather than of individual risk, and benefits are set by statute.

Five features distinguish social insurance from private insurance:

1. Participation is compulsory.
2. Benefits are prescribed by legislation rather than by contract.
3. Contributions are not fully risk-based, so **cross-subsidy is intentional**.
4. It has a social adequacy objective alongside individual equity.
5. Financing may be pay-as-you-go rather than fully funded.

## Why participation is compulsory

The economic justification is [[Adverse Selection|adverse selection]]. A voluntary program covering unemployment, or health care for the sick, attracts only the high risks and collapses. Compulsion eliminates selection, which is what makes universal coverage affordable.

## Individual equity and social adequacy

Private insurance aims for individual equity: each insured pays their own expected cost. Social insurance deliberately gives up some equity so that benefits are adequate for those who need them. It accepts the resulting [[Moral Hazard|moral hazard]] and cross-subsidy as the price.

The private market often provides the **top-up** layer above the social floor, such as supplementary health, disability and life coverage. Program design therefore determines the size of the private market beside it.

## Evaluating a government program

A government program is evaluated against five questions:

1. **Objective.** Does it meet its stated objective?
2. **Sustainability.** Is it financially sustainable, in [[Solvency|solvency]] and funding?
3. **Efficiency.** What are its administrative cost and incentive effects?
4. **Equity.** Is it equitable?
5. **Interaction with the private market.** Does it crowd the private market out, complement it, or leave a gap?

Funding is the recurring weakness. Pay-as-you-go financing works while demographics are favourable and fails when they are not, and benefits set politically tend to be increased in good times faster than contributions are.

> [!example]- How does this public earthquake program fare against the five evaluation questions? {Example}
> A province proposes a compulsory public earthquake insurance program, funded by a flat levy on every property, to replace private earthquake coverage.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - **Objective.** Private take-up of earthquake coverage is low, and compulsion solves the adverse selection and under-insurance problems at once.
> > - **Sustainability.** Earthquake is a low-frequency, extreme-severity peril. The fund accumulates for decades and then faces a claim far exceeding it, so without reinsurance, a capital markets facility or an explicit government backstop the program is not sustainable.
> > - **Efficiency.** A flat levy is cheap to administer but removes the price signal. Building in a high-hazard zone costs no more than building on bedrock, which discourages mitigation, the same criticism made of [[Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements|disaster assistance]].
> > - **Equity.** A flat levy transfers cost from low-hazard to high-hazard property owners. Whether that is fair is a policy judgement, and the actuary should quantify the subsidy.
> > - **Interaction with the private market.** Replacing private coverage removes the insurers who price, model and reinsure this risk. A layered design, with public coverage above a private layer or public reinsurance behind private policies, usually does better than full replacement.
> > - Risk-based levies with a mitigation credit, and continued private participation, close the coverage gap at lower cost.

> [!trap] Treating cross-subsidy as a design flaw
> In private insurance a cross-subsidy is a pricing error. In social insurance it is intended, because contributions follow earnings rather than risk so that benefits are adequate for those who need them.
