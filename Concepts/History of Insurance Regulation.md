The **History of Insurance Regulation** in the United States is the development by which insurance, a business the Supreme Court once held was *not* commerce, came to be regulated by the states under a federal statute. Federal pressure in 1944, 1990, 1999 and 2010 has reshaped that settlement but never replaced it.

## State regulation before 1944

New Hampshire appointed the first state insurance commissioner in 1851. In *Paul v. Virginia* (1869) the Court held that "issuing a policy of insurance is not a transaction of commerce", which left regulation and taxation to the states. Their commissioners met in 1871 as the National Insurance Convention, known since 1935 as the **NAIC**, to handle what crossed state lines, beginning with uniform financial reporting.

Fire insurers priced collectively through rating bureaus. New York's Merritt Committee (1910–11) accepted cooperative rating as preferable to destructive price-cutting, provided the state supervised it. This became the model for regulated bureau pricing.

## SEUA and McCarran-Ferguson

In *United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association* the Justice Department prosecuted a fire-insurance rating bureau for price-fixing and boycotting. The Court held that insurance conducted across state lines **is** interstate commerce, subject to the Sherman Act. State rating laws built on bureau pricing, and state premium taxes, were suddenly exposed.

In the **McCarran-Ferguson Act** (1945) Congress declared continued state regulation and taxation to be in the public interest. No federal act supersedes state insurance law unless it specifically relates to insurance. The federal antitrust laws apply only to the extent the business is not regulated by state law, with boycott, coercion and intimidation excepted ([[Antitrust Law]]). The states answered with the 1946 **All-Industry** model rating laws, which require rates *not excessive, inadequate or unfairly discriminatory*. That standard is still applied to [[Emerging Regulatory Issues|new pricing tools]].

> [!example]- Why did the 1944 SEUA decision threaten the state system, and how did McCarran-Ferguson resolve each threat? {Example}
> - Once insurance was interstate commerce, bureau rating, where insurers agreed on common rates, was price-fixing under section 1 of the Sherman Act. McCarran-Ferguson applied federal antitrust law only where the business is not regulated by state law, so the states quickly passed rating laws bringing bureau activity under supervision.
> - Every state insurance law was open to displacement by federal statutes and to Commerce Clause challenge, including premium taxes that favoured domestic insurers. McCarran-Ferguson declared state regulation and taxation to be in the public interest and barred displacement by any federal act not specifically about insurance.
> - Congress had no insurance regulator ready to step in. A moratorium on antitrust enforcement, to 1948, gave the states time to legislate.
> - The price of the settlement was the boycott exception: collective refusals to deal remained federally actionable whatever the state did.

## Competition and control of rates

From the 1960s many states moved from prior approval toward file-and-use and open competition. California, long an open-rating state, went the other way with Proposition 103 (1988), which brought prior approval, a rate rollback and an elected commissioner.

## Federal pressure since 1990

A wave of insolvencies in the 1980s and the congressional report *Failed Promises* (1990) produced NAIC accreditation and RBC, the subject of [[History of Solvency Regulation]].

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999) allowed banks, securities firms and insurers to affiliate and reaffirmed McCarran-Ferguson. It adopted **functional regulation**, under which each activity is supervised by its own regulator, and threatened a federal producer-licensing body unless the states achieved licensing reciprocity. Optional federal charter bills followed, and none passed.

AIG's near-failure in 2008 was driven mainly by a derivatives unit that state insurance regulators did not supervise. It led to [[Dodd-Frank]] (2010), which created the Federal Insurance Office and FSOC and reformed surplus lines and reinsurance. The NAIC's own response, the Solvency Modernization Initiative, produced ORSA and governance disclosure ([[Insurance Governance]]).

> [!example]- What problem did each of SEUA, *Failed Promises*, Gramm-Leach-Bliley and Dodd-Frank respond to, and what state-level change followed? {Example}
> - SEUA (1944) responded to collusive pricing and was followed by state rating laws under McCarran-Ferguson.
> - *Failed Promises* (1990) responded to insolvencies and weak state supervision and was followed by NAIC accreditation, RBC and risk-focused surveillance.
> - Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999) responded to the convergence of banking and insurance and was followed by functional regulation and producer-licensing reciprocity.
> - Dodd-Frank (2010) responded to the systemic risk and group-level gaps AIG exposed. It created the FIO and FSOC federally, and the states followed with ORSA, Form F and group supervision.
> - Each wave of federal attention has been answered by state reform strong enough to keep the primary authority where McCarran-Ferguson put it.
