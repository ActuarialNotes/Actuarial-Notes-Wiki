An insurer's risk-based capital ratio is 340% of Authorized Control Level, above every action level. Its quarterly filings show premium up 60% in a year, after it began writing commercial auto through a managing general agent, and its liabilities are rising faster than its liquid assets. What can its domiciliary regulator do?

- A) Escalate the analysis and order a limited-scope examination, and act if it finds hazardous financial condition
- B) Nothing until the RBC ratio falls below an action level, because RBC is the only legal trigger
- C) Ask the NAIC to examine the insurer, since states cannot examine between scheduled exams
- D) Place the insurer in liquidation, because rapid growth through an MGA is unlawful

## Explanation

Off-site analysis exists to find a deteriorating insurer early. Regulators usually act on a finding of **hazardous financial condition**, often well before RBC is breached; RBC is a backstop that adds automatic authority. Growth this fast into a new line through delegated authority is a classic warning sign, so the analyst requests the business plan, controls and reserving support and schedules a limited-scope examination. If the examination finds, say, material under-reserving, the regulator can limit writings or require more capital.

- **B** treats RBC as the only tool, when the hazardous financial condition standard lets regulators act first.
- **C** gets the roles wrong: the domiciliary state examines its insurers, and the NAIC has no examination power of its own.
- **D** jumps to the last resort. Growth through an MGA is not unlawful, and liquidation comes only after corrective action fails.
