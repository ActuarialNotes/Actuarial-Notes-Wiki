The **Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators** (CCIR) is the association of Canada's provincial, territorial and federal insurance regulators. It has no legal authority of its own: it coordinates, harmonises and issues joint positions, which each member regulator must then adopt within its own jurisdiction.

Insurance regulation is split between the provinces and the federal government ([[Federal-Provincial Jurisdiction|federal-provincial jurisdiction]]). Without coordination, an insurer writing nationally would face fourteen different filing formats, complaint regimes and conduct expectations. The CCIR produces:

1. Harmonised annual filings, including the **Annual Statement on Market Conduct**. That statement is the CCIR's, distinct from OSFI's prudential [[Canadian Annual Return]].
2. The instructions for the P&C annual return filed with provincial regulators.
3. Joint guidance, with CISRO, on the **fair treatment of customers**.
4. Position papers, for example on auto insurance and travel insurance.

The CCIR is not a regulator, not a standard setter with legal force, and not a substitute for [[OSFI]] on solvency. A CCIR guidance document binds an insurer only once the relevant superintendent adopts it.

Two related bodies are often confused with it. **CISRO** is the association of insurance intermediary regulators, which covers agent and broker licensing. Internationally, the **IAIS** sets the Insurance Core Principles that Canada is assessed against.

> [!example]- The CCIR issues joint guidance on the fair treatment of customers. Is an insurer now legally bound by it? {Example}
> - Not by the CCIR itself, which has no legal authority.
> - The guidance becomes binding where a member regulator adopts it: by bulletin, by incorporating it into licensing conditions, or by treating departures from it as evidence in a market-conduct proceeding.
> - In practice the insurer should treat it as binding. Every major regulator has signalled adoption, and a superintendent can point to a departure from a published joint expectation when justifying an order.
> - The insurer's obligation still traces back to a provincial statute. The CCIR harmonises before the law, not instead of it.

## Harmonisation and local accountability

Harmonisation lowers compliance costs and improves comparability between provinces. A province that adopts a CCIR position, however, loses the ability to tailor it to a local problem such as an auto affordability crisis.
