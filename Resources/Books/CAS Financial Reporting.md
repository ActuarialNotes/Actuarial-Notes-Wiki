A CAS text on financial reporting and taxation as they affect reserving and statutory reporting, seen through a property/casualty actuary's lens. EY prepared it to replace a number of syllabus readings with a single publication: it walks through the U.S. statutory Annual Statement and the filings that accompany it, the measurement of financial health, the differences from GAAP, IFRS, Solvency II and U.S. tax, and Canadian-specific reporting. This version updates the Fictitious Insurance Company Annual Statement to 2018, reflects the U.S. tax law enacted in December 2017, and brings IFRS, Solvency II, Schedule F and the Canadian chapter current to 2018.

## Part I Introduction
- 1 Financial Reporting in the Property/Casualty Insurance Industry
    - Importance and Objectives of Financial Reporting
        - Financial reports let stakeholders — policyholders, claimants, investors, directors and management — and regulators track performance and compare companies under a set of common rules.
    - Overview of the Bases of Financial Reporting (Statutory, GAAP, IFRS, Tax, Canadian) and Differences in Terms of Use
        - [[Statutory Accounting Principles]] serve regulators' focus on solvency and tend to be conservative to give early warning of deterioration; certain illiquid assets are not admitted despite having economic value.
        - [[GAAP]] is not built on conservatism: it presents results in line with performance, historically by matching revenues and expenses, so acquisition costs are deferred where SAP expenses them as incurred.
        - In the U.S. the starting point for an insurer's taxable income is SAP income, and the insurance-specific tax adjustments tend to accelerate income (discounting loss reserves, reducing unearned premiums).
        - [[IFRS]] is set by the IASB; at the time of writing IFRS 4 let insurers report under their local rules with slight modifications.
- 2 Relevance of Financial Reporting to the Actuary
    - Importance and Objectives of Financial Reporting
        - An estimate of unpaid claims must match the accounting basis it is compared with: loss reserves are recorded net of reinsurance under SAP and gross under GAAP.
        - The accounting framework also changes the result of work on solvency monitoring, pricing, capital requirements, [[Risk Transfer|risk transfer]] of reinsurance, self-insurers' reserves, tax returns and valuations in mergers and acquisitions.

## Part II Overview of Basic Accounting Concepts
- Introduction to Part II
- 4 Primary Financial Statements
    - Primary Financial Statements
        - Most frameworks rely on the [[Balance Sheet|balance sheet]] and the [[Income Statement|income statement]], with the statement of [[Capital and Surplus|capital and surplus]] (or equity) and the [[Cash Flow Statement|statement of cash flow]], accompanied by notes.
    - Balance Sheet
        - Assets and liabilities at a point in time; under SAP their difference is statutory (policyholders') surplus, counting admitted assets only. The uncertainty in estimating loss reserves sets insurers' balance sheets apart.
    - Income Statement
        - Revenues less expenses over a period; insurance accounting standards address how premiums are earned and how and when claim costs are measured and recorded.
    - Capital and Surplus
        - Reconciles beginning to ending surplus for changes not recorded in the income statement, including items unique to insurers such as nonadmitted assets and the provision for reinsurance.
    - Cash Flow
        - Presents operations strictly from a cash perspective; less important for insurers, which collect premiums before paying losses, and not covered in detail.
    - Notes to Financial Statements
        - [[Notes to Financial Statements|The notes]] disclose the basis of accounting and details of balances that rest on estimates or are subject to uncertainty.
- 5 Key Accounting Concepts
    - Liquidation vs. going concern: investors are generally most interested in a business's value as a going concern, while regulators may think in terms of liquidation.
    - Fair value vs. historical cost: a trade-off between the reliability of historical cost, which is objectively verifiable, and the accuracy of fair value.
    - Principle-based vs. rule-based: rules may be easier to understand and to audit; principles adapt more readily to changes in the business environment.

- 25 Solvency II
    - [[Solvency II]] is a principle-based regulatory system for insurers in the European Union, in effect from January 1, 2016, that links required capital to risk profile through three pillars.
    - Pillar I — Quantitative Capital Requirements
        - Technical provisions are the discounted best estimate of the liabilities plus a [[Risk Margin|risk margin]] calculated by a cost-of-capital method at 6%.
        - The Solvency Capital Requirement is set at a one-year 99.5% value at risk, by the standard model or an approved internal model; below it the company faces regulatory intervention, and below the Minimum Capital Requirement it loses its licence.
    - Pillar II — Supervisory Activities
        - Requires internal audit, actuarial, risk management and compliance functions, and an own risk and solvency assessment ([[ORSA]]) reported to the supervisor.
    - Pillar III — Transparency
        - Disclosure of capital and regulatory position to supervisors and the financial markets, to increase market discipline.
    - Comparison to the U.S. Solvency Regime
        - The NAIC's Solvency Modernization Initiative gave state regulators "windows" into group operations while keeping the "walls" at the legal entity; the U.S. ORSA and its three-section Summary Report implement several of those windows.

## Part VII Canadian-Specific Reporting
- Introduction to Part VII
- 27 Overview of Financial Reporting in Canada
    - Overview
        - Insurance is regulated federally and provincially; most insurers are regulated federally by [[OSFI]], and since 1992 registered insurers have been required to appoint an [[Appointed Actuary|actuary]] to value policyholders' liabilities and report on the insurer's financial condition.
    - Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions
        - OSFI supervises all federally regulated financial institutions under a principles-based framework and, unlike the NAIC, has authority over the entities it regulates.
    - International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)
        - Regulated insurers, as publicly accountable enterprises, adopted IFRS from January 1, 2011; IFRS 4 let them keep Canadian GAAP valuation, under which liabilities follow [[Accepted Actuarial Practice|accepted actuarial practice]] — discounted, with a provision for adverse deviation.
        - Foreign branches differ in two ways: their assets are under the control of the Minister of Finance or the Chief Agent, and a head office account replaces share capital.
    - Canadian Institute of Actuaries
        - The CIA's [[Standards of Practice]] are binding on its members for work in Canada, and a deviation from a recommendation is presumed a deviation from accepted actuarial practice; [[Educational Note|educational notes]] are not binding.
    - Differences Between Statutory and Other Financial/Regulatory Reporting Frameworks in Canada
        - [[IFRS 17]], issued in May 2017 and effective January 1, 2023, measures groups of contracts as the risk-adjusted present value of future cash flows plus a [[Contractual Service Margin|contractual service margin]]; its simplified [[Premium Allocation Approach|premium allocation approach]] is expected to be widely adopted by Canadian P&C insurers.
