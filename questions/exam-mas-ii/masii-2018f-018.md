You are given the following statements regarding Hamiltonian MCMC.

- I. It is possible to use a relatively small number of effective posterior samples if you are only trying to measure the posterior mean.
- II. It is always necessary to run multiple chains to make inferences from the posterior distribution.
- III. In general, using weakly informative priors will improve the efficiency of the sampling compared to non-informative priors.

Determine which of the above statements are true.

- A) None are true
- B) I and II only
- C) I and III only
- D) II and III only
- E) The answer is not given by (A), (B), (C), or (D)

## Explanation

**I is true.** The Monte Carlo error of a posterior mean shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}}$, and a few hundred effective samples already estimate a mean well. It is the tails of the posterior — extreme percentiles and the ends of wide intervals — that are sparsely visited and need many more effective samples.

**II is false.** Multiple chains are how you *check* a sampler: running several from dispersed starting points and comparing them (with $\hat{R}$) is the standard test of convergence. But once a model is known to sample well, a single chain is enough to make inferences from. "Always necessary" is too strong.

**III is true.** With flat, non-informative priors the sampler is free to wander into absurd regions of the parameter space, producing wild chains, divergent transitions and poor mixing. A weakly informative prior that rules out implausible values tames the chain, so each iteration yields more effective samples.

I and III are true, so the answer is **C**.
