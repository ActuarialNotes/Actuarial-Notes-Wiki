**The Duty to Report** is the [[Appointed Actuary]]'s statutory obligation to escalate any matter that has **material adverse effects on the financial condition** of the insurer. Under the [[Insurance Companies Act]], the actuary reports first to the **CEO and CFO**; if the matter is not suitably addressed, to the **directors**; and if still not addressed, to **[[OSFI]]**.

> $$\text{CEO \& CFO} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Directors} \;\rightarrow\; \text{OSFI}$$

- **The trigger** is a matter with **material adverse effects on the financial condition** of the company. It is not limited to reserve deficiencies: an improperly accounted reinsurance contract, a projected [[MCT]] breach, a data failure that prevents a reliable valuation, or a governance failure preventing the actuary from doing the work all qualify.
- **The escalation is sequential, and each step must be given a real opportunity.** The actuary does not go straight to OSFI; management is entitled to address the matter, and the directors after them. What the actuary may not do is stop when an answer is unsatisfactory.
- **Civil immunity makes it usable.** Section 361 protects the actuary from liability for statements made in **good faith** in carrying out these duties. Without it the duty would expose the actuary to suit by the company they are required to report on.
- **Resignation is not a substitute.** Resigning without reporting leaves the matter undisclosed — the precise outcome the framework is designed to prevent. Both the company and the actuary must notify OSFI of a change in the appointment, and the actuary may state the reasons, which is what makes a dismissal over a valuation disagreement visible.
- **The successor actuary is part of the design.** Under the [[Standards of Practice]] an actuary asked to accept an appointment must communicate with the predecessor before accepting, so an insurer cannot escape a finding by replacing the person who made it.
- **Documentation is the actuary's protection.** Dates, what was reported, to whom, and what response was received. If the matter is later examined, the file is the record of whether the duty was discharged.

> [!example]- Working the Escalation {Example}
> In November the Appointed Actuary concludes that a reinsurance contract booked as reinsurance does not transfer significant [[Risk Transfer|insurance risk]] and must be accounted for as a deposit. Correcting it would reduce [[Capital Available]] by $\$45$ million and take the [[MCT]] ratio from $166\%$ to $141\%$ — below the $150\%$ supervisory target.
>
> The CFO responds that the auditors are comfortable and no change will be made. What does the actuary do?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > **Step 1 — done.** The matter was raised with the CFO. The response is that no change will be made, which is a decision, not a resolution.
> >
> > **Step 2 — the directors.** The threshold is plainly met: a $25$-point overstatement of the capital ratio, taking a reported position above the supervisory target to one below it, is material adverse effect on financial condition by any standard. The actuary reports to the **board** — in writing, setting out the risk transfer analysis, the $\$45$ million effect, and the resulting ratio.
> >
> > **On the "auditors are comfortable" point.** It is not an answer. The [[Risk Transfer]] assessment is an actuarial judgement the actuary is required to make; deferring it to the auditor is not permitted by the [[Standards of Practice]]. It is worth **speaking to the auditor directly** — they may not have been given the same analysis, and if they have and disagree, that disagreement should be on the record before the statements are signed.
> >
> > **Step 3 — OSFI.** If the board declines to act, the actuary reports to OSFI. This is not discretionary once the first two steps have failed.
> >
> > **In parallel, the [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion]].** If the financial statements reflect the contract improperly, the actuary cannot give an unqualified opinion — that obligation runs independently of the escalation.
> >
> > **What is protected and what is not.** Section 361 protects the reports made in good faith. It does not protect an actuary who says nothing: silence here would be a failure of both the statutory duty and the Rules of Professional Conduct, and the file would show the actuary knew.
