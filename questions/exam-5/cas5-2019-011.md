A risk manager has the following concerns about a quote for a large deductible policy compared to the company's current small deductible policy:

i. The expense provision in the rates includes expenses associated with claims below the deductible.
ii. The rates include a provision associated with the chance of the insured becoming bankrupt. This does not appear to be relevant to the exposure being underwritten.
iii. The profit margin is higher than the one used for the small deductible option.

Explain why each of the concerns may be invalid.

### Explanation
**i.** For large deductible policies, the insurer typically handles and pays all claims on a ground-up basis, including claims that fall entirely below the deductible. The insurer then seeks reimbursement from the insured for the deductible portion. Because the insurer is involved in administering and paying claims below the deductible (incurring claim handling, billing, and processing costs), it is appropriate to include a provision for those expenses in the rates. The concern is therefore **invalid** — the expense load is justified by the insurer's actual claim-handling activities below the deductible.

**ii.** Under a large deductible arrangement, the insurer pays claims ground-up and then bills the insured for the deductible portion. If the insured becomes bankrupt, the insurer may be unable to recover the deductible amounts it has already paid out. This creates **credit risk** for the insurer that does not exist (or is much smaller) under a small deductible policy. A provision for this insolvency/credit risk is therefore a legitimate cost of writing the business, making the concern **invalid**.

**iii.** Large deductible policies involve greater uncertainty and volatility in the losses that the insurer retains. Because claims below the deductible are ultimately reimbursed by the insured, the insurer's retained losses tend to be higher-severity (above the deductible), which are more difficult to predict and more volatile. Additionally, the credit risk exposure is greater for large deductible policies. Higher risk warrants a higher expected return (profit margin). Therefore, a higher profit margin for large deductible policies is appropriate, and the concern is **invalid**.

Alternatively:

Part (i) sample answers:

- Insurers typically handle claims below the deductible, then bill the losses to the insured.

Insurers are responsible for claims expenses below the deductible so it makes sense to price for them.

- The company may still have expense costs associated with claims below the deductible if they are handling all claims and getting reimbursed later. Therefore, the concern is invalid.

- Assume Insurer handles and pays claims ground-up, then seeks reimbursement for claims below deductible. Insurer still incurs claim handling expenses as well as billing and processing expenses. Invalid.

Part (ii) sample answers:

- If insurer pays the deductible and needs to be reimbursed for the deductible, then there is credit risk for the insurer. If the insured goes bankrupt the insurer may not be able to recover all deductible payments.

- If the company is handling all the claims below the deductible and the insured goes bankrupt, it will never be reimbursed for those claims thus a provision should be included in the rates. Therefore, concern is invalid.

Part (iii) sample answers:

- Higher deductible policies charge lower premiums and have more volatile loss experience from higher severity. Higher volatility is more risk which justifies a higher profit margin.

- Since high-deductible policies come with a greater level of risk (e.g., high severity and volatility), it makes sense for the company to want a greater return (i.e. higher profit margin) on that risk.

- Losses above a large deductible are more difficult to predict as data is sparser and more volatile at higher levels. Thus, it is appropriate to be compensated at a higher rate for taking on a higher level of risk.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the difference between a small deductible policy and a large deductible policy, including identifying common practices for large deductible policies and justifying the expenses that come with these unique practices.

Common mistakes included:

- Explanations in the wrong direction
- Not understanding the functionality of a large deductible policy
- Incomplete explanations such as identifying the common practice but neglecting to tie back to the need for expenses.
