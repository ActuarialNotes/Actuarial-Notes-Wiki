**Joint and Several Liability** is the [[Tort Law|tort]] doctrine that lets a plaintiff harmed by several wrongdoers recover damages from the defendants collectively or from any one of them individually. The plaintiff can take all of the damages from one defendant even if that defendant was only partly at fault. If the plaintiff collects $P_j$ from each other defendant $j$, what it collects from defendant $i$ is

> $$P_i = D - \sum_{j \neq i} P_j$$

- $D$ is the plaintiff's damages.

Under joint and several liability, $i$ owes whatever the plaintiff does not collect from the others. With every co-defendant insolvent, a defendant $1\%$ at fault pays $100\%$ of the loss, the outcome IBC objects to in [[Harris]]. That makes a solvent, insured co-defendant the "deep pocket", and the doctrine is a standing target of [[Tort Reform|tort reform]].

## Several liability

Under **several (proportionate) liability** each defendant pays its own share and no more. What the plaintiff collects from defendant $i$ is

> $$P_i = f_i \, D$$

- $f_i$ is defendant $i$'s share of fault.

When a co-defendant cannot pay, the plaintiff bears its share.

## Arguments for and against

The case for the doctrine is that the plaintiff is fully compensated as long as one solvent defendant is in the picture. "The number one rule of law is that the innocent party gets full compensation," says the Ontario Trial Lawyers Association in Harris. Trial lawyers add that it promotes settlement. If each party paid only a percentage, there would be more trials, and trials to fix each defendant's share of fault cost time and money.

The case against it is that it is unfair to make a defendant with a small share of fault carry most of the loss. It also breeds the **deep-pocket syndrome**, where plaintiffs name a defendant for its resources rather than its fault. A municipal insurer in Harris says, "we are often the deep pocket they come to. And all they have to do is find that 1%."

For the insurer, the doctrine raises the cost and the unpredictability of insuring a defendant that can be held responsible for more than its share. The leverage carries into mediation. Trial lawyers "know if they have a deep pocket there they can use it to the maximum", in the words of a school-board insurer in Harris.

## Reforms

The syllabus and the published answers name four reforms:

1. Replace it with **proportionate (several) liability**: "if you are in for 1%, just pay your 1%".
2. Bar joint and several liability for **non-economic damages** such as pain and suffering, keeping it for economic loss. This is the RIMS position in Harris.
3. Apply it only to defendants above a **fault threshold**. The published answers use $25\%$.
4. To answer the trial lawyers' concern, set up a **fund**, "similar to [[PACICC]]", to pay when the at-fault party lacks the means or coverage to indemnify the injured party.

> [!example]- How much does the municipality's insurer pay, and the plaintiff recover, under joint and several liability and under each of the first three reforms? {Example}
> A plaintiff who is not at fault has damages of $\$4{,}000{,}000$: $\$1{,}500{,}000$ economic and $\$2{,}500{,}000$ non-economic. The municipality is found $10\%$ at fault and is insured. A contractor is found $90\%$ at fault and is insolvent and uninsured.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Under joint and several liability the contractor pays nothing, so the municipality pays $\$4{,}000{,}000 - 0 = \$4{,}000{,}000$.
> > - Under proportionate liability the municipality pays $0.10 \times \$4{,}000{,}000 = \$400{,}000$, and the plaintiff bears a $\$3{,}600{,}000$ shortfall.
> > - With joint and several liability barred for non-economic damages, it still applies to the economic loss, so the municipality pays $\$1{,}500{,}000 + 0.10 \times \$2{,}500{,}000 = \$1{,}750{,}000$.
> > - With a $25\%$ fault threshold, the municipality at $10\%$ is below it and pays its proportionate $\$400{,}000$.
> > - In every case the plaintiff recovers only what the municipality pays, because the contractor pays nothing.
> > - The insurer's bill runs from $\$400{,}000$ to $\$4{,}000{,}000$ on the same verdict, depending only on the liability rule, which is why the doctrine makes such defendants costly and unpredictable to insure.
> > - Every reform that shrinks the insurer's bill leaves a gap of equal size in the plaintiff's recovery. A fund for the shares of insolvent defendants closes the gap without returning the cost to the $10\%$ defendant.

## Asbestos and mass torts

The major [[Asbestos|asbestos]] defendants went bankrupt, so plaintiffs pursued **peripheral defendants**. These were not the makers or sellers of asbestos but its users, or makers of products that encapsulated the major defendants' asbestos. Under joint and several liability those defendants can be liable for the full damages ([[Mass Tort]]). Where bankruptcy trusts replace once-solvent defendants, the published answer says total compensation is unaffected under joint and several liability. Under several liability it could rise, fall or stay the same.

> [!example]- How much can the plaintiff recover from this peripheral defendant? {Example}
> A peripheral defendant whose product contained another company's asbestos is $5\%$ at fault. The bankrupt major defendant was $95\%$ at fault.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Under joint and several liability the plaintiff can recover $100\%$ of the damages from the peripheral defendant.
> > - Under several liability it would pay $5\%$.
> > - The insolvency of the others is what turns a small share of fault into the whole loss.

## Tort reform in Canada

Harris found in 2005 that no province was actively looking at tort reform. Joint and several liability leads IBC's reform list in the article, alongside the [[Collateral Benefits|collateral source rule]], gross rather than net income as the basis for loss-of-income damages, and [[Vicarious Liability|vicarious liability]]. The common law, including negligence, is provincial, and Quebec operates on the civil code. Reform would therefore have to be made province by province.
