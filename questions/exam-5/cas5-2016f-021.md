An actuary is estimating the IBNR for a company using the data below, as of December 31, 2015.

| Accident Year | Reported Claims (\$000) | On-Level Earned Premium (\$000) | Reported Development Technique Ultimate Claims Estimate (\$000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,275 | 2,400 | 1,339 |
| 2013 | 1,152 | 2,300 | 1,355 |
| 2014 | 932 | 2,200 | 1,370 |
| 2015 | 604 | 2,100 | 1,332 |

- The actuary estimates the expected claims ratio to be 60% for all years.
- There is no loss trend.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Estimate the accident year 2015 IBNR using the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

### Answer
\$688,650 (688.65 thousand)

### Explanation
The Bornhuetter-Ferguson IBNR is the expected claims for the year multiplied by the portion of those claims not yet reported:

$$\text{IBNR}^{BF} = \text{OLEP} \times \text{ECR} \times \%\text{ unreported}$$

The percent unreported is not given directly, but the reported development technique's ultimate supplies it. That ultimate is reported claims times the reported CDF, so the reported and ultimate figures for accident year 2015 pin down the development pattern:

$$\%\text{ reported} = \frac{\text{Reported}}{\text{Ultimate}^{Dev}} = \frac{604}{1{,}332} = 0.45345$$

$$\%\text{ unreported} = 1 - 0.45345 = \frac{1{,}332 - 604}{1{,}332} = 0.54655$$

Equivalently, the implied reported CDF is $1{,}332/604 = 2.2053$ and $\%\text{ unreported} = 1 - 1/2.2053$.

Applying that to the expected claims:

$$\text{IBNR}^{BF}_{2015} = 2{,}100 \times 0.60 \times 0.54655 = 1{,}260 \times 0.54655 = \mathbf{688.65}\ (\$000)$$

so approximately **\$688,650**. Note that this is IBNR, not the ultimate — the corresponding BF ultimate would be $604 + 688.65 = 1{,}292.65$, and the two must not be confused.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recover the percent unreported from the ratio of reported claims to the reported development technique ultimate, then multiply it by expected claims (on-level earned premium times the expected claims ratio).

Common errors included reporting the development technique's own IBNR (ultimate less reported); reporting the expected claims technique's IBNR (expected claims less reported); multiplying expected claims by the percent *reported* instead of the percent unreported; and quoting the BF ultimate in place of the IBNR.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Fully assess the reasonableness of the 60% expected claims ratio assumption.

### Explanation
The check is to compare the assumed a priori against the claims ratios the data itself implies. With no loss trend and premium already on level, the reported development technique ultimates divided by on-level earned premium are directly comparable across years:

| Accident Year | Ultimate (dev) | OLEP | Implied claims ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,339 | 2,400 | 0.558 |
| 2013 | 1,355 | 2,300 | 0.589 |
| 2014 | 1,370 | 2,200 | 0.623 |
| 2015 | 1,332 | 2,100 | 0.634 |

The implied claims ratio rises steadily and materially — about 7.6 points over four years — with no year breaking the pattern. Two things follow.

First, the 60% assumption is **not** a reasonable single selection for all years: it is above the indication for 2012 and 2013 and understates 2014 and 2015, the two years where the BF estimate carries the most weight because the least is reported.

Second, and more fundamentally, the deterioration itself undermines the assumption. The Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique assumes a stable expected claims ratio that the actual emergence to date does not inform; a claims ratio trending consistently upward means one flat a priori cannot describe all four years, and the a priori for the most recent years should at minimum be re-selected from the recent experience rather than held at 60%.

Note that premium is on level and the problem states there is no loss trend, so neither rate change nor inflation explains the drift away — it is a genuine deterioration in experience.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute the implied claims ratio for all four accident years, note the clear upward trend, and state an opinion that the trend invalidates a flat 60% expected claims ratio.

Common errors included hedging or declining to state a clear opinion, asserting without support that the increase was random fluctuation, and computing the ratios but never commenting on the trend.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Recommend and briefly justify a technique that would be more appropriate than the Bornhuetter-Ferguson for this data set.

### Answer
The Cape Cod technique (the reported development technique was also accepted).

### Explanation
The defect identified in part b. is that the BF technique's expected claims ratio is an outside assumption, fixed at 60%, that does not update as experience deteriorates.

**Cape Cod.** The Cape Cod technique has the same structure as BF but derives its expected claims ratio from the experience itself, as reported claims over used-up (on-level, decayed) premium. That ratio is recomputed from the current data, so it picks up the deterioration automatically instead of holding an a priori the data has outrun. It keeps BF's stability advantage on immature years while removing the assumption that failed.

*Alternatively*, the **reported development technique** gives full weight to actual reported emergence and no weight to an a priori claims ratio at all, so it responds fully to the higher loss ratios — the paid development technique was accepted on the same reasoning.

Berquist-Sherman is not the answer here: it corrects for changes in settlement rates and case reserve adequacy, and the problem identified is a trend in loss ratios, not in the claims process.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to name either the Cape Cod technique or the reported development technique (paid development was also accepted) and to justify it — that the recommended technique is more responsive to updated data, that BF relies on an early a priori estimate that ignores current experience, or that the recommended technique incorporates the higher loss ratios. Partial credit was given for naming an appropriate technique without a valid justification.

Common errors included recommending a technique that does not address increasing claims ratios, and recommending Berquist-Sherman, which extends the reported development technique to handle changes in settlement patterns rather than trends in loss ratios.
