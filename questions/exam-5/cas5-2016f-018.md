An actuary is estimating unpaid claims for a company using the data below as of December 31, 2015.

| Accident Year | On-Level Earned Premium (\$000) | Paid Claims (\$000) | Unpaid Claims Estimate — Paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson Technique (\$000) | Unpaid Claims Estimate — Paid Development Technique (\$000) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 2,000 | 1,450 | 0 | 0 |
| 2013 | 2,000 | 1,000 | 102 | 100 |
| 2014 | 2,000 | 700 | 373 | 350 |
| 2015 | 2,000 | 400 | 622 | 500 |

- The actuary uses the same expected claims ratio for all years.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Calculate the expected claims ratio used in the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

### Answer
Approximately 56%.

### Explanation
The expected claims ratio (ECR) is not given, so it has to be backed out of the Bornhuetter-Ferguson unpaid estimates. The paid BF unpaid claims for an accident year are

$$\text{Unpaid}^{BF} = \text{OLEP} \times \text{ECR} \times \%\text{ unpaid}$$

so the ECR falls out once the percent unpaid is known — and the percent unpaid comes from the **paid development** column, not the BF column.

**Step 1 — recover the paid CDF from the development technique.** The development-technique ultimate is paid plus its unpaid estimate, so

$$\text{CDF} = \frac{\text{Paid} + \text{Unpaid}^{Dev}}{\text{Paid}}, \qquad \%\text{ unpaid} = 1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}}$$

| AY | Paid | Unpaid (dev) | Ultimate | Paid CDF | % unpaid |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,450 | 0 | 1,450 | 1.000 | 0.00% |
| 2013 | 1,000 | 100 | 1,100 | 1.100 | 9.09% |
| 2014 | 700 | 350 | 1,050 | 1.500 | 33.33% |
| 2015 | 400 | 500 | 900 | 2.250 | 55.56% |

**Step 2 — solve for the ECR in each year.**

$$\text{ECR} = \frac{\text{Unpaid}^{BF}}{\text{OLEP} \times \%\text{ unpaid}}$$

| AY | Calculation | ECR |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | $102 / (2{,}000 \times 0.0909)$ | 0.5610 |
| 2014 | $373 / (2{,}000 \times 0.3333)$ | 0.5595 |
| 2015 | $622 / (2{,}000 \times 0.5556)$ | 0.5598 |

Accident year 2012 gives nothing: it is fully developed, so both unpaid estimates are zero and the ECR cancels out of the identity.

**Step 3 — select.** The three years agree to within three thousandths, which is the confirmation that a single ratio was used for all years:

$$\text{ECR} = \frac{0.5610 + 0.5595 + 0.5598}{3} = 0.5601 \approx \mathbf{56\%}$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to invert the two techniques: read the paid claim development factor (and hence the percent unpaid) out of the paid development column, then solve the Bornhuetter-Ferguson unpaid formula for the expected claims ratio.

Common errors included using the BF unpaid to infer the development factor rather than the development-technique unpaid; substituting the paid-development unpaid into the BF formula; writing the ultimate formula (paid plus OLEP times ECR times percent unpaid) where the unpaid formula belonged; and dividing an ultimate by on-level earned premium, which yields an implied loss ratio rather than the a priori ratio the BF technique used.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Select an unpaid claims estimate for accident year 2015 from the two techniques given above and justify the selection.

### Explanation
Either estimate earns full credit; what is graded is the justification, which has to rest on how the two techniques respond to what the data shows.

**Selecting the BF estimate of \$622,000.** The implied 12-to-ultimate paid CDF for accident year 2015 is $900/400 = 2.25$ — only 44% of the ultimate is paid at 12 months, so the development technique multiplies a thin, immature base by a highly leveraged factor and any distortion in the paid figure is magnified. There is also a signal in the data: with premium flat at \$2,000 every year, the paid BF estimate exceeds the paid development estimate in each of 2013, 2014 and 2015, which is consistent with a slowdown in payments. The BF technique, which weights the a priori claims ratio by the percent unpaid, is far less responsive to that distortion, so the more stable **\$622,000** is preferred.

**Selecting the paid development estimate of \$500,000.** Compare the loss ratios the two techniques imply:

| AY | BF implied claims ratio | Paid development implied claims ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 | $1{,}450/2{,}000 = 0.725$ | $1{,}450/2{,}000 = 0.725$ |
| 2013 | $1{,}102/2{,}000 = 0.551$ | $1{,}100/2{,}000 = 0.550$ |
| 2014 | $1{,}073/2{,}000 = 0.537$ | $1{,}050/2{,}000 = 0.525$ |
| 2015 | $1{,}022/2{,}000 = 0.511$ | $900/2{,}000 = 0.450$ |

Both techniques show the claims ratio falling steadily, but the BF estimate is anchored to a 56% a priori that the experience has moved away from. Where the underlying loss ratio is genuinely improving, the technique that gives full weight to actual emergence is preferred, so **\$500,000** is selected.

A simple, weighted or Benktander average of the two was also accepted.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to pick one of the two estimates and justify it from a property of the technique behind it — the leverage of a 2.25 development factor on immature paid data, or the unresponsiveness of an a priori claims ratio to a real trend in experience.

Common errors included selecting the BF estimate and calling it "more stable" without saying what makes it stable; answering with an ultimate rather than an unpaid amount; and reading the decreasing paid claims down the column as a payment slowdown, when those figures are the paid amounts at year-end 2015 for accident years at four different maturities.

## Part c (1.5 points)

After constructing these estimates, the actuary learns of a change in the claims department in 2014 that has led to slower claims payments. Discuss whether the unpaid claims estimate from each technique below would be overstated or understated when calculated without making any adjustments to recognize the slower claims payments:

i. Expected claims technique

ii. Paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique

iii. Paid development technique

### Answer
(i) Neither overstated nor understated. (ii) Understated, but by less than the paid development technique. (iii) Understated.

### Explanation
The common thread is that the historical paid development factors were fitted to a faster payment pattern, so applied to slower-paying data they are **too low**. Each technique carries that error differently.

**(i) Expected claims technique.** The unpaid estimate is

$$\text{Unpaid}^{EC} = \text{OLEP} \times \text{ECR} - \text{Paid}$$

The first term is an a priori that does not look at emergence at all, so the payment slowdown leaves it unchanged. The second term does fall — that is exactly what a slowdown means. The estimate therefore rises by precisely the amount of payment that has been deferred, which is the correct response: the ultimate is unchanged and more of it is still outstanding. The estimate is **neither overstated nor understated**.

**(ii) Paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.**

$$\text{Unpaid}^{BF} = \text{OLEP} \times \text{ECR} \times \%\text{ unpaid}$$

Here the slowdown does bite: the true percent unpaid has gone up, but the percent unpaid used is $1 - 1/\text{CDF}$ built from development factors that are too low, so it is too small. The estimate is **understated**. It is understated less severely than the paid development technique, because BF is a credibility weighting of the expected claims technique — which part (i) showed responds correctly — and the paid development technique, so only the development half of the blend carries the error.

**(iii) Paid development technique.**

$$\text{Unpaid}^{Dev} = \text{Paid} \times (\text{CDF} - 1)$$

Both factors work in the same direction. The CDF is too low because it was fitted to a faster pattern, and the paid claims it is applied to are themselves depressed by the slowdown. A too-small multiplier on a too-small base gives the most **understated** of the three.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to work each formula through: for the expected claims technique, that the a priori term is unchanged while paid claims fall, so the unpaid estimate responds correctly in both direction and amount; for the BF technique, either that it is a credibility weighting of the other two or that its percent unpaid is too low because the historical development factors are too low; and for the paid development technique, that the understated CDF is applied to an understated paid amount.

Common errors included describing the ultimate as unchanged without following through to the paid figure and hence the unpaid; asserting the ECR itself must be wrong because it could have been contaminated by the slowdown; identifying the drop in paid claims correctly but then declaring the unpaid estimate over- or understated; phrasing an answer entirely in terms of ultimates rather than unpaid claims; and stating a direction with no supporting discussion.
