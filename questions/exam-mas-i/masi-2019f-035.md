Two variables, X and Y, exhibit the following relationship:

$$
Y_i = 1.5X_i + 2 + \varepsilon_i
$$

where $\varepsilon_i$ is a standard normal random variable, and each $\varepsilon_i$ is mutually independent.

For some sample of data, an actuary uses ordinary least squares regression of the form:

$$
Y_i = \hat{\beta}_1 X_i + \hat{\beta}_0 + e_i
$$

to estimate the relationship. The following parameter estimates were formed:

| Parameter | Estimate |
|:-:|:-:|
| $\hat{\beta}_0$ | 2.5 |
| $\hat{\beta}_1$ | 1.3 |

Calculate the bias of the estimate.

- A) Less than -0.1
- B) At least -0.1, but less than 0.1
- C) At least 0.1, but less than 0.3
- D) At least 0.3
- E) There is not enough information provided to calculate the bias.

## Explanation

Bias is a property of the estimator, not of one set of estimates: it is the gap between the estimator's expected value over repeated samples and the true parameter,

$$
\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\beta_j) = E[\hat\beta_j] - \beta_j
$$

Here the regression has the same form as the true relationship: $Y$ is linear in $X$ with intercept 2 and slope 1.5, and the errors are independent with mean zero. Under a correctly specified linear model, the OLS estimators are unbiased:

$$
E[\hat\beta_1] = 1.5, \qquad E[\hat\beta_0] = 2
$$

so the bias of each estimate is 0.

The differences in the table, $2.5 - 2 = 0.5$ and $1.3 - 1.5 = -0.2$, are the estimation errors of this one sample. Another sample would give different errors, and across samples they average to zero; a single set of estimates cannot reveal a bias.

A bias of 0 falls in $[-0.1, 0.1)$, so the answer is **B**.

*Note: the CAS answer key accepted both **B** and **E** for this question.* E was presumably credited because the question does not say which estimate it means, and a single sample's estimates cannot on their own be used to calculate a bias.
