A five-page study note (April 2023) by Erich A. Brandt and Laura A. Maxwell on how an Appointed Actuary identifies the materiality standard and decides whether there are significant risks or uncertainties that could result in material adverse deviation (RMAD), as NAIC Statements of Actuarial Opinion require. It describes materiality standards in use, two quantitative tests for RMAD, the qualitative considerations that go with them, and how often RMAD is found in practice.

> [!info] On the syllabus
> - [[Exam 6U (CAS)|Exam 6U]] — objective C7; the whole study note.

## Introduction
- NAIC [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion|Statements of Actuarial Opinion]] require the Appointed Actuary to identify the materiality standard and whether there are significant risks or uncertainties that could result in material adverse deviation.
- ASOP No. 36 states that the actuary should consider whether there are significant risks and uncertainties that could result in future paid amounts being materially greater than those provided for in the reserves.

## Materiality
- ASOP No. 36 does not define [[Materiality|materiality]] but includes it in its analysis of issues and recommended practices; the actuary evaluates it from professional judgment, any applicable materiality guidelines or standards, and the intended purpose of the statement.
- ASOP No. 1 treats an item or combination of related items as material if its omission or misstatement could influence a decision of an intended user.
- The AAA Task Force on Materiality defines materiality as an omission, understatement or overstatement in a work product that is likely to affect the intended principal user's decision-making or reasonable expectations.
- A table shows the materiality standards used in 2019 Property and Casualty SAOs: of 913 records, 646 (70.76%) used a percent of surplus, 251 (27.49%) a percent of reserves, 6 (0.66%) a risk-based capital measure and 10 (1.10%) another basis; 10% of surplus (320 records) and 10% of net held reserves (137 records) were the most common.
- Some Appointed Actuaries review several standards, such as 10% of surplus, 10% of held reserves and the difference between policyholders surplus and the Company Action Level risk-based capital amount, before selecting one that reflects the riskiness and complexity of the company.

## Risk of Material Adverse Deviation
- Two tests the Appointed Actuary may review are the Bright Line Indicator Test and a comparison of the range of reasonable reserve estimates to the sum of held reserves and the materiality standard (the Materiality Range Test).
- The Bright Line Indicator Test, from the NAIC Financial Analysis Handbook, can be used for companies subject to [[Risk-Based Capital|risk-based capital]] reporting; it compares 10 percent of the company's net loss reserves to the difference between RBC total adjusted capital and RBC company action level, and a worked example gives the Annual Statement references.
- The Materiality Range Test fails when held reserves plus the materiality standard fall below the high end of the range of reasonable estimates; two worked examples show one failing and one passing.
- Passing both quantitative tests is not sufficient to conclude that RMAD does not exist; qualitative considerations include long-tailed or volatile lines, catastrophes late in the year, unreported excess claims, high retained limits and retained excess layers.
- One or both quantitative tests could indicate RMAD while mitigating factors lead to a determination of no RMAD, which the regulator would consider very unusual.

## RMAD in Practice
- Of the SAOs reviewed in a recent study, approximately 30% determined that there were significant risks or uncertainties that could result in material adverse deviation: 35.3% of the stock insurers in the sample, against 9.8% of the mutual companies.
- The finding of an RMAD informs a company's Board of Directors that its booked reserves may develop beyond the principal user's expectations.
