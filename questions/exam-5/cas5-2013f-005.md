Given the following information:

- All policies are annual, and rate level is reviewed annually.
- Rate change takes effect on January 1, 2013.
- Unlimited annual loss frequency trend = -1%.
- Unlimited annual loss severity trend = +5%.
- Annual average written premium trend per exposure = 0%.
- Assume the exposures are inflation sensitive.
- Annual exposure trend = +1%.

| Calendar/Accident Year | On-Level Earned Premium (\$000s) | Reported Losses as of December 31, 2012 (\$000s) | Reported Losses Excess of \$1 Million as of December 31, 2012 (\$000s) | Unlimited Loss Development Factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2003 | \$60,612 | \$34,054 | \$456 | 1.00 |
| 2004 | \$61,941 | \$44,617 | \$4,888 | 1.00 |
| 2005 | \$66,893 | \$41,086 | \$5,348 | 1.00 |
| 2006 | \$67,092 | \$39,025 | \$8,774 | 1.00 |
| 2007 | \$65,960 | \$45,646 | \$8,134 | 1.00 |
| 2008 | \$65,037 | \$36,383 | \$0 | 1.00 |
| 2009 | \$65,242 | \$38,487 | \$1,398 | 1.00 |
| 2010 | \$67,732 | \$36,799 | \$0 | 1.03 |
| 2011 | \$69,450 | \$38,608 | \$2,002 | 1.08 |
| 2012 | \$67,213 | \$45,295 | \$9,000 | 1.20 |
| **Total** | **\$657,172** | **\$400,000** | **\$40,000** | **N/A** |

## Part a (1.5 points)

For accident year 2012, determine the trended ultimate loss ratio to use in the January 1, 2013 rate level analysis incorporating a large loss adjustment for claims above \$1 million.

### Answer

**Trended ultimate loss ratio for AY 2012 ≈ 0.752**

**Large loss (excess loss) factor**, from the whole ten-year history:

$$\text{Excess factor} = \frac{\sum \text{excess losses}}{\sum \text{reported losses} - \sum \text{excess losses}} = \frac{40{,}000}{400{,}000 - 40{,}000} = 0.1111$$

so limited losses are loaded by $1.1111$.

**Trend period:** from the average accident date of AY 2012 (July 1, 2012) to the average accident date of annual policies written in the year beginning January 1, 2013 (January 1, 2014) = 1.5 years.

**Loss ratio trend:** the pure premium trend is frequency × severity, $0.99 \times 1.05 = 1.0395$ a year. Average written premium per exposure has no trend, but the exposures are inflation sensitive and grow at +1% a year, so premium is trended at $1.01$ a year:

$$\text{Net loss ratio trend} = \left(\frac{0.99 \times 1.05}{1.01}\right)^{1.5} = (1.02921)^{1.5} = 1.0441$$

**AY 2012 trended ultimate loss ratio:**

$$= \frac{(45{,}295 - 9{,}000) \times 1.20 \times 1.1111 \times (0.99 \times 1.05)^{1.5}}{67{,}213 \times (1.01)^{1.5}} = \frac{48{,}393 \times 1.0441}{67{,}213} \approx 0.752$$

### Explanation

The large loss adjustment replaces each year's volatile excess losses with a long-run average load:

1. Remove the AY 2012 losses excess of \$1 million from its reported losses: $45{,}295 - 9{,}000 = 36{,}295$.
2. Develop the limited losses with the unlimited development factor (1.20), as CAS's sample answer does.
3. Load them by the long-run excess factor, $1 + 40{,}000/360{,}000 = 1.1111$.
4. Trend losses by $(0.99 \times 1.05)^{1.5}$ and premium by $(1.01)^{1.5}$.
5. Divide by AY 2012 on-level earned premium (\$67,213).

### Examiner Report

Many candidates skipped this question or received little partial credit with very few receiving full credit.

Candidates most often determined the appropriate trend period of 1.5 years. Candidates received credit for knowing the components of the loss ratio trend. Candidates did receive credit whether they treated the loss development factors given in the problem as either cumulative or incremental factors. Candidates struggled with the large loss factor. Many did not calculate it correctly and others did not apply it correctly. An example of incorrect application was not subtracting excess losses from reported losses in 2012 before applying the excess loss factor.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Discuss the appropriateness of using a large loss adjustment in part a. above.

### Answer

The large loss adjustment is appropriate for AY 2012 because the amount of excess losses (losses above \$1 million) varied considerably from year to year across the experience period (ranging from \$0 to \$9 million). This volatility means that any individual year's experience is an unreliable indicator of expected excess losses. AY 2012 had \$9 million in excess losses, the most of any year, which is likely not representative of future expectations. The large loss adjustment smooths the data by using an average excess loss provision based on the full historical period, resulting in more stable and credible indications.

### Examiner Report

Candidates needed to identify the volatility in the data and the benefit of stability in the indications. Multiple responses were acceptable for each piece, but candidates often were able to elaborate on only one component.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Assume the rate associated with the first \$1 million of coverage is analyzed using only the data above. Briefly discuss three modifications to loss and premium elements that would produce a more accurate rate analysis.

### Answer

Three modifications that would improve the analysis:

1. **Adjust the on-level premium to reflect only the first \$1 million of coverage:** The on-level premium should be reduced by the portion attributable to limits above \$1 million so that the premium matches the limited losses being analyzed. The adjustment may vary by year.

2. **Adjust the loss development factors to reflect the lower development associated with capped losses:** When losses are limited to \$1 million, late-emerging large losses above the cap do not contribute to development, so the unlimited development factors overstate development for limited losses. A separate limited-loss development factor should be used.

3. **Adjust the severity trend to reflect the lower trend applicable to capped losses:** Limiting losses to \$1 million reduces the impact of severity trend because losses at the cap do not increase further. A limited-loss severity trend (which is lower than the unlimited severity trend) should be applied.

### Examiner Report

Candidates often were able to identify 3 different enhancements, receiving full credit.
