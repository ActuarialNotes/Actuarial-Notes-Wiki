An article in the New England Journal of Medicine of March 18, 2010 (volume 362, number 11, pages 1014-1021) that asks whether cost-profiling tools can accurately distinguish higher-cost physicians from lower-cost physicians. Using 2004 and 2005 claims data from four Massachusetts health plans, it estimates the reliability of each physician's cost-profile score and the proportion of physicians who would be misclassified in a two-tiered insurance product.

## Abstract
- Background: insurance products with incentives for patients to choose physicians classified as lower-cost on the basis of cost-profiling tools are increasingly common, but no rigorous evaluation has determined whether the tools can accurately distinguish them.
- Methods: claims were grouped into clinically homogeneous episodes of care, assigned to a physician, and summarized as a cost profile; reliability (signal-to-noise ratio) was estimated on a scale of 0 to 1.
- Results: median reliabilities ranged from 0.05 for vascular surgery to 0.79 for gastroenterology and otolaryngology; 59% of physicians had cost-profile reliabilities of less than 0.70, and an estimated 22% of physicians would be misclassified in a two-tiered system.
- Conclusions: current methods for profiling physicians with respect to costs of services may produce misleading results.
