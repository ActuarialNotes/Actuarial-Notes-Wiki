Actuary A and Actuary B are each performing a reserve analysis for a small insurance company. To enhance credibility:

- Actuary A relies only on internal data, aggregated across all lines of business.
- Actuary B supplements internal data with industry data separately by line of business.

Describe the benefits and deficiencies of each of these two strategies.

### Explanation
Both actuaries face the same problem — a small company's data is too thin to produce stable development factors — and each buys volume by giving something up. The trade-off is **homogeneity against credibility**: A sacrifices homogeneity to keep the data its own, B sacrifices "its own" to keep the data homogeneous.

**Actuary A — internal data, all lines combined.**

*Benefits.* The data is entirely the company's own, so it reflects that company's actual operations — its settlement rates, case reserving practices, underwriting standards and claim handling — with no adjustment needed to make an outside dataset comparable. Pooling the lines raises the volume behind each development factor, and if the lines genuinely do share similar claim characteristics, severity and payment patterns, the combined factors are more credible than any one line's.

*Deficiencies.* Lines of business are usually **not** homogeneous. Combining a short-tailed line with a long-tailed one blends fundamentally different settlement rates, frequencies and severities into a single development pattern that describes none of them, distorting the estimate for every line. The aggregate is also sensitive to **shifts in the mix of business**: if the proportion written in each line moves over the experience period, the historical factors no longer describe the current book, and most estimation techniques perform poorly under a changing mix. For a small company, even the combined volume may still be too thin to be stable.

**Actuary B — internal data supplemented with industry data, by line.**

*Benefits.* Analyzing each line separately preserves homogeneity: long-tailed and short-tailed business are kept apart, so each line's development pattern describes claims that actually behave alike, and the factors are insulated from changes in the mix of business. Bringing in industry data supplies the volume the small company lacks, so the company's own experience can be credibility-weighted against a much larger base rather than left to swing on a handful of claims.

*Deficiencies.* The industry data is **not the company's own**. Its coverage levels, policy limits, underwriting guidelines, claim settlement philosophy, case reserve adequacy and mix of business may all differ from the company's, and those differences are largely unobservable to the actuary — so the external benchmark may need adjustment before it can be used, and there is limited information with which to make that adjustment. Splitting by line also cuts the internal data into smaller pieces, so each line's own experience carries less credibility than the aggregate would have.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the role of homogeneity and credibility of data in estimating unpaid claims, and to give both a benefit and a deficiency for each of the two strategies.

Common mistakes included vague responses that did not actually engage with the methodology each actuary used — for instance, never referencing the use of external data in Actuary B's estimate; answers where it was unclear whether a stated benefit or deficiency belonged to A or to B; and listing the desirable qualities of a complement of credibility instead of addressing the homogeneity and credibility considerations of the reserve analysis strategy itself.
