Given the following ground-up uncapped loss profile for a book of business:

| Claim Type | Number of Claims | Loss Amount of each Claim |
|---|---|---|
| A | 200 | $5,000 |
| B | 100 | $20,000 |
| C | 10 | $100,000 |
| D | 10 | $400,000 |

## Part a (1.25 points)

Calculate the increased limits factor for an increased limit of $25,000 and a basic limit of $10,000.

### Explanation
Limited average severity at each limit, over all 320 claims:

$$
\begin{align*}
\text{LAS}(10\text{K}) &= \frac{200(5\text{K}) + (100 + 10 + 10)(10\text{K})}{200 + 100 + 10 + 10} \\
                       &= 6.875\text{K} \\
\text{LAS}(25\text{K}) &= \frac{200(5\text{K}) + 100(20\text{K}) + (10 + 10)(25\text{K})}{200 + 100 + 10 + 10} \\
                       &= 10.9375\text{K} \\
\text{ILF}(25\text{K})  &= \frac{10.9375}{6.875} \\
                       &= 1.591
\end{align*}
$$

Equivalently, total losses capped at each limit: $3{,}500{,}000 / 2{,}200{,}000 = 1.591$.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know how to calculate an increased limits factor. Since the data provided was ground up & uncapped and both layers had the same frequency, the candidate could calculate either limited average severities or total capped losses at both $25k and $10k.
Common mistakes included:

- Calculating the Limited Average Severity as the total capped loss instead of taking the average.
- Taking a straight average of the loss amounts instead of incorporating the claim count distribution.

## Part b (1.5 points)

Calculate the severity trend for the layer excess of $50,000 assuming a ground-up severity trend of 10% over the next year.

### Explanation
Only the two claim types above $50,000 contribute to the layer. Trend each ground-up claim by 10% and re-take the excess:

| Claim size | Excess of 50,000 now | Trended amount | Excess of 50,000 after trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100,000 (10 claims) | 50,000 | 110,000 | 60,000 |
| $400,000 (10 claims) | 350,000 | 440,000 | 390,000 |
| **Total** | **4,000,000** | | **4,500,000** |

$$
\text{Severity trend in the layer} = \frac{4{,}500{,}000}{4{,}000{,}000} - 1 = 12.5\%
$$

The excess layer trends at 12.5% while the ground-up trend is 10% — leverage: a uniform ground-up increase moves proportionally more dollars into a layer that sits above a fixed attachment point.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to apply the ground up trend factor to the ground up losses, then calculate either the average claim size or the total claim amount excess of $50k both before and after trend. The excess trend is the ratio of the two calculated values.

Common mistakes included:

- Simply adding the various losses excess of $50k rather than taking the average or total losses in the layer.
- Calculating the average claim size for the excess layer including all claim counts. The average excess severity includes only the 20 claims that reach the excess layer.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Provide one reason why the data above would not be appropriate to determine an increased limits factor for $100,000 and suggest an alternative source that could be used.

### Explanation
There are too few claims at or above $100,000 for an increased limits factor at that limit to be credible — only ten claims reach $400,000 and none fall between $100,000 and $400,000. Use industry increased limits factors instead.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to note the small amount of claims excess of $100k and comment on the lack of credibility in the data due to size. A number of alternative sources were accepted such as industry data (from rating bureaus such as NCCI or ISO, or from other external sources such as RAA), competitor's analysis, additional internal data either from another similar line of business or by incorporating more years, and fitting a loss distribution curve to the data and modeling output for higher layers.

Common mistakes included:

- Assuming that the decrease in claim counts at higher layers violates the assumption that frequency be the same for various layers in an ILF analysis. Fewer claims at higher amounts is not necessarily a decrease in frequency but rather a product of a loss distribution where larger claims are not as common.
