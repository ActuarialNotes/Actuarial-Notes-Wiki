Given the following information for an insurance company:

| Limit of Liability | Current Increased Limits Factor | Indicated Increased Limits Factor |
|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 250,000 | 2.20 | 2.20 |
| 500,000 | 2.50 | 2.75 |
| 750,000 | 2.75 | 3.00 |
| 1,000,000 | 2.90 | 3.00 |

- The indicated increased limit factors are based on the company's own loss experience.
- Losses limited to \$100,000 have been consistent over time.
- Expected losses limited to \$100,000 = \$500,000,000.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Compare the expected losses for the excess layer between \$500,000 and \$1,000,000 based on the current increased limits factors and the indicated increased limits factors.

### Answer
Current: \$200 million; Indicated: \$125 million — the indicated losses are \$75 million lower

### Explanation
An increased limits factor is the ratio of expected losses limited to that limit to expected losses limited to the basic limit. The expected losses in a layer are therefore the basic limit losses times the **difference** between the two limits' factors:

$$\text{Layer losses} = E[\text{losses limited to basic limit}] \times \left(ILF_{\text{upper}} - ILF_{\text{lower}}\right)$$

**Current ILFs:**

$$\$500{,}000{,}000 \times (2.90 - 2.50) = \$200{,}000{,}000$$

**Indicated ILFs:**

$$\$500{,}000{,}000 \times (3.00 - 2.75) = \$125{,}000{,}000$$

**Comparison.** The indicated factors imply \$75 million *less* expected loss in the \$500,000 to \$1,000,000 layer than the current factors do — the indicated is only about 63% of the current. The company's own experience in this layer has been considerably better than the current rating structure anticipates.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the losses in the \$500K–\$1M layer under both the current and the indicated ILFs and to show a comparison between the two. Full credit required actually showing or stating the difference between the current and indicated losses.

Common errors included calculating both figures but making no comparison; calculating the expected losses for only one of the two sets of factors; using the wrong layer; and applying an incorrect formula for the losses expected in a layer.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Assess the appropriateness of implementing the indicated increased limits factors.

### Explanation
**The indicated factors are not appropriate to implement as they stand.** The decisive observation is that the indicated ILF at \$750,000 and at \$1,000,000 are **both 3.00**.

*The effect.* Equal factors mean the implied expected losses in the \$750,000 to \$1,000,000 layer are $500\text{M} \times (3.00 - 3.00) = \$0$. The company would charge exactly the same premium for a \$1,000,000 limit as for a \$750,000 limit, collecting nothing at all for a quarter of a million dollars of additional coverage. That is plainly inadequate: the layer has real, if remote, expected loss.

*The cause.* The indicated factors come from the company's own loss experience, and the equality almost certainly reflects that the company simply **experienced no losses above \$750,000** in the experience period. An absence of observed losses in a high, thinly populated layer is not evidence that the expected loss there is zero — it reflects a lack of credible data, and the ILFs at the top end are correspondingly unreliable.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to notice that the indicated ILFs for the \$750K and \$1M limits were equal, and therefore inappropriate, and to explain either the cause or the effect of that. Candidates who assessed the indicated ILFs as appropriate received no credit unless they commented on the inappropriateness of two separate limits carrying equal factors. Common errors included not identifying the equality of the two factors, and identifying it without offering a cause or an effect.

## Part c (0.5 points)

The company wishes to offer policy limits exceeding \$1,000,000 in the future. Propose an approach to calculating increased limits factors for the higher limits and briefly describe an implementation challenge the company may encounter.

### Explanation
**Approach — fit a severity curve to the company's loss data and extrapolate.** Fit a parametric severity distribution to the company's ground-up losses, then compute the limited expected value at each new limit from the fitted curve and take ratios to the basic limit LEV. Because the curve is defined above the range of observed losses, it produces a positive, internally consistent charge for layers in which the company has no loss experience.

Other acceptable approaches, provided the mechanism is stated: use **industry ILFs or bureau benchmarks** for limits above \$1M; **credibility-weight** the company's own indicated ILFs against those industry factors; compute the factors from **ground-up, uncensored** loss data rather than data capped at the policy limit; or **simulate** losses in excess of \$1M from a fitted distribution and compute the ILFs from the simulated data.

**Implementation challenge — curve selection and censored data.** Choosing the severity curve is not trivial, and the behaviour of the very largest losses is the hardest part of the distribution to model; different plausible curves can imply materially different charges in the highest layers. The company's own data compounds this, because losses are **censored at the current policy limits** — no loss above \$1,000,000 has ever been observed, so the tail of the fitted curve is driven by extrapolation rather than by data, and the resulting factors have limited credibility.

Alternatively: writing higher limits materially increases the volatility of the book, so the company may face a challenge **allocating additional capital** to support the new limits, or **purchasing additional reinsurance** to cover the new exposure.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to show how ILFs would be calculated for a limit the company had not previously offered, together with an implementation challenge of offering that higher limit. Full credit required explaining how the proposed approach actually leads to new ILFs — "use industry ILFs" or "credibility weight insurer ILFs with industry ILFs" were acceptable, but "use industry data" was not specific enough, and "use GLMs" received no credit without explaining how ILFs would be derived from them.

Common errors included offering an approach but no challenge (or the reverse), describing an approach too vaguely to show how the factors would be produced, giving a challenge unrelated to implementing higher limits, and explaining curve fitting incorrectly.
