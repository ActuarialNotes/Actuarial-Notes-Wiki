An article on the rise and fall of structured finance: pooling loans, bonds and mortgages and issuing prioritized tranches against the pool. Using the collateralized debt obligation (CDO) as its example, it shows how a capital structure amplifies errors in the default risks and correlations of the underlying securities, and how securitization substitutes highly systematic risks for largely diversifiable ones. It closes with an assessment of what went wrong. Published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, Volume 23, Number 1, Winter 2009, pages 3–25.

## Manufacturing AAA-rated Securities
- Manufacturing a security of a given credit rating means tailoring its cash-flow risk, measured by the likelihood of default and the magnitude of loss on default, to the guidelines set by the credit rating agencies; [[Structured Finance|structured finance]] does it in two steps, pooling and tranching.
- Pooling: a large collection of credit-sensitive assets is assembled in a portfolio called a "special purpose vehicle", kept separate from the originator's balance sheet to isolate the credit risk of its liabilities, the tranches, from the originator.
- If the vehicle issued only fractional, non-prioritized claims on the portfolio, the structure would be a pass-through securitization; the portfolio's rating would be the average rating of the securities in the pool and the claims would inherit it, so there is no credit enhancement.
- Tranching: a capital structure of prioritized claims is issued against the pool, and senior tranches absorb losses only after the junior claims are exhausted, which lets senior tranches be rated above the average rating of the collateral pool.
- The protection offered by the junior claims, or overcollateralization, determines the largest portfolio loss that can be sustained before a more senior claim is impaired.
- Two-bond example: two identical $1 bonds, each with default probability $p_D$, pooled into a $2 fund against which a junior tranche (bearing the first $1 of losses) and a senior tranche (which defaults only if both bonds default) are issued; the tranche default probabilities depend on the likelihood of both bonds defaulting together, which can be described by one parameter, the joint probability of default or the default correlation.
- With a 10 percent default probability and uncorrelated defaults, the senior tranche has only a 1 percent chance of default; with perfectly correlated defaults it inherits the 10 percent risk of the underlying bonds and there is no credit enhancement; intermediate correlations give a senior default risk between 1 and 10 percent.
- Junior tranches, being risky, have low prices and high promised returns, while senior tranches have relatively higher prices and lower promised returns.
- Adding a third $1 bond (10 percent default probability, uncorrelated) makes the senior tranche default 0.1 percent of the time, the middle tranche 2.8 percent and the junior tranche 27.1 percent, so two-thirds of the capital is repackaged into claims less risky than the underlying bonds.
- Reapplying the machinery to junior tranches: two $1 junior tranches (each defaulting with 19 percent probability) from separate two-bond pools can be combined and tranched again, giving a senior tranche with a 3.6 percent default probability; a collateralized debt obligation made from tranches of other CDOs is called CDO-squared (CDO²).
- The lower the default correlation across the underlying assets, the more improbable it is that all default at once and the safer the senior-most claim can be made; as defaults become more correlated, the senior-most claims become less safe.

## The Challenge of Rating Structured Finance Assets
- Credit ratings measure the ability of issuers or entities to meet future financial commitments, based either on the anticipated likelihood of default or on the expected economic loss, the product of the likelihood of default and the severity of loss given default, so a rating can be thought of as a measure of a security's expected cash flow.
- Rating agencies stress that ratings are designed only to give an ordinal ranking of long-run ("through-the-cycle") payoff prospects, whereas the expected-cash-flow reading takes a cardinal view.
- Corporate bonds rated BBB or higher are investment grade, thought to represent low to moderate default risk; those rated BB and below are speculative grade, already in default or closer to it.
- Table 1 reports Fitch's 10-year default probabilities and annualized default rates by rating at issuance, derived from historical data and used in Fitch's model for rating CDOs.
- Within the investment-grade range there are ten rating categories (AAA to BBB) although the annualized default rate varies only between 0.02 and 0.75 percent, so distinguishing between them takes a striking degree of precision; the ten speculative-grade categories (BB to C) have default rates from 1.07 to 29.96 percent.
- In the single-name rating business securities were assessed independently, leaving agencies agnostic about default correlation; rating structured finance securities forced them to characterize the entire joint distribution of payoffs for the collateral pool, since CDOs rely on diversification to achieve credit enhancement.
- The structure of CDOs magnifies the effect of imprecise estimates of default likelihoods, recovery amounts and default correlation, and of model errors from misspecified default dependencies; sequential application of capital structures, as in CDO², accentuates the problem.
- Simulation set-up: 40 CDO pools, each of 100 bonds with a five-year default probability of 5 percent and a recovery rate of 50 percent of face value (each bond would be rated BB by Table 1), with pairwise default correlation 0.20 within a pool and zero between pools.
- Defaults are simulated with a simplified version of the copula method, the industry standard for portfolio losses, in which a firm defaults if its variable falls below the percentile matching its default probability and dependence comes from the joint distribution, often the multivariate Gaussian.
- Each pool has three tranches: a junior tranche absorbing losses until the portfolio loss exceeds 6 percent, a mezzanine tranche absorbing losses between 6 and 12 percent, and a senior tranche absorbing losses above 12 percent; a CDO² ("CDO² [6–12]") is built by issuing a second capital structure against a pool of the 40 mezzanine tranches.
- Under the baseline parameters the original CDO's mezzanine tranche is rated BBB− and its senior tranche, 88 percent of the capital structure, AAA; the CDO²'s mezzanine and senior tranches are both rated AAA (Table 2: default probabilities of 97.52 percent for the CDO's junior tranche, 2.07 percent for its mezzanine and 56.94 percent for the CDO²'s junior).
- Figure 1 varies default correlation within each pool: the expected payoff of the underlying collateral does not depend on correlation; as correlation rises above 0.20, risk shifts from the junior to the senior claims, so the junior tranche's expected payoff rises and the mezzanine's falls.
- The mezzanine tranche's response is nonmonotonic: its expected payoff falls until correlation reaches 0.80, where it has lost approximately 10 percent of its value, and then rises as defaults become perfectly correlated; at perfect correlation each tranche faces the same 5 percent five-year default chance as each bond.
- The second-generation structure amplifies the shift: as pairwise correlation within the pools rises from 20 to 60 percent, the expected payoff on the CDO²'s mezzanine claim, investment grade at baseline, drops by 25 percent.
- Figure 2 varies the default probability of the underlying bonds with correlation held at 0.20: raising it from 5 to 10 percent cuts the expected payoff 55 percent for the junior tranche, 8 percent for the mezzanine tranche and 0.01 percent for the senior tranche, while the CDO²'s junior and mezzanine values fall quickly towards zero and its senior value falls substantially.
- Table 3 shows ratings after the changes: because investment-grade ratings are finely partitioned, even modest parameter changes can downgrade the original CDO's senior tranche (to A+ at a 10 percent default probability and BBB− at 12.5 percent).
- When the default probability rises to 10 percent, the CDO²'s mezzanine claim, initially AAA, loses 50 percent of its expected payoff and falls below the rating scale; a rise to 7.5 percent is enough to take the AAA-rated mezzanine CDO² claim to BBB.
- The ".SF" rating modifiers proposed by regulators for structured finance instruments are perhaps best regarded as warning labels, given the plausible uncertainty in the underlying parameters.
- With plausible overcollateralization (12 percent in the example) the senior tranche of the original CDO is well protected from large changes in default probabilities and correlations, but all tranches of the CDO² are highly sensitive to the baseline parameters, including the most senior claims.
- A large fraction of CDOs issued over the previous decade had subprime residential mortgage-backed securities as underlying assets, and since many of these are themselves tranches of an original securitization, CDOs of mortgage-backed securities are effectively CDO²s; with scarce historical data on subprime defaults, errors in default correlations, default probabilities and recovery rates, magnified by re-securitization, help explain the losses.

## The Relation of Structured Finance to Subprime
- Government-sponsored agencies (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Ginnie Mae) purchase mortgages that satisfy size and credit-quality requirements and repackage them into mortgage-backed securities resold with the implicit guarantee of the U.S. government; non-conforming mortgages are held by their issuers or sold in secondary markets.
- Origination of subprime mortgages grew from $96.8 billion in 1996 to approximately $600 billion in 2006, 22 percent of all mortgages issued that year, while the average credit quality of subprime borrowers fell (rising mortgage-to-house-price ratios, more second-lien loans, low or no documentation).
- Subprime mortgages, ineligible for agency securitization, reached capital markets through "private-label" mortgage-backed securities, which carried the dual risk of high default rates from low borrower credit quality and high default correlation from pooling mortgages from similar geographic areas and vintages.
- Many subprime mortgage-backed bonds were re-securitized into collateralized mortgage obligations, effectively creating a CDO²; according to Moody's, the share of CDOs with other structured assets as collateral rose from 2.6 percent in 1998 to 55 percent in 2006 of the total notional value of all securitizations, and structured finance CDO issuance reached $350 billion in notional value in 2006 alone.
- Every factor determining expected losses on tranches of CDOs backed by mortgage-backed securities was biased against the investor: overlap in geography and vintage raised default correlations, default probabilities and recovery values were worse than expected (including fire sales driving down related asset prices), and the prevalence of CDO² structures magnified the errors.
- The ABX.HE indices, compiled by Markit with major Wall Street banks, track subprime residential mortgage-backed securities along the rating spectrum; the ABX.HE.BBB 07-01 index traded as high as 98.35 but by August 2008 had an average rating of CCC and a market price of roughly 5 cents on the dollar.
- Each of the mortgage-backed securities in such an index loosely corresponds to a mezzanine tranche in the simulation, so CDOs backed by this collateral, in effect CDO²s, are virtually guaranteed to fail.

## The Pricing of Systematic Risk in Structured Products
- In the logic of the capital asset pricing model, securities correlated with the market as a whole should offer higher expected returns than securities with the same expected payoffs, or credit ratings, whose fortunes are less correlated with it; credit ratings by design assess only the risk to the expected payoff and say nothing about whether default is likely to coincide with a recession or a market decline.
- Securities with a given credit rating can therefore command a wide range of yield spreads over Treasuries of the same duration, depending on their exposure to systematic risks.
- At one end is a security whose default likelihood is constant and independent of the economic state, such as a traditional [[CAT Bonds|catastrophe bond]]: a single natural disaster cannot materially affect the world economy, so the bond's risks can be eliminated through diversification and it earns a spread consistent with compensation for expected losses.
- At the other end, the maximum yield spread for a given rating goes to a security whose defaults are confined to the worst economic states: a digital call option on the stock market, which pays $1 if the market is above a strike at maturity and $0 otherwise, with the strike chosen to match any desired credit rating.
- Pooling and tranching effectively create securities whose payoff profiles resemble a digital call option on the market index: pooling diversifies idiosyncratic default risks, so in the limit of a large diversified portfolio losses are driven entirely by systematic risk exposure.
- In effect, structured finance enabled investors to write insurance against large declines in the aggregate economy; senior-tranche investors bear enormous systematic risk and should earn more than on single-name bonds, whose defaults reflect firm-specific bad luck.
- Investors who do not appreciate the insurance they are writing earn a yield that looks attractive relative to similarly rated securities but is well below what writing such insurance directly, for example through options on a broad stock index, would earn; the authors cite their forthcoming "Economic Catastrophe Bonds" as evidence that senior-tranche yield spreads do not compensate for the systematic risk borne.
- Because the risks of corporate bonds and structured finance securities differ so much from a pricing standpoint, it is doubtful that they can be considered comparable, whatever the rating agencies choose to do.

## The Rise and Fall of the Structured Finance Market
- Issuance of structured finance products in the U.S. grew more than ten-fold in under a decade: $25–$40 billion per quarter in the first three quarters of 2005, a peak of about $100 billion per quarter from the last quarter of 2006 to the second quarter of 2007, and less than $5 billion per quarter in the first two quarters of 2008.
- Growth coincided with strong economic growth and few defaults, and many AAA-rated structured securities offered yields attractive relative to rating-matched alternatives such as corporate bonds; the yields were too low for the true risks because ratings were biased downward (a naive extrapolation of favorable conditions) and yields ignored the extreme exposure to declines in aggregate economic conditions, that is, systematic risk.
- The spuriously low yields on senior claims left holders of the remaining claims overcompensated, giving market participants an incentive to hold the "toxic" junior tranches, so demand grew for claims of every seniority; banks collected fees for origination and structuring, and demand for the collateral reduced borrowing costs for homeowners and corporations, fueling the real estate bubble.
- When subprime mortgage defaults started to rise, errors in default probabilities hurt all tranches, the junior ones first, and the CDO² structure, especially common in this market, magnified the errors so that even senior-most tranches could be significantly impaired.
- Rating agencies made some significant mistakes: in May 2008 Moody's acknowledged inadvertently giving AAA ratings to billions of dollars of structured products because of a bug in one of its models, and in March 2007 First Pacific Advisors found that Fitch used a model assuming constantly appreciating home prices, one that would break down completely if prices declined 1 to 2 percent for an extended period.
- The lack of understanding was shared by regulators who tied bank capital requirements to ratings and by investors who outsourced due diligence to rating agencies; none of the key parties seemed to recognize that small errors in rating individual securities, immaterial in the single-name market, are magnified in CDO tranches and further in CDO²s.
- Perverse incentives: the issuer, rather than the investor, pays for the rating; Mason and Rosner argue that rating agencies had to "become part of the underwriting team", whereas the Bank for International Settlements' Committee on the Global Financial System (2005) concluded that the conflicts of interest were no more severe than for single-name credit products.
- The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2008) found much to improve in the rating process, and that analysts and managers generally understood how their actions affected profits and could conflict with accurate credit risk assessment, citing internal communications at one rating agency.
- The investment banks played a dual role as investors and dealers; low capital requirements on AAA-rated assets and a perception that they were "safe" let banks hold unsold senior tranches, so when the market collapsed in late 2007 they held hundreds of billions of dollars of low-quality asset pools, among them leveraged buy-out loans, subprime mortgages and bonds from CDOs in process.
- Under Basel I and II, banks holding AAA-rated securities needed only half as much capital as for other investment-grade securities, creating built-in demand for AAA-rated securities, so a small yield advantage may have led to a large increase in the quantity demanded; the machinery produced AAA-rated securities with a yield advantage over single-name AAA securities only by filling them with systematic risk or by rating them incorrectly.

## Implications and Conclusions
- During the credit crunch from late 2007 into 2008 buyers of highly rated structured finance products largely stopped buying, and the authors take the skeptical view that the market for structured credit has serious structural problems that may be difficult to overcome.
- The claims are highly sensitive to three assumptions: default probability and recovery value, correlation of defaults, and the relation between payoffs and the economic states that investors care about most; from late 2007 each proved systematically biased against investors.
- The ability to create large quantities of AAA-rated securities from a pool of assets is likely to be forever diminished as the rating process evolves to account for parameter and model uncertainty, because small errors that are not costly in the single-name market are magnified by the CDO structure and again when CDOs are built from other CDOs' tranches.
- The authors propose that a [[Bayesian]] approach explicitly acknowledging that parameters are uncertain would go a long way towards fixing this, at the cost of far fewer AAA-rated securities issued and fewer opportunities to offer investors attractive yields.
- Investors need to recognize that, unlike corporate bonds driven by firm-specific considerations, securities created by tranching large asset pools are strongly affected by the performance of the economy as a whole; senior structured finance claims have the features of economic catastrophe bonds, designed to default only under extreme economic duress.
- Because credit ratings are silent on the state of the world in which default is likely to happen, they do not capture exposure to systematic risk, which reduces the usefulness of ratings however precise they are made.
