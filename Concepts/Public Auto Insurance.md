![[Media/Figures/Public_Auto_Insurance.svg|340]]

**Public Auto Insurance** is the delivery of compulsory automobile coverage by a government-owned monopoly rather than by competing private insurers. Four Canadian provinces use it in some form:

| Province | Public insurer | What it covers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| British Columbia | ICBC | Basic coverage, as a monopoly |
| Saskatchewan | SGI | Basic coverage, as a monopoly |
| Manitoba | MPI | Basic coverage, as a monopoly |
| Quebec | SAAQ | Bodily injury only, with property damage left to private insurers |

A basic-coverage monopoly still leaves a private market for optional and excess coverage in British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba.

## Arguments for and against the public model

The arguments made for the public model are:

1. A lower expense ratio, with no commissions, no advertising, no profit margin and a single administrator.
2. Universal availability, with no [[Residual Market|residual market]] problem.
3. Premiums collected efficiently with vehicle registration.
4. The ability to fund road safety programs from the same body.

The arguments made against it are:

1. No competition, and therefore no pressure to innovate or improve service.
2. A political dynamic that suppresses rates before elections and requires large catch-up increases afterward.
3. Cross-subsidies decided politically rather than actuarially.
4. No consumer choice at all where the monopoly extends to optional coverage.

A public insurer is evaluated with the [[Social Insurance]] framework: its objective, sustainability, efficiency, equity, and effect on the private market.

## Rate setting and capital

Rate setting is public. The insurer files with a provincial utilities board, and the hearing is adversarial and on the record. This makes the actuarial work unusually transparent and unusually contested.

A public insurer is not subject to [[OSFI]] and often not to the [[MCT]]. Its capital adequacy is governed instead by a legislated rate stabilisation or capital target. Where governments have drawn surpluses into general revenue, the resulting capital deficiency has been a recurring controversy, and ICBC's is the best-documented Canadian case.

## Comparing premiums across provinces

A cross-province premium comparison means nothing unless the product is held constant. Benefit levels, access to [[Tort Litigation|tort]], accident benefit limits, whether bodily injury is in the private premium at all, and vehicle and driving-density differences all vary. Quebec, where bodily injury is outside the private premium, is the clearest illustration ([[Autorité des marchés financiers]]).

> [!example]- Can one conclude from these figures that the public model is cheaper? {Example}
> Province X (public monopoly) reports an average auto premium of $\$1{,}150$ and an expense ratio of $18\%$. Province Y (private, competitive) reports $\$1{,}680$ and an expense ratio of $28\%$. Both have similar populations and vehicle counts.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Expenses are $0.18 \times 1{,}150 = \$207$ in X and $0.28 \times 1{,}680 = \$470$ in Y, a difference of about $\$263$.
> > - The rest of the premium is $\$943$ in X and $\$1{,}210$ in Y, so about $\$267$ of the $\$530$ gap comes from loss cost, not from efficiency.
> > - Benefit design may explain it. If X restricts [[Tort Litigation|tort]] access or caps [[Statutory Accident Benefits|accident benefits]] more tightly, its product is cheaper because it is a smaller product.
> > - Exposure may explain it. Traffic density, urbanisation, theft rates, weather, repair costs and litigation propensity all differ by province, and none is under the insurer's control.
> > - X's premium may not cover its costs. A public insurer running down a rate stabilisation reserve reports a low premium and accumulates a deficit, as ICBC did. The fair comparison uses the premium adequate to fund the benefits.
> > - If X funds part of its scheme from vehicle registration levies or general revenue, its premium understates the cost to the citizen.
> > - No. The figures show only that the public model has a lower expense ratio. The rest of the gap is product design and exposure, and attributing it to ownership is the error.
