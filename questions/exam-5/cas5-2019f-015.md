A workers compensation annual policy for a large insured is expiring on January 1, 2020. The following changes have taken place at the insured since the policy was last issued on January 1, 2019:

- The insured has implemented a new job-specific training program to reduce the expected number of claims.
- The insured has doubled the number of their employees.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Briefly describe how each of these changes could be accounted for in the final premium for the January 1, 2020 renewal policy.

### Explanation
**The new training program — schedule rating.** The program was only just implemented, so it has produced no loss experience yet and cannot be picked up by experience rating, which looks backward at historical losses. The underwriter can instead recognize it prospectively through a **schedule rating credit**, a subjective debit-or-credit adjustment for risk characteristics not otherwise reflected in the manual rate. The training program is exactly such a characteristic, so it earns a credit that lowers the final premium.

**The doubling of employees — manual premium via the exposure base.** Workers compensation is rated on payroll, so doubling the workforce roughly doubles the exposure. This flows straight through the **manual premium** calculation — rate times exposure — with no special adjustment needed; the premium rises because the exposure rose. The larger premium may additionally qualify the insured for a **premium discount**, since fixed expenses become a smaller percentage of premium as the policy grows.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how the short-term and long-term premium calculation is impacted due to insured changes. Candidates were expected to demonstrate an understanding of rating mechanisms.

Candidates were expected to know when to apply schedule rating versus manual rating and the affect (as printed) the policyholder's actions have on the final premium.

Common mistakes included:

- Discussing discounts for the training program, but no identification of what discount (schedule/underwriting) applies
- Providing general commentary about premium increasing or decreasing, but no demonstration of how the insured's actions affect the premium calculation.

## Part b (1 points)

Describe how each of these changes are accounted for in the final premium on January 1, 2024 if the insured has no additional changes over the next four years.

### Explanation
**The training program — now through experience rating, and the schedule credit is removed.** By 2024 the program has been in place four years, so its effect on claim frequency has emerged in the insured's own loss history. That history feeds the **experience modification factor**, which will fall to the extent the program actually reduced claims, lowering the premium on the strength of demonstrated results rather than underwriter judgment.

Critically, the **schedule rating credit granted in part a should now be removed**. Leaving it in place while the experience modification also reflects the program would double-count the same benefit. Schedule rating is the stop-gap used before the experience exists; once the experience exists, experience rating takes over.

**The doubling of employees — still simply exposure.** There is no change to the manual rates on account of the larger workforce. Premium remains higher than the 2019 level because payroll is higher, and the exposure continues to flow through the manual premium calculation exactly as before. If the insured's size qualified it for a premium discount, that continues to apply.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate an understanding of when experience rating applies and that the schedule modifications applied in part a. are removed. Candidates were also expected to demonstrate an understanding of how exposures are applied in the rating process.

Common mistakes included:

- Providing discussion related to how the long-term impact of the changes would affect loss experience with no demonstration of how that would be reflected in the premium calculation.
- Not recognizing that the discount given in part a. should be removed once the training program would be reflected in the experience.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Describe one additional rating mechanism that would benefit the company if the insured has grown substantially larger and more operationally complex between January 1, 2020 and January 1, 2024.

### Explanation
Any one rating mechanism not already relied on in parts a and b — so not experience rating and not schedule rating. For example:

**Retrospective rating.** The final premium is determined *after* the policy period from the insured's own losses during that period, subject to a maximum and a minimum premium. For a large, complex risk this is attractive on both sides: the insured gets an immediate financial incentive to control losses, and the insurer is protected because the premium adjusts to the losses that actually emerge rather than resting on an estimate made before a period of rapid growth and increasing operational complexity.

Alternatively:

- **A large-deductible policy**, under which the insured retains losses below a substantial deductible. This reduces the premium and transfers routine losses back to an insured now large enough to absorb them, while the insurer covers the volatility above the deductible.
- **A premium discount**, recognizing that expenses — particularly fixed acquisition and administrative costs — do not rise in proportion to premium, so a much larger policy should carry a lower expense ratio.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify a rating mechanism not relied upon in parts a. and b. and describe when the rating mechanism applies or what the rating mechanism is used for.

Common mistakes included:

- Discussing experience rating, introduction of a new rating variable, application of GLMs or commentary on ways that insured can reduce its loss experience.
