Given the following ground-up uncapped loss profile for a book of business:

| Claim Type | Number of Claims | Loss Amount of each Claim |
|---|---|---|
| A | 200 | \$5,000 |
| B | 100 | \$20,000 |
| C | 10 | \$100,000 |
| D | 10 | \$400,000 |

## Part a (1.25 points)

Calculate the increased limits factor for an increased limit of \$25,000 and a basic limit of \$10,000.

### Answer
1.591

### Explanation
An increased limits factor is the ratio of limited average severities at the two limits:

$$\text{ILF}(L) = \frac{E[X \wedge L]}{E[X \wedge B]}$$

The losses are ground-up and uncapped, so each claim is capped at the limit before averaging, and the average is taken over **all 320 claims** — not only those that reach the limit.

**Limited average severity at the \$10,000 basic limit.** Type A claims are below the limit and enter at their full \$5,000; types B, C and D all exceed \$10,000 and enter capped at \$10,000:

$$E[X \wedge 10{,}000] = \frac{200(5{,}000) + (100 + 10 + 10)(10{,}000)}{320} = \frac{2{,}200{,}000}{320} = \$6{,}875$$

**Limited average severity at the \$25,000 increased limit.** Now types A and B are both below the limit and enter at their full amounts; only C and D are capped:

$$E[X \wedge 25{,}000] = \frac{200(5{,}000) + 100(20{,}000) + (10 + 10)(25{,}000)}{320} = \frac{3{,}500{,}000}{320} = \$10{,}937.50$$

$$\text{ILF}(25{,}000) = \frac{10{,}937.50}{6{,}875} = \mathbf{1.591}$$

*Alternatively*, since both limits share the same 320 claims, the claim count cancels and the ratio of **total capped losses** gives the same factor directly:

$$\text{ILF}(25{,}000) = \frac{3{,}500{,}000}{2{,}200{,}000} = 1.591$$

This shortcut works only because the ILF assumes frequency is independent of the limit, so the same denominator sits under both layers.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to cap each claim at both limits and form either the two limited average severities or the two total capped losses, then take their ratio. Because the data is ground-up and uncapped and both layers share the same frequency, either route is valid.

Common errors included reporting the total capped loss as the limited average severity without dividing by the claim count, and taking a straight average of the four loss amounts instead of weighting them by the claim count distribution.

## Part b (1.5 points)

Calculate the severity trend for the layer excess of \$50,000 assuming a ground-up severity trend of 10% over the next year.

### Answer
12.5%

### Explanation
Trend applies to the **ground-up** loss, and the excess layer is then re-carved out of the trended amounts. The leverage comes from the fact that the \$50,000 retention does not trend — the whole 10% increase in a large claim falls into the excess layer.

**Losses excess of \$50,000 today.** Types A (\$5,000) and B (\$20,000) never reach the retention and contribute nothing. Only C and D pierce it:

| Type | Claims | Ground-up | Excess of \$50,000 | Layer loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 200 | \$5,000 | \$0 | \$0 |
| B | 100 | \$20,000 | \$0 | \$0 |
| C | 10 | \$100,000 | \$50,000 | \$500,000 |
| D | 10 | \$400,000 | \$350,000 | \$3,500,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **\$4,000,000** |

**Losses excess of \$50,000 after a 10% ground-up trend.** Every claim grows by 10% first; types A and B are still far below the retention:

| Type | Claims | Trended ground-up | Excess of \$50,000 | Layer loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 200 | \$5,500 | \$0 | \$0 |
| B | 100 | \$22,000 | \$0 | \$0 |
| C | 10 | \$110,000 | \$60,000 | \$600,000 |
| D | 10 | \$440,000 | \$390,000 | \$3,900,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **\$4,500,000** |

$$\text{Excess severity trend} = \frac{4{,}500{,}000}{4{,}000{,}000} - 1 = 1.125 - 1 = \mathbf{12.5\%}$$

*Alternatively*, work in average severities. The number of claims reaching the layer is unchanged at 20, so dividing both totals by 20 gives an excess severity of \$200,000 today and \$225,000 after trend, and $225{,}000 / 200{,}000 = 1.125$ — the same 12.5%. The excess trend exceeds the 10% ground-up trend because the retention is fixed in nominal terms; this is the leveraged effect of inflation on excess layers.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to trend the ground-up losses first, then compute either the total loss or the average loss in the layer excess of \$50,000 before and after trend, and take the ratio of the two.

Common errors included adding up the individual excess amounts without forming a total or an average consistently across the two calculations, and computing the average excess severity over all 320 claims rather than over the 20 claims that actually reach the layer — a denominator that must be the same before and after trend for the ratio to mean anything.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Provide one reason why the data above would not be appropriate to determine an increased limits factor for \$100,000 and suggest an alternative source that could be used.

### Explanation
**Reason.** Only 20 of the 320 claims reach \$100,000, and only the 10 type-D claims exceed it. An ILF at \$100,000 would therefore rest on a handful of observations and would not be credible — a single additional large claim, or the absence of one, would swing the factor materially. The data is too thin and too volatile at that limit to support a factor from the company's own experience.

**Alternative source.** Any of:

- Industry data from a rating bureau or statistical agent (ISO, NCCI) or an excess-loss data source such as the RAA.
- Competitors' approved filings and published rate pages.
- More of the company's own data — additional accident years, or a similar line of business combined with this one.
- Fitting a severity distribution (Pareto, lognormal, mixed exponential) to the full loss profile and reading the limited expected values off the fitted curve rather than off the empirical points.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to observe how few claims sit at or above \$100,000, tie that to a lack of credibility in the tail of the data, and name a workable alternative. Industry or bureau data, competitor filings, additional internal data from more years or a similar line, and curve fitting were all accepted.

A common error was arguing that the declining claim counts at higher loss amounts violate the ILF assumption that frequency is the same across layers. Fewer claims at larger amounts is simply what a severity distribution looks like; it is not a change in frequency.
