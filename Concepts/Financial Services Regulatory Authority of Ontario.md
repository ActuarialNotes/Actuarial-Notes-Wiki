The **Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario** (FSRA) is Ontario's insurance regulator, created in 2019 to replace the Financial Services Commission of Ontario (FSCO). It is self-funded, financed by industry assessments rather than the provincial budget, and principles-based, designed to be more outcome-focused and less rules-prescriptive than FSCO.

FSRA's responsibilities in insurance are:

1. Licensing insurers, agents and adjusters in Ontario.
2. Regulating [[Market Conduct Regulation|market conduct]].
3. Approving private passenger automobile rates and [[Risk Classification Restrictions|risk classification systems]] before they may be used.
4. Administering the [[Statutory Accident Benefits]] framework in practice: the [[Minor Injury Guideline]], the professional services guideline, and the arbitration and dispute rules.
5. Supervising the [[Facility Association]] and Ontario's [[Risk Sharing Pool|risk sharing pool]] arrangements.

FSRA does not regulate the solvency of federally incorporated insurers, which remains [[OSFI]]'s job. A national insurer therefore satisfies FSRA on price and conduct and OSFI on capital at the same time.

## Auto rate filings

Ontario private passenger auto is file-and-approve. An insurer files a proposed rate level and classification change, FSRA reviews the actuarial support, and the rates may not be used until they are approved. FSRA publishes approved average rate changes quarterly, which makes Ontario's rate history unusually public.

FSRA publishes technical notes and bulletins that tell filers what actuarial support it expects: trend selections, [[Loss Development|development]], expense and profit provisions, and the treatment of reform savings. An approved rate must reflect the cost level that will apply during the policy period, under the law as it will then be.

> [!example]- Which problems would FSRA raise with this rate filing? {Example}
> An insurer files for a 9.8% Ontario private passenger auto rate increase. The indication uses a loss trend of $+8\%$ a year selected from four years of the insurer's own data, about $1{,}200$ claims a year. No adjustment is made for a recently enacted benefit reform that industry analysis suggests will reduce accident benefit costs by roughly 5%.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - About $1{,}200$ claims a year over four years is thin support for an 8% trend. FSRA expects the filer to [[Credibility|credibility-weight]] its own experience with industry data and to show the fit rather than assert the selection.
> > - The rate omits an enacted benefit reduction, so it charges policyholders for costs the law has removed. This is the most common ground for rejection after a reform.
> > - The corrected indication would be materially below 9.8%.

> [!trap] Ignoring an enacted reform
> FSRA tests the cost of the coverage during the policy period, not the cost in the experience period. A filing that projects historical costs forward without removing the savings of an enacted reform will not be approved.
