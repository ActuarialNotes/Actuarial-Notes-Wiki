Given the following characteristics of two insurance companies writing business exclusively in the U.S.:

| | Insurance Company A | Insurance Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Lines Written | Homeowners | Auto Liability, General Liability |
| States Operating | Florida | All 50 states |
| Invested Assets | U.S. equities only | Foreign equities and foreign bonds only |
| Credit Rating | Strong with negative outlook | Strong with stable outlook |
| Reinsurance Used | Quota Share | Excess of Loss |
| Employee Count | 50 | 300 |

## Part a (2 points)

Identify four sources of risk that the two insurers face to different degrees and for each source identified, briefly contrast the two insurers' risk.

### Explanation
Any four, contrasting both companies, e.g.:

- **Currency risk** — B's invested assets are all foreign (equities and bonds), so B is exposed to currency risk; A holds only U.S. equities and has none.
- **Concentration risk** — A writes one line (homeowners) in one state; B writes several lines in all 50 states, so A is far more concentrated.
- **Catastrophe / event risk** — A writes a catastrophe-prone coverage in a catastrophe-prone state (Florida hurricanes) with only quota share reinsurance, which does not reduce extreme losses relative to premium. B writes lines less prone to catastrophe across all states, with excess of loss reinsurance that cuts extreme losses significantly — B has less catastrophe risk.
- **Liquidity risk** — A's negative credit outlook could lead to a downgrade and cash calls, and its catastrophe exposure could bring large cash calls after an event; B has neither threat, so less liquidity risk.
- **Net risk retention** — A's quota share leaves it exposed to many policies being hit at once by a hurricane; B's excess of loss cover reduces this tail risk.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to be able to identify at least four types of risks that insurance companies are exposed to and then contrast the two given companies' risk profiles in the four mentioned risk areas.
The following were the most common ways in which candidates lost credit:

1. Not describing both companies' risk profile for the risks named (often just describing one company and not mentioning the other company at all)
2. Naming a risk but describing a different risk (for example, stating market risk and then stating that Company A's market risk was high because they only wrote in one state, which is underwriting risk, not market risk)
3. Naming downgrade risk as a risk and then stating that Company A had downgrade risk because they had a negative credit outlook without explaining exactly what a downgrade would do (lower company bond value or share value, cause policyholders to leave, etc.).
4. Only listing three risks instead of four.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Propose two changes that Insurance Company A can make to reduce its risk profile

### Explanation
Two of:

- Buy excess of loss (catastrophe) reinsurance to protect against hurricanes.
- Expand into states other than Florida.
- Diversify the asset portfolio into bonds (preferably highly rated) to reduce market risk.

### Examiner Report
Most candidates received full credit for part b, suggesting two ways for Company A to reduce its risk profile, though quite a few candidates did not provide a response to part b.
The most common error was to state that Company A should improve its credit rating outlook, without suggesting specific ways to do so, which was not a full enough response to earn full credit.
