## Part a (0.5 points)

Briefly describe one similarity and one difference between the purposes of risk classification and individual risk rating.

### Explanation
**Similarity.** Both exist to match the premium charged to the expected losses of the insured more closely than a single average rate would — that is, both are ways of pricing at a more granular level than the overall rate, and both protect the insurer against adverse selection by doing so. Both work from historical loss experience and observable risk characteristics.

**Difference.** Risk classification achieves that granularity by *grouping* risks: insureds sharing similar characteristics are placed into classes, and relativities differentiate the classes from one another based on their experience relative to the whole. Individual risk rating instead prices a *single* risk largely from that risk's own loss experience and its own exposure characteristics, tailoring the rate to the insured rather than to a group it belongs to.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe one way the purposes of risk classification and individual risk rating are similar and one way they differ. Common mistakes included giving the purpose of both as overall rate adequacy rather than adequacy for an individual or a group; describing the purpose of risk classification as rate development for a single group rather than rate *differentiation* across multiple groups; and stating that risk classification groups similar risks without explaining how those groups are then used.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Briefly describe a situation for each of the following:

i. A classification rating plan is more appropriate than individual risk rating

ii. Neither a classification rating plan nor individual risk rating is necessary

### Explanation
**i. Classification rating is more appropriate.** A personal automobile book made up of a large number of small, homogeneous risks. No single insured is large enough for its own experience to be credible, so individual risk rating would price on noise; but there are many risks sharing characteristics such as age, territory and vehicle type, so grouping them into classes produces credible relativities and prices each group appropriately.

**ii. Neither is necessary.** A market in which the expected loss cost is genuinely the same for every exposure — every risk is equally risky, so there is nothing to differentiate and a single rate is adequate for all. The same is true where rates are set by the state regulator rather than by the insurer, so the insurer has no rating structure to design at all.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand when risk classification is more appropriate than individual risk rating and when neither is necessary, and to describe a situation satisfying each. Common mistakes included offering thin data or a new line of business as the situation, and naming a product or a risk characteristic without explaining how it satisfies the condition described.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Briefly describe three reasons a rating characteristic might not be included in a classification rating plan.

### Explanation
Any three distinct reasons, for example:

- **Not statistically significant.** The characteristic shows no measurable relationship to loss experience once the other variables are in the model, so including it adds complexity without improving the rate.
- **Highly correlated with another rating variable.** The characteristic is largely explained by a variable already in the plan, so it would double-count the same signal rather than add information.
- **Not objectively verifiable.** The value cannot be measured or confirmed reliably — it depends on the insured's self-report or on the underwriter's judgment — so it is open to manipulation and inconsistent application.
- **Prohibited by law or regulation.** The jurisdiction bars its use as a rating variable regardless of its predictive power.
- **Too expensive to collect or implement.** The cost of gathering and maintaining the data exceeds the value of the improved segmentation.
- **Raises privacy concerns**, or is socially unacceptable to policyholders even where it is legal.
- **Not credible.** Too little data exists at the level of the characteristic to estimate a stable relativity.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know what an actuary considers when deciding whether to include a rating characteristic, and to give three reasons one might be excluded. Common mistakes included describing fewer than three reasons, giving duplicate reasons in different words, and naming a reason a characteristic would be *more* likely to be included — such as being affordable to insureds or having a causal relationship to expected losses.
