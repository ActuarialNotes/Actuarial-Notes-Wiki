## Part a (0.5 points)

Contrast the components of IBNR for a claims-made policy and an occurrence policy.

### Explanation

IBNR has two main components: **pure IBNR** (claims that have occurred but have not yet been reported) and **IBNER** (incurred but not enough reported — development on known claims).

- **Occurrence policy**: IBNR includes both pure IBNR and IBNER. Because claims may be reported long after the accident date, the occurrence policy accumulates unreported claims over time, in addition to development on claims already known.
- **Claims-made policy**: At the end of a report year, all claims that occurred during the policy period and have been reported are known by definition. Therefore, a mature claims-made policy has **no pure IBNR** — its remaining development is limited to IBNER alone.

The key distinction is that occurrence policies have pure IBNR (due to the reporting lag between occurrence and report), while claims-made policies do not.

### Examiner Report

More than half of the candidates provided enough components of IBNR for both claims- made and occurrence to get full credit. Many candidates named only the pure IBNR component but did not state that it was the only difference between the policies. No credit was granted for candidates stating that Occurrence has IBNR and Claims-Made does not, because Claims-Made has IBNER, a component of IBNR.

Other candidates named additional components of IBNR, such as claims in transit or reopened claims. No credit was granted or deducted for these additional components, unless they were assigned incorrectly.

In general the majority of candidates seemed to understand the question and what was being asked. The most common mistakes were not including both Pure IBNR and IBNER in their contrast or simply stating that Claims-made has no IBNR.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Explain why a claims-made policy should cost less than an occurrence policy, provided claim costs are increasing.

### Explanation

A claims-made policy provides coverage for claims **reported** during the policy period, regardless of when the loss occurred. This means that claims-made losses are reported — and therefore priced — at approximately today's cost levels, with a relatively short gap between the loss event and claim reporting.

An occurrence policy, by contrast, must cover claims that **occur** during the policy period but may be reported (and ultimately paid) many years in the future. With rising claim costs, these future settlements will be at higher cost levels. The insurer must price for these higher future costs and faces additional pricing risk from the long reporting lag.

Because the claims-made policy's liability is established at today's (lower) cost levels while the occurrence policy's liability extends into the future at higher cost levels, the claims-made policy costs less when claim costs are increasing.

### Examiner Report

About half of the candidates received full credit for either some reference to Occurrence policies having claims reported further in the future at a higher cost level, or additional pricing risk associated with having to make a longer projection for Occurrence policies.

Several candidates received partial credit for showing a specific numeric example of lower costs, but without a full explanation of the cause.

Some candidates received no credit for simply stating that Claims-Made lack pure IBNR, or have no claims reported after the policy expiration, so the overall cost is less. However, these claims are balanced by claims reported from earlier accident years, such that it is the higher future cost levels (& additional pricing risk), not additional claims, that result in Claims-Made policies costing less than Occurrence policies.

Many candidates stated that Claims-Made policies have only one year of trend, or are fully settled &/or paid at the end of the year, while Occurrence policies have many years of trend. These responses received no credit, as it is the report lag that is shorter for the Claims-Made policies, not the settlement lag. Just like for Occurrence policies, inflation will act on Claims-Made policies for as long as the settlement lag lasts, which will likely be several years for a long-tailed line.

In general, a large number of candidates spent far too much time on this part. A simple statement with one or two sentences would have garnered full credit, but candidates seemed to misunderstand the intent and provided much lengthier responses – which cost them time and also increased the risk that they would misstate something resulting in only partial credit.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Explain why a change in underlying trends will impact the estimated premium for an occurrence policy more than for a claims-made policy.

### Explanation

The trend period for an occurrence policy extends from the average date of loss (the policy period) to the average date of settlement, which may be many years in the future. A change in the trend rate compounds over this long horizon, so the total impact on estimated losses — and thus the required premium — is substantial.

For a claims-made policy, the trend period only spans from the average date of loss to the date of claim **reporting** (which is within the report year). Settlement may still take years, but the base cost level is anchored to the report date rather than a distant future date. Therefore, a change in trend accumulates over a shorter period for claims-made policies.

Because a trend change leverages over a longer time horizon for occurrence policies, any shift in underlying trends has a greater impact on their estimated premium than on claims-made premium.

### Examiner Report

About half of the candidates received full credit for some reference to Occurrence policies having claims reported further in the future.

Several candidates received partial credit for showing a specific numeric example of the higher impact, but without a full explanation of the cause.

Many candidates stated that Claims-Made policies have only one year of trend, or are fully settled &/or paid at the end of the year, while Occurrence policies have many years of trend. These responses received no credit, as it is the report lag that is shorter for the Claims-Made policies, not the settlement lag. Just like for Occurrence policies, inflation will act on Claims-Made policies for as long as the settlement lag lasts, which will likely be several years for a long-tailed line.

Similar to part B, we found that candidate provided much lengthier responses than was necessary for full credit.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Briefly describe the provision that exists to eliminate coverage overlap if an insured switches from an occurrence policy to a claims-made policy, and why an overlap would exist without it.

### Explanation

**Provision — Retroactive Date:** A retroactive date is included on the claims-made policy. It restricts coverage to claims arising from losses that **occurred on or after** the retroactive date. Without a retroactive date, the new claims-made policy would cover any claim reported during the report year, regardless of when the loss occurred — including losses that occurred before the policy inception and are now being reported.

**Why overlap exists without it:** Suppose an insured had an occurrence policy in 2011 and switches to a claims-made policy in 2012. The occurrence policy (2011) already covers all losses that occurred in 2011, whenever they are eventually reported. The new claims-made policy (2012), without a retroactive date, would also cover a loss that occurred in 2011 and is first reported in 2012, creating a double-coverage overlap.

Setting the retroactive date to the inception date of the first claims-made policy (e.g., January 1, 2012) eliminates this overlap by restricting the claims-made policy to losses occurring on or after that date.

**Diagram:**

```
Occurrence policy (2011) — covers the diagonal: losses occurring in 2011, reported at lag 0, 1, 2, ...
Claims-made policy (2012, no retro date) — covers entire row: all losses reported in 2012
Overlap: Loss occurring in 2011 (lag 1) reported in 2012
```

| Lag → | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occ 2011 | L(11,0) | L(11,1) | L(11,2) |
| CM 2012 (no retro) | L(12,0) | **L(11,1)** ← overlap | |

### Examiner Report

More than half the candidates received credit for stating any of the following for the provision: retroactive date, first-year claims-made policy (or second-year, etc.), or for describing the provision as a date restricting the mature claims-made policy to cover only claims occurring on or after that date.

Several candidates did not get credit for the provision because they incorrectly described it as the date on or after which claims must be reported for the claims-made policy, which is simply the effective date of the claims-made policy.

About half of the candidates received partial credit for the overlap description using either a written description or a diagram showing at least one occurrence & claims-made policies, and where the policies intersected as the overlap.

Several candidates did not get credit for the written overlap description because they did not mention both the reporting & occurring situation for the overlap to happen, or they did not assign them correctly.

Several candidates did not get credit for the diagram overlap description because they labeled one axis as AY with the Occurrence policy on the diagonal, which is incorrect. Other candidates did not get credit for the diagram because they did not identify the following: the axis labels, the occurrence and claims-made policies & the overlap.

In both the written response and diagram, several candidates received no credit for describing the overlap as happening when both the claims-made and occurrence policies were effective at the same time (rather than in a subsequent year), which would cause an overlap regardless of the type of policy.

Based on the responses of the candidates, it does seem that they understood the question part and formulated appropriate responses. Some candidates did spend more effort than necessary elaborating on the provision and overlap rather than 'briefly describing' them as requested.

## Part e (0.5 points)

Explain why there would be a coverage gap if an insured switches from a claims-made policy to an occurrence policy, and what an insurer can do to provide coverage.

### Explanation

**Why a gap exists:** Suppose an insured had a claims-made policy in 2011 and switches to an occurrence policy in 2012. The claims-made policy (2011) covers losses that occurred in any prior year and were **reported** in 2011. The new occurrence policy (2012) covers losses that **occur** in 2012, reported at any future time.

Losses that occurred in 2011 (or earlier) but are **not reported until 2012 or later** are covered by neither policy: they occurred before the new occurrence policy period, and they were not reported during the old claims-made policy year. This creates a **coverage gap**.

For example, a loss occurring in 2011 but first reported in 2012 is not covered by the 2011 claims-made policy (not reported in 2011) and not covered by the 2012 occurrence policy (did not occur in 2012).

**Solution — Tail coverage (Extended Reporting Period Endorsement):** The insurer can attach an Extended Reporting Period Endorsement (tail policy) to the expiring claims-made policy. This endorsement extends the reporting window of the claims-made policy beyond its expiration, covering claims that occurred while the claims-made policy was in effect but are reported after its expiration, thereby filling the gap.

L(11,1) — occurs in 2011, reported in 2012 — falls in the gap. Tail coverage on the 2011 claims-made policy fills this gap.

### Examiner Report

Most candidates received at least partial credit for stating either of the following for the provision: tail policy or extended reporting endorsement. Similar responses were also accepted, as long as either the tail or extended reporting period for the claims-made policy was included in the response.

About half of the candidates received credit for the gap description using either a written description or a diagram showing at least one occurrence & claims-made policies, and the area between the policies where the gap would be.

Several candidates did not get credit for the written gap description because they did not mention both the reporting & occurring situation for the gap to happen, or they did not assign them correctly.

Several candidates did not get credit for the diagram gap description because they labeled one axis as AY with the Occurrence policy on the diagonal, which is incorrect. Other candidates did not get credit for the diagram because they did not identify the following: the axis labels, the occurrence and claims-made policies & the gap (or alternatively, the area where the tail coverage would fill in).

In both the written response and diagram, several candidates received no credit for describing the gap as happening when both the claims-made and occurrence policies were effective at the same time, rather than in a subsequent year.

As with part D, candidates did demonstrate a strong understanding of what was being asked, but some provided responses that were more involved than needed.
