Given the following data as of December 31, 2016:

**Industry Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 500,000 | 800,000 | 900,000 | 950,000 |
| 2014 | 400,000 | 750,000 | 880,000 | |
| 2015 | 450,000 | 750,000 | | |
| 2016 | 500,000 | | | |

**Industry Case Outstanding (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 200,000 | 300,000 | 50,000 | 0 |
| 2014 | 150,000 | 250,000 | 80,000 | |
| 2015 | 170,000 | 300,000 | | |
| 2016 | 230,000 | | | |

**Self-Insured Company Case Outstanding (\$000) as of December 31, 2016**

| Accident Year | Case Outstanding (\$000) |
|---|---|
| 2013 | 0 |
| 2014 | 80 |
| 2015 | 250 |
| 2016 | 400 |

- There is no development after 48 months.
- Industry cumulative reported claims development factor at 12 months = 2.100.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Calculate an accident year 2016 unpaid claim estimate for the company.

### Answer
Approximately \$1,649 thousand

### Explanation
The case outstanding development technique derives, from the **industry** data, a factor that converts case outstanding at a given maturity into total unpaid claims, and then applies that factor to the **company's own** case reserves. Accident year 2016 is at 12 months, so a 12-month factor is needed.

**Step 1 — build the industry paid triangle.** Paid = reported $-$ case outstanding:

| AY | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 300,000 | 500,000 | 850,000 | 950,000 |
| 2014 | 250,000 | 500,000 | 800,000 | |
| 2015 | 280,000 | 450,000 | | |
| 2016 | 270,000 | | | |

**Step 2 — paid age-to-age factors and the paid CDF at 12 months.**

| AY | 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 1.667 | 1.700 | 1.118 |
| 2014 | 2.000 | 1.600 | |
| 2015 | 1.607 | | |
| **Selected (avg)** | **1.758** | **1.650** | **1.118** |

With no development after 48 months the tail is 1.000:

$$CDF^{\text{paid}}_{12} = 1.758 \times 1.650 \times 1.118 = 3.242$$

**Step 3 — the case outstanding factor.** Let $U$ be ultimate claims for an industry accident year at 12 months. The two given development factors express the same ultimate two ways:

$$U = 2.100 \times \text{Reported}_{12} \qquad U = 3.242 \times \text{Paid}_{12}$$

so $\text{Reported}_{12} = U/2.100$ and $\text{Paid}_{12} = U/3.242$. Case outstanding is the difference, and unpaid claims are ultimate less paid:

$$\text{Case}_{12} = \frac{U}{2.100} - \frac{U}{3.242} \qquad \text{Unpaid}_{12} = U - \frac{U}{3.242}$$

$$\text{Case O/S factor} = \frac{\text{Unpaid}_{12}}{\text{Case}_{12}} = \frac{1 - \dfrac{1}{3.242}}{\dfrac{1}{2.100} - \dfrac{1}{3.242}} = \frac{0.69155}{0.16774} = 4.1228$$

Equivalently, $1 + \dfrac{(2.100 - 1)(3.242)}{3.242 - 2.100} = 4.1228$.

**Step 4 — apply to the company's case outstanding.**

$$\text{Unpaid}_{2016} = 4.1228 \times \$400 \text{ thousand} = \$1{,}649 \text{ thousand}$$

**Alternatively**, the technique can be run directly off the industry triangles with two ratios per period — the ratio of case outstanding to the prior case outstanding, and the ratio of incremental paid in the period to the prior case outstanding:

| Period | Case-to-prior-case | Paid-on-prior-case |
|---|---|---|
| 12-24 | $\tfrac{1}{3}(1.500 + 1.667 + 1.765) = 1.644$ | $\tfrac{1}{3}(1.000 + 1.667 + 1.000) = 1.222$ |
| 24-36 | $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.167 + 0.320) = 0.243$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}(1.167 + 1.200) = 1.183$ |
| 36-48 | 0.000 | 2.000 |

Rolling the company's \$400 forward: case at 24 = $400(1.644) = 658$, case at 36 = $658(0.243) = 160$, case at 48 = 0; incremental paid = $400(1.222) = 489$, $658(1.183) = 778$, $160(2.000) = 320$. Total unpaid $= 489 + 778 + 320 = \$1{,}587$ thousand. Both formulations were accepted.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to apply the case outstanding technique using the industry data and to apply the resulting case outstanding factor to the company's own case reserves to estimate unpaid claims.

Common errors included arithmetic mistakes in deriving the paid triangle, its age-to-age factors and the 12-to-ultimate paid development factor; mistakes in deriving the case reserve development ratios and the incremental-paid-on-prior-case ratios under the alternative formulation; and errors in the closed-form case outstanding factor itself, most often forgetting the $+1$ term.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Identify two potential limitations of the approach used in part a. above.

### Explanation
Any two of the following:

- **The industry factors may not be representative of this company.** The self-insured entity's case reserving philosophy, adequacy level, claim handling and settlement practices, mix of business and retention almost certainly differ from the industry composite, and the technique assumes the industry relationship between case outstanding and remaining unpaid claims holds for the company.
- **The factors are highly leveraged at early maturities.** At 12 months the case outstanding is a small base carrying a factor above 4, so a modest error in the industry factor — or in the company's case reserves — is magnified into a large error in the unpaid estimate. The method is volatile exactly where it is needed most.
- **It does not estimate pure IBNR.** The estimate is driven entirely off *known* case reserves, so an accident year with claims that have occurred but not yet been reported gets no explicit provision for them beyond whatever the industry ratio implicitly carries. If the company's reporting pattern differs from the industry's, that implicit provision is wrong.
- **A large loss sitting in case outstanding distorts the result.** Because the estimate is a multiple of the case reserve, one unusually large open claim inflates the projected unpaid claims proportionally, even though a single large claim is not expected to develop like the average claim.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the assumptions underlying the case outstanding development technique and to recognize the limitations of relying on industry data for a self-insured company.

Common errors included stating that the case outstanding method does not account for IBNR at all, when the correct statement is that it does not account for *pure* IBNR in future estimates, and offering a broad criticism of industry data ("industry data may be biased") without tying it to the case outstanding process specifically.
