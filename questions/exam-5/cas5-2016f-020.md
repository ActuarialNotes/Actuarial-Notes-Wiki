An actuary is calculating ultimate claim estimates for a long-tailed line of business using the frequency-severity technique using disposal rates. Given the following information:

- This line of business commonly has partial payments made on claims.
- Recently the statute of limitations was extended, resulting in a significant increase in claim counts at later development periods compared to previous years.
- The claims department has been strengthening case reserves for the last several years.
- There has been significant claim inflation over the last several years.
- The claims department has been attempting to settle claims faster.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Briefly describe two reasons the frequency-severity technique using disposal rates may be appropriate in the current situation.

### Explanation
Any two of the following, each tied to a specific fact in the problem:

- **Case reserve strengthening does not touch it.** The disposal rate technique is built entirely from *closed* claim counts and *paid* claims. Reported claims and case outstanding never enter the calculation, so the several years of case reserve strengthening — which would distort any reported-based technique — leaves this method's inputs unaffected.
- **Claim inflation is handled explicitly.** The technique projects severity separately from counts, so the significant claim inflation can be addressed directly by trending closed claim severities to a common cost level and applying a selected severity trend forward, rather than being buried inside aggregate development factors.
- **It suits a long-tailed line.** Separating frequency from severity avoids applying large, highly leveraged aggregate development factors to thin, immature data, which is where a development technique is weakest on a long-tailed line.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the mechanics of the frequency-severity technique using disposal rates and to identify which of the listed circumstances the method handles well — in particular that it is a paid- and closed-count-based method, so case reserve strengthening is irrelevant to it, and that it isolates severity so inflation can be trended directly.

The common error was simply restating bullet points from the problem without explaining why the method is appropriate for that issue.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Briefly describe two reasons the frequency-severity technique using disposal rates may not be appropriate in the current situation.

### Explanation
Any two of the following:

- **Partial payments break the severity assumption.** The disposal rate technique assumes paid claims relate to claims that have been closed, so that paid claims divided by closed counts is a true average settled cost. Where partial payments are common, the paid figures include amounts on claims that are still open, which inflates measured severity at each age and corrupts the severity development.
- **The statute of limitations change breaks the disposal rate assumption.** The technique requires a stable relationship between closed counts and ultimate counts across accident years. A statute extension that adds claim counts at later development periods changes both the ultimate count and the shape of the closing pattern, so historical disposal rates are not predictive of future ones. It also changes what a "claim" effectively is over time, and the technique requires a consistent claim count definition.
- **Severity trend selection becomes unreliable.** The method leans heavily on a selected severity trend. Significant claim inflation over several years makes that selection both more influential and harder to make accurately, so an error in the trend assumption is magnified in the ultimate.

Note that case reserve strengthening is *not* a valid objection here — this technique uses no case data at all.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify which of the listed circumstances violate the technique's assumptions: partial payments contaminate paid severity by age, and the statute of limitations extension destabilizes both the claim count definition and the disposal rate pattern.

Common errors included listing bullets from the problem without explaining why they make the method inappropriate, and citing the strengthening of case reserves, which does not affect a technique built on paid claims and closed counts.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Briefly describe an adjustment to the frequency-severity technique using disposal rates for each of the issues listed in part b. above.

### Explanation
One adjustment per issue raised in part b.:

- **Partial payments.** Exclude partial payments from the paid claims used to compute severity by age, and bring each claim's payments back in only in the period in which that claim actually closes. Severity by age is then a genuine closed-claim average and consistent with the closed counts it is divided by.
- **Statute of limitations / changing settlement pattern.** Use the Berquist-Sherman paid claims adjustment to restate the historical paid data to a common disposal rate basis, then run the disposal rate technique on the restated triangle. Alternatively, select disposal rates judgmentally, or from external and industry information reflecting the new statute, rather than reading them off a history written under the old one.
- **Severity trend.** Use industry data or another external benchmark as a complement of credibility when selecting the severity trend, so the selection does not rest entirely on a short and volatile company history.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to propose a workable adjustment for each problem they raised in part b. — restating data for the change in settlement rates, isolating partial payments from closed severity, or supplementing a trend selection with external data — so that the technique could still be used.

Common errors included offering an adjustment that did not address the problem identified in part b., and invoking Berquist-Sherman for case reserve adequacy rather than for the change in claim settlement rates, which is the relevant adjustment for a paid-based technique.
