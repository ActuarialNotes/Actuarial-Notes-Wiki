**Health Care Insurance** in Canada is the single-payer, provincially administered system ("medicare") that covers medically necessary hospital and physician services for all residents, funded from general taxation rather than from risk-based premiums. Private insurance is prohibited from duplicating covered services in most provinces, and instead fills the supplementary layer.

A province must satisfy the five principles of the *Canada Health Act* to receive full federal transfer payments:

1. Public administration.
2. Comprehensiveness.
3. Universality.
4. Portability.
5. Accessibility.

## Federal and provincial roles

Health care delivery is provincial ([[Federal-Provincial Jurisdiction]]). The federal role is the spending power: the Canada Health Transfer, conditional on the five principles. Ottawa influences a system it does not administer by paying for part of it.

## Services outside the public plan

Private insurance covers what the public plan does not: prescription drugs outside hospital, dental, vision, physiotherapy and psychology, private or semi-private rooms, and out-of-country care. Group benefit plans covering these are a very large private market beside the public one. Out-of-country travel insurance exists because portability covers only the provincial rate, which is a small fraction of a foreign hospital bill.

## The case for a single payer

Health care is the extreme [[Adverse Selection|adverse selection]] case, because the sick would buy and the healthy would not. Administrative cost is also far lower with one payer than with many. The counter-arguments are queues for elective procedures, the absence of a price signal, and no consumer choice of insurer.

## Interaction with property and casualty insurance

Provincial health insurers hold statutory subrogation rights to recover the cost of treating accident victims from at-fault parties, so auto and liability insurers pay health-system costs through subrogated claims. Auto [[Statutory Accident Benefits|accident benefits]] pay for treatment the public system does not cover. Canada's auto insurers are, in effect, a second health payer for accident victims, which is why a change in what the public plan covers moves auto insurance costs directly.

> [!example]- Who pays for each part of this accident victim's care? {Example}
> A person is seriously injured in an automobile collision caused by another driver. Their care includes emergency and surgical treatment in hospital, six months of physiotherapy, prescription drugs at home, a modified vehicle, and lost income.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Hospital and physician care is paid by the provincial health plan, at no charge to the patient. The province may then subrogate against the at-fault driver's liability insurer to recover its cost.
> > - Physiotherapy is outside the *Canada Health Act* basket, so the auto insurer's accident benefits respond, up to the applicable injury-tier limit ([[Minor Injury Guideline|MIG]] or non-catastrophic).
> > - Prescription drugs at home are paid by accident benefits, with a private group drug plan as an alternative payer. Coordination-of-benefits rules decide the order.
> > - Vehicle modification is paid by accident benefits within the medical and rehabilitation limit, effectively only at the non-catastrophic or [[Catastrophic Impairment|catastrophic]] tier.
> > - Lost income is paid first by accident benefits income replacement, then by a [[Tort Litigation|tort]] claim against the at-fault driver for the shortfall, with benefits already received deducted as a [[Collateral Benefits|collateral benefit]].

> [!trap] Assuming medicare pays for an accident victim's rehabilitation
> The public plan covers hospital and physician services only. Physiotherapy, drugs at home and other paramedical care fall to the auto insurer's accident benefits, which is why paramedical treatment dominates Canadian accident benefit costs.
