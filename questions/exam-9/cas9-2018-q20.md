A company writes in two distribution channels, A and B. At the start of a new year, the company decides to allocate a risk load to each renewing distribution channel using the covariance share method and the table of events below. The calculated risk load for channel A is \$2.024 million. The calculated risk load for channel B is \$1.070 million.

| Event | Probability | Channel A Loss (million) | Channel B Loss (million) | Covariance Share A | Covariance Share B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.005 | 24 | 8 | 1.43 | 0.48 |
| 2 | 0.010 | 20 | 10 | 2.64 | 1.32 |
| 3 | 0.010 | 15 | 15 | 2.23 | 2.23 |
| 4 | 0.015 | 15 | 7 | 2.12 | 0.99 |
| 5 | 0.020 | 12 | 7 | 2.08 | 1.21 |
| 6 | 0.025 | 8 | 9 | 1.65 | 1.86 |
| Total | | | | 12.15 | 8.09 |
| Variance | | 16.76 | 7.19 | | |

## Part a (0.5 points)

Determine the risk load multiplier used to calculate the risk loads.

### Explanation
Under the covariance share method, channel B's risk load is the multiplier $y$ times its variance plus its covariance share:

$$R_B = y\left(\text{Var}(B) + \text{CovShare}(B)\right) = 1.07$$

$$y(7.19 + 8.09) = 1.07 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = 0.07$$

Alternatively:

The method is renewal additive, so the multiplier can be taken from the total: total risk load $= 2.024 + 1.07 = 3.094$, total variance $= 16.76 + 7.19 + 12.15 + 8.09 = 44.19$, and

$$X = \frac{3.094}{44.19} = 0.070016$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the risk load multiplier, using the variance and covariance share of A or B.
Common mistakes include

- Using the wrong risk load formula
- Trying to calculate the risk load multiplier from the marginal surplus method

## Part b (1.25 points)

The company purchases a reinsurance contract that caps losses in distribution channel A at \$15 million. Assuming the company maintains the same risk load multiplier, calculate the change in the overall risk load.

### Explanation
Cap channel A's losses at 15 and recompute its variance and both covariance shares:

| Event | P | A | B | CS(A) | CS(B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.005 | 15 | 8 | 0.78 | 0.41 |
| 2 | 0.01 | 15 | 10 | 1.78 | 1.19 |
| 3 | 0.01 | 15 | 15 | 2.23 | 2.23 |
| 4 | 0.015 | 15 | 7 | 2.12 | 0.99 |
| 5 | 0.02 | 12 | 7 | 2.08 | 1.21 |
| 6 | 0.025 | 8 | 9 | 1.65 | 1.86 |
| Total | | | | 10.64 | 7.89 |
| Variance | | 13.28 | 7.19 | | |

Risk load for A $= 0.07 \times (13.28 + 10.64) = 1.6744$

Risk load for B $= 0.07 \times (7.19 + 7.89) = 1.0556$

Overall change $= 2.73 - 3.094 = -0.364$ million: the overall risk load decreases by \$0.364 million.

Alternatively:

Since risk load = multiplier × Var(A+B), the change in risk load = multiplier × the change in Var(A+B). The current $\text{Var}(A+B) = \text{risk load}/\text{multiplier} = (2.024 + 1.07)/0.07 = 44.2$. With A capped:

| Event | A | B | A+B | prob |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15 | 8 | 23 | 0.005 |
| 2 | 15 | 10 | 25 | 0.01 |
| 3 | 15 | 15 | 30 | 0.01 |
| 4 | 15 | 7 | 22 | 0.015 |
| 5 | 12 | 7 | 19 | 0.02 |
| 6 | 8 | 9 | 17 | 0.025 |

$\text{Var}(A+B) = \sum \left(L_i^2 - r_i (1 - r_i)\right) = 39$ (as printed; the binomial approximation applied term by term is $L_i^2 \times r_i (1 - r_i)$, as in another sample's $15^2 \times 0.005 \times 0.995 = 1.119$). The change in Var(A+B) is $44.2 - 39 = 5.2$, and the change in risk load is $5.2 \times 0.07 = 0.364$M: the risk load will decrease by 0.364M.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to restate the loss distribution for channel A according to the reinsurance contract described. Candidates were expected to compute the components of the risk load formula according to the new distribution to get the overall risk load and calculate the change from the original risk load.

Common mistakes include

- Calculating the change in risk loads for channel A only
- Not using the binomial approximation to calculate var(A) or var(A+B)
- Not restating the variance of A
- Not stating the sign of the risk load change (negative sign or reduction)
- Forgetting to calculate the overall change in risk loads (only calculating the overall risk load)
- Using the wrong formula to calculate the covariance shares
