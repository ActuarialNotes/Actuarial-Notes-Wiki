**Specialized Insurance Topics**, in the Exam 6U syllabus, are the parts of the U.S. market that regulation treats differently from the standard admitted, state-by-state model: [[Excess and Surplus Lines|excess and surplus lines]], [[Credit-Based Insurance Scoring|credit-based insurance scores]] and [[Risk Retention Groups|risk retention groups]]. Each illustrates a different regulatory tool.

| Topic | Regulatory tool | Who regulates |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Excess and surplus lines | A home-state rule | The insured's home state, for the placement |
| Credit-based insurance scores | A consumer-protection overlay on a rating variable | The Fair Credit Reporting Act plus each state |
| Risk retention groups | Federal preemption of multistate licensing | The chartering state only, for solvency |

## Excess and surplus lines

Nonadmitted insurers write risks the admitted market will not: unusual, hazardous or very large exposures. They are free of rate and form filing, which is the point of the market. Protection is aimed at the transaction instead:

1. Placement only through a licensed surplus lines broker, after a **diligent search** of the admitted market, unless the coverage is on a state export list or the insured is an **exempt commercial purchaser**.
2. Eligibility standards for the insurer: capital and surplus minimums for U.S. insurers, and for alien insurers the NAIC's *Quarterly Listing of Alien Insurers*.
3. Premium tax collected through the broker, and generally **no guaranty fund** protection.

Since the NRRA (2011), only the insured's home state regulates and taxes the placement ([[Dodd-Frank]]).

> [!example]- Admitted insurers have declined a Texas-headquartered fireworks manufacturer. How is its surplus lines placement regulated? {Example}
> - Texas, as the home state, alone regulates and taxes the placement.
> - The broker must be licensed in Texas, satisfy its diligent-search rule unless the insured is an exempt commercial purchaser, and remit the tax.
> - The rate and form are not filed.
> - A claim against an insolvent surplus lines insurer generally has no guaranty fund behind it.

## Credit-based insurance scores

Scores built from credit-report data are strongly predictive of personal auto and homeowners losses. A 2007 FTC study found them effective predictors of auto claims. They are contested because they correlate with income and race, and their causal link to loss is disputed. Three layers of rules apply:

1. The federal **Fair Credit Reporting Act** (FCRA) permits insurers to obtain reports and requires an adverse-action notice.
2. Most states follow the NCOIL model act (2002, readopted 2015). Credit may not be the sole basis to decline, cancel or non-renew, and listed items (medical collections, inquiries the consumer did not initiate) may not count against the consumer. The act provides re-scoring on request and exceptions for extraordinary life circumstances, and scoring models are filed with the regulator.
3. A few states ban it in auto, California, Hawaii and Massachusetts among them.

> [!example]- What protections does this homeowner have? {Example}
> A homeowner in a state following the NCOIL model had a renewal premium increase after a credit re-score during a medical emergency.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The homeowner can ask for an **extraordinary life circumstances** exception, and serious illness is a listed example.
> > - Medical collections cannot be used against the homeowner.
> > - The insurer must have sent an adverse-action notice under FCRA.
> > - Under the model act, the insurer must also have explained the principal credit factors behind the increase.

## Risk retention groups

Under the federal **Liability Risk Retention Act** (1986), a risk retention group (RRG) is a liability insurer owned by insureds with similar or related liability exposures. It is chartered and licensed in one state and may write in every other after registering. RRGs may write only commercial liability (no property, no workers compensation), and every policy must warn that state guaranty funds are **not** available.

Non-chartering states keep a limited role: registration, premium tax, unfair claims and deceptive practices laws, residual-market participation, and the power to seek an examination or an injunction if the RRG is in hazardous condition. Many RRGs are chartered as captives, which is why the NAIC extended accreditation standards to RRG domiciles and added governance standards, including independent directors.

> [!example]- How would a malpractice carrier formed by 300 physicians in several states be regulated? {Example}
> - The physicians share a related liability exposure, so they can form an RRG.
> - They charter it in one state, often a captive domicile, and register it in the others.
> - Only the chartering state regulates its solvency, while the other states collect premium tax and police claims practices.
> - If the RRG fails, **no** state guaranty fund will pay the members' claims.

> [!trap] Assuming a guaranty fund stands behind the policy
> State guaranty funds generally do not cover a surplus lines policy, and every RRG policy must warn that they are not available.
