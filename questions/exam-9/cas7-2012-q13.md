Given the following data and estimates for a personal lines insurer:

| Current Business | Expected claim count | Expected claim severity |
|---|---|---|
| Auto | 6,800 | \$700 |
| Homeowners | 9,000 | 750 |

| Loss Reserves | Claim count | Claim severity |
|---|---|---|
| Auto | 2,600 | \$2,100 |
| Homeowners | 400 | 1,250 |

The insurer's total losses are modeled by a lognormal distribution with the following Value at Risk (VaR) amounts for certain percentiles:

- $\text{VaR}_{95} = 20{,}346{,}511$
- $\text{VaR}_{96} = 20{,}540{,}313$
- $\text{VaR}_{97} = 20{,}795{,}018$
- $\text{VaR}_{98} = 21{,}132{,}866$
- $\text{VaR}_{99} = 21{,}680{,}765$

The risk based capital required for underwriting risk is given by the following factor based formula:

$$\text{TVaR}_{95} - \text{Expected Loss on Current Business} - \text{Loss Reserve}$$

## Part a (1.5 points)

Calculate the estimated risk based capital required to support this insurer's underwriting risk.

### Explanation
| | Expected loss, current business | Loss reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Auto | 6,800 × 700 = 4.76M | 2,600 × 2,100 = 5.46M |
| HO | 9,000 × 750 = 6.75M | 400 × 1,250 = 0.5M |
| Total | 11.51M | 5.96M |

$\text{VaR}_{95} = 20{,}346{,}511$, and $\text{TVaR}_{95} = 20{,}346{,}511 + \text{mean excess at } 95\%$.

The lognormal has an increasing mean excess function, so we would expect $\text{TVaR}_{95} > \text{VaR}_{97.5}$. Take the average of the mean excess at 98% and 99% as an estimate:

$$\text{TVaR}_{95} \approx 20{,}346{,}511 + \left(\frac{21{,}132{,}866 + 21{,}680{,}765}{2} - 20{,}346{,}511\right) = 21{,}406{,}816$$

$$\text{RBC} = \text{TVaR}_{95} - 11.51\text{M} - 5.96\text{M} = 3{,}936{,}816$$

Alternatively:

Estimate $\text{TVaR}_{95}$ as the average of the VaRs given at and above the 95th percentile:

$$\text{TVaR}_{95} = \frac{\text{VaR}_{95} + \text{VaR}_{96} + \text{VaR}_{97} + \text{VaR}_{98} + \text{VaR}_{99}}{5} = 20{,}899{,}095$$

$$\text{RBC} = 20{,}899{,}095 - 11{,}510{,}000 - 5{,}960{,}000 = 3{,}429{,}095$$

The examiners accepted any reasonable, well-explained estimate of $\text{TVaR}_{95}$; using $\text{VaR}_{95}$ itself was a common mistake.

### Examiner Report
For the a. part we accepted anything reasonable as long as it was well explained.  Some examples include TVaR based on VaR > 95%, average of excess, and assumed TVaR based on formula.

Common mistakes included using VaR95, arithmetic errors, and just writing down the numeric result without any explanation.

Common mistakes for the b. part included:

- Gave problem but no solution
- Creating a hypothetical situation that didn't make sense based on information provided For the c. part, also accepted:

The RBC calculated in part a is for underwriting risk only so a 50/50 quota share reinsurance should cut it in half.

Common mistakes:

- TVaR will not be cut in half
- Quota share won't take care of large losses and use excess of loss instead
- Bringing in premium as the issue

## Part b (1.5 points)

Briefly discuss three limitations with the above model, and suggest changes that would address each.

### Explanation
- **Catastrophes.** These lines could be prone to catastrophes; add a provision for catastrophes to the required RBC.
- **TVaR is linear in the tail.** Even with a fairly heavy-tailed lognormal, TVaR may not pick up enough of the extreme tail losses (auto liability, a horrendous catastrophe). Consider something like a distortion measure instead.
- **Tail correlation.** Losses on the two lines may be more highly correlated in the extreme tails (weather, etc.). If this isn't modelled, with something like a copula, the insurer may be exposed to much higher losses in extreme scenarios than the RBC it holds.

Alternatively:

- The lognormal may not be heavy enough in the tails for tail-based estimates of risk capital to be a good measure; consider modelling the losses and reserves stochastically to capture the heaviness of the tail.
- TVaR is linear in large losses, which does not reflect the desirable property that losses twice as large are more than twice as bad; RTVaR adds a loading for the standard deviation, weighting the tail more.
- A tail measure alone fails to capture risk that is not in the tail but could be significant; perhaps measure the distribution at a lower value than 0.95.

## Part c (1 point)

The CEO of this insurer wants to reduce the risk based capital calculated in part a. above by half going forward. The CEO plans to achieve this goal by buying 50% quota share reinsurance on the entire book of business. Fully explain why that will not achieve this goal.

### Explanation
- Reinsurance reduces risk somewhat, but credit risk remains: the reinsurer may default, especially in extreme scenarios, and the regulator is not likely to give full credit for reinsurance ceded.
- A quota share decreases losses uniformly, but losses in the extreme tails will still be very high. A main function of RBC is to hold enough capital for these extreme scenarios, so RBC would not be reduced by half.
- Those extreme scenarios are also when the reinsurer is most likely to default, in which case the insurer would pay nearly the full loss anyway.

Alternatively:

A 50% quota share will reduce the expected losses on future business, but it will not affect business previously written. The volatility of the loss reserves — which include the most severe losses — and of current business, which cannot be ceded retroactively, remains.
