Each person in a large population independently has probability $p$ of testing positive for diabetes, $0 < p < 1$. People are tested for diabetes, one person at a time, until a test is positive. Individual tests are independent.

Determine the probability that $m$ or fewer people are tested, given that $n$ or fewer people are tested, where $1 \leq m \leq n$.

- A) $\dfrac{m}{n}$
- B) $(1-p)^{m-n}$
- C) $1 - (1-p)^m$
- D) $\dfrac{1 - p^m}{1 - p^n}$
- E) $\dfrac{1 - (1-p)^m}{1 - (1-p)^n}$

## Explanation

Let $X$ be the number of people tested until the first positive. Testing stops at the first success, so $X$ is geometric with

$$
P[X = k] = p(1-p)^{k-1}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots
$$

Summing this geometric series gives the cumulative probability:

$$
P[X \leq j] = \sum_{k=1}^{j} p(1-p)^{k-1} = 1 - (1-p)^j
$$

The same result follows faster from the complement: $X > j$ requires the first $j$ tests all to be negative, which has probability $(1-p)^j$.

Because $m \leq n$, the event $\{X \leq m\}$ is contained in $\{X \leq n\}$, so their intersection is $\{X \leq m\}$ and

$$
\begin{align*}
P[X \leq m \mid X \leq n] &= \frac{P[X \leq m]}{P[X \leq n]} \\
                          &= \mathbf{\frac{1 - (1-p)^m}{1 - (1-p)^n}}
\end{align*}
$$

Choice (C) is the numerator alone — the unconditional probability. Choice (D) swaps $p$ for $1-p$ in the powers, which would count *positive* results in a row rather than negatives. Setting $m = n$ is a quick check — the answer must equal 1 — but it rules out only (C): (A), (B) and (D) all equal 1 there too. (B) fails another test: for $m < n$, $(1-p)^{m-n} = 1/(1-p)^{n-m}$ exceeds 1, so it cannot be a probability.
