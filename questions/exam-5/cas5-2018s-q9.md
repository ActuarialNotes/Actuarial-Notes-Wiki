Given the following information for two personal automobile policies:

**Three-Year Time Horizon for 21 Year-old Driver**

| Year | Age | Premium (\$) | Loss (\$) | Expense (\$) | Renewal Probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 21 | 1,215 | 1,200 | 50 |  |
| 2 | 22 | 1,200 | 1,125 | 20 | 75% |
| 3 | 23 | 1,185 | 1,050 | 20 | 75% |
| Total |  | 3,600 | 3,375 | 90 |  |

**Three-Year Time Horizon for 65 Year-old Driver**

| Year | Age | Premium (\$) | Loss (\$) | Expense (\$) | Renewal Probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 65 | 900 | 795 | 50 |  |
| 2 | 66 | 900 | 839 | 20 | 95% |
| 3 | 67 | 900 | 859 | 20 | 95% |
| Total |  | 2,700 | 2,493 | 90 |  |

|  |  |
|---|---|
| 5% | Annual discount rate |

- Policies are written on January 1.
- Premium is collected and the expense and losses are incurred on January 1.

Evaluate whether the 21 year-old or 65 year-old has a larger percentage return on premium over the three-year horizon.

### Explanation
Every cash flow occurs on January 1, so year 1 is not discounted, year 2 is discounted one year and year 3 two years at 5%. Each year's flows are also weighted by the probability the policy is still in force — the renewal probabilities **cumulate**, so year 3 uses the product of the two renewal probabilities.

**21 year-old driver** — in force: year 1 100%, year 2 75%, year 3 0.75 × 0.75 = 56.25%

| Year | Persistency | Discount | PV premium | PV loss + expense |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 1,215.00 | 1,250.00 |
| 2 | 0.7500 | 1/1.05 | 857.14 | 817.86 |
| 3 | 0.5625 | 1/1.05² | 604.59 | 545.92 |
| **Total** | | | **2,676.73** | **2,613.78** |

$$\text{Return on premium} = \frac{2{,}676.73 - 2{,}613.78}{2{,}676.73} = \frac{62.96}{2{,}676.73} = 2.35\%$$

**65 year-old driver** — in force: year 1 100%, year 2 95%, year 3 0.95 × 0.95 = 90.25%

| Year | Persistency | Discount | PV premium | PV loss + expense |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 900.00 | 845.00 |
| 2 | 0.9500 | 1/1.05 | 814.29 | 777.19 |
| 3 | 0.9025 | 1/1.05² | 736.73 | 719.54 |
| **Total** | | | **2,451.02** | **2,341.73** |

$$\text{Return on premium} = \frac{2{,}451.02 - 2{,}341.73}{2{,}451.02} = \frac{109.29}{2{,}451.02} = 4.46\%$$

The **65 year-old** has the larger percentage return on premium over the three-year horizon (4.46% versus 2.35%): the 21 year-old's first-year loss, and its lower persistency into the more profitable later years, weigh down its return.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to:

- Recognize the effect of persistency on premium volumes and profit dollars over a three-year horizon. This includes recognizing that the renewal probability is cumulative over the three years.
- Accurately discount cash flows (premiums, losses, expenses) over the three-year period.
- Calculate an underwriting profit measure that relates dollars of profit to premiums, incorporating both losses and expenses as part of the costs.
- Draw a conclusion on which of the two age groups provides the higher profit percentage over the three-year lifetime of the policy terms.

Common errors included:

- Not using a cumulative measure of persistency on year 3 or not using persistency at all in the calculations.
- Improper discounting of cash flows, including the use of incorrect discount factors or discounting only premium amounts.
- Not using discounted premiums in the calculation of a profit percentage.
