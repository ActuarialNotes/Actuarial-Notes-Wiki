The **Meredith Principles** are the five foundations of Canadian [[Workers Compensation Insurance|workers' compensation]], set out in Sir William Meredith's 1913 Ontario royal commission report and adopted, with local variation, by every province. They define the historic bargain: workers surrender the right to sue in exchange for guaranteed, no-fault benefits funded collectively by employers.

1. **No-fault compensation.** Benefits are paid regardless of who caused the injury, and neither the worker's carelessness nor the employer's diligence is investigated. This removes the three common-law defences that had defeated most injured workers' claims.
2. **Collective liability.** Employers are collectively liable through an assessment pool rather than individually liable to their own workers. A single catastrophic claim does not bankrupt one employer, and no worker's benefit depends on their employer's solvency.
3. **Security of payment.** A funded pool guarantees benefits will be paid for the life of the claim, which is why board funding ratios draw statutory and political attention.
4. **Exclusive jurisdiction.** The board alone decides claims, and the courts have no role. This eliminates litigation cost and delay, and it is the principle most in tension with the modern expectation of judicial review.
5. **An independent board.** The board is autonomous of government and of the parties, so that decisions are not politically driven.

The bargain cuts both ways. Workers lose the possibility of full tort damages, including pain and suffering, and lose access to the courts. Employers lose the ability to defend on fault and pay for injuries they did not cause.

## Modern strains on the principles

Exclusive jurisdiction is strained by the expectation of a right of appeal. [[Experience Rating|Experience rating]] pulls against pure collective liability. Coverage gaps open as work shifts to contractors and gig arrangements outside the definition of "worker".

> [!example]- Which Meredith principle does each of these developments strain? {Example}
> 1. A province introduces aggressive experience rating with large surcharges and rebates.
> 2. A board's funding ratio falls to $75\%$, and the government defers assessment increases to avoid burdening employers.
> 3. A worker seeks judicial review of a board decision denying entitlement.
> 4. Growth in gig and independent-contractor work leaves a large share of the workforce outside coverage.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Experience rating strains collective liability, because it moves cost back toward the individual employer. It improves safety incentives, but it also gives employers a financial reason to contest claims, which pulls against no-fault.
> > - The underfunded board strains security of payment. Benefits already awarded are not fully funded, and deferring assessments shifts their cost to future employers, the same intergenerational problem as an underfunded pension plan.
> > - Judicial review strains exclusive jurisdiction. Courts have generally deferred to boards, but every successful review is a small erosion.
> > - Gig work strains all five by omission. A worker outside the definition has no no-fault benefit, is not in the collective pool and has no security of payment, and keeps a right to sue that is worth little against a small employer. It is a coverage-definition problem rather than a funding one.

> [!trap] Describing only the workers' side of the bargain
> The bargain has two halves. Workers give up tort damages and the courts, and employers give up the fault defence and pay for injuries they did not cause.
