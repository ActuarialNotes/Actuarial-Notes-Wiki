**Dodd-Frank**, the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010), is the federal response to the 2008 financial crisis. For insurance it left state regulation in place and added three things:

1. A federal **monitoring** office, the Federal Insurance Office (Title V, Subtitle A).
2. A **systemic-risk** council, FSOC, able to put a nonbank financial company, an insurer included, under Federal Reserve supervision (Title I).
3. A **home-state** rule for surplus lines and reinsurance credit, the Nonadmitted and Reinsurance Reform Act (Title V, Subtitle B).

## The Federal Insurance Office

The Federal Insurance Office (FIO), in Treasury, monitors all lines except health insurance, most long-term care and crop insurance. It identifies regulatory gaps that could contribute to a systemic crisis, monitors access to affordable insurance for traditionally underserved communities, and can recommend insurers to FSOC for designation. It assists in administering the terrorism program ([[TRIA]]), coordinates federal policy on international insurance matters, including at the IAIS, and, with the U.S. Trade Representative, negotiates **covered agreements**. It reports annually to Congress. It has **no general supervisory, licensing or rate authority**.

The U.S.–EU (2017) and U.S.–UK (2018) covered agreements remove reinsurance collateral requirements for qualifying reinsurers from the other jurisdiction and limit the reach of foreign group supervision over U.S. groups. The states amended the NAIC credit-for-reinsurance models to conform rather than face preemption.

> [!example]- One state's homeowners rate regulation is suppressing rates enough to threaten availability. What can FIO do about it? {Example}
> - FIO has no authority to disapprove or order rates.
> - It can describe the problem in its annual report to Congress and in its monitoring work.
> - Its one preemptive power runs through a covered agreement, and even then the statute protects state measures governing rates, premiums, underwriting, sales practices and coverage requirements.
> - State capital or solvency measures can be preempted only to the extent they treat a non-U.S. insurer less favourably than a U.S. one, so rate regulation stays with the states.

## The Financial Stability Oversight Council

FSOC has ten voting members, one of them an independent member with insurance expertise. The FIO director and a state insurance commissioner sit as non-voting members. It can designate a nonbank financial company for Fed supervision and enhanced prudential standards. AIG, Prudential and MetLife were designated. A federal court rescinded MetLife's designation in 2016, and by 2018 no insurer remained designated.

## The Nonadmitted and Reinsurance Reform Act

The NRRA took effect in July 2011. Only the insured's **home state** may regulate and tax a nonadmitted placement, and eligibility standards for surplus lines insurers are made uniform. **Exempt commercial purchasers** may be placed without a diligent search of the admitted market ([[Excess and Surplus Lines]]). For reinsurance, an accredited ceding insurer's domiciliary state alone decides credit for reinsurance, and an accredited reinsurer's domiciliary state alone regulates its solvency.

> [!example]- How does the NRRA home-state rule change this policy's surplus lines tax? {Example}
> A surplus lines property policy with premium $\$400{,}000$ covers locations in states H ($50\%$ of the exposure), B ($30\%$) and C ($20\%$). The insured's principal place of business is in H. Hypothetical surplus lines tax rates are H $5\%$, B $4\%$ and C $6\%$. There is no interstate compact.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Allocated by location, the usual pre-2011 approach, the tax is $0.05 \times 200{,}000 = \$10{,}000$ to H, $0.04 \times 120{,}000 = \$4{,}800$ to B and $0.06 \times 80{,}000 = \$4{,}800$ to C.
> > - That totals $\$19{,}600$.
> > - Under the home-state rule, H taxes the whole premium: $0.05 \times 400{,}000 = \$20{,}000$.
> > - The tax bill barely moves, but one state's broker licensing, diligent-search rules, filings and tax replace three.
> > - The Act allowed states to share tax through a compact, but such compacts never became widespread.

## Other titles touching insurers

The Federal Reserve took over supervision of **savings and loan holding companies**, including insurance groups that own a thrift, when the Office of Thrift Supervision was abolished. The orderly liquidation authority (Title II) leaves insurance companies to state receivership. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was given no general authority over the business of insurance. Dodd-Frank also made smaller public companies' exemption from [[Sarbanes-Oxley]] §404(b) auditor attestation permanent.

For the regulatory history around the Act, see [[History of Insurance Regulation]] and [[State and Federal Insurance Regulation]].

> [!trap] Treating FIO as a federal regulator
> FIO monitors, reports and negotiates covered agreements. It does not license insurers, approve rates or supervise solvency, which remain with the states.
