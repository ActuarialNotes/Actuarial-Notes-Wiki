The following information is available for a single-state, mono-line insurer:

| Calendar Year (\$000) | 2013 | 2014 | 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|
| General Expense | 4,525 | 4,175 | 3,875 |
| Other Acquisition | 5,220 | 6,000 | 6,750 |
| Commissions/Brokerage | 8,700 | 8,000 | 7,500 |
| Taxes, Licenses and Fees | 3,480 | 3,200 | 3,000 |
| Total Expenses | 21,925 | 21,375 | 21,125 |

| Calendar Year (\$000) | 2013 | 2014 | 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Written Premium | 87,000 | 80,000 | 75,000 |
| Earned Premium | 90,500 | 83,500 | 77,500 |

The company's pricing actuary is asked to calculate an expense provision for 2016, and does so using a ratio of three years' total expense to three years' earned premium as follows:

$$\text{Expense provision} = \frac{21{,}925{,}000 + 21{,}375{,}000 + 21{,}125{,}000}{90{,}500{,}000 + 83{,}500{,}000 + 77{,}500{,}000} = 25.6\%$$

## Part a (0.75 points)

Briefly discuss three reasons why the actuary's approach is not appropriate.

### Explanation
Three distinct criticisms, any three of which earn full credit:

**1. It divides every expense category by earned premium, but expenses are not all incurred as premium is earned.** Commissions/brokerage and taxes, licenses and fees are incurred at policy inception and are proportional to *written* premium, so they belong over written premium; only general expenses and (partly) other acquisition are reasonably related to earned premium. Using a single earned-premium denominator mismatches the timing of the expense against the premium it is measured against, and the mismatch is material here because premium is falling.

**2. It treats every expense as fully variable when a substantial share is fixed.** General expenses and much of other acquisition do not scale with premium — they are largely fixed per policy. Loading them as a flat percentage of premium overcharges risks with above-average premium and undercharges those with below-average premium. The all variable expense method is only defensible when premium is stable and expenses genuinely scale with it; here written premium has fallen from \$87.0 million to \$75.0 million, a 14% decline, so the distortion is not academic.

**3. Averaging three years gives the most weight to the oldest, least relevant year.** The book is shrinking, so 2013 contributes the largest premium and the largest expense dollars to the ratio, yet it is the year least representative of 2016 conditions. A weighting that leans on the most recent experience, or a straight average of the annual ratios, would reflect the current book better.

**4. It hides a clear upward pattern in the expense ratio.** Computed year by year the ratio is $21{,}925/90{,}500 = 24.2\%$, then $21{,}375/83{,}500 = 25.6\%$, then $21{,}125/77{,}500 = 27.3\%$ — expenses are growing relative to premium every year, and other acquisition is rising in absolute dollars while every other category falls. Collapsing three years into one aggregate ratio suppresses that signal. The actuary should compute a ratio for each category in each year, look for the trend within each, and consider whether expense trend exceeds premium trend before selecting a 2016 provision. A 25.6% selection is below the most recent year and is likely to understate 2016 expenses.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know how each type of expense is typically incurred and what distortions the all variable expense method introduces, and to give three valid, genuinely distinct criticisms of the actuary's calculation.

Three answers were specifically not credited on their own. Arguing that the premium was not trended or brought to current rate level earned credit only when accompanied by an explanation tying it to fixed expenses, or noting that expenses would need trending as well — for a fully variable expense, on-leveling or trending the historical premium alone makes the provision worse, not better. Suggesting the use of countrywide data was rejected, since the question states the insurer writes in a single state. And identifying an improvement the actuary already made — such as dividing general expenses by earned premium — earned nothing.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Identify an alternative approach to calculate the expense provision and briefly explain its benefit relative to the actuary's approach without performing any additional calculations.

### Explanation
**Alternative: the exposure/policy-based projection method.** Split expenses into fixed and variable components. Divide the fixed portion by earned exposures to get a fixed expense **dollar amount per exposure**, trend it to the policy period, and load it into the rate as a flat per-exposure cost. Express only the genuinely variable portion — commissions/brokerage, taxes, licenses and fees — as a percentage of premium.

**Benefit:** the fixed expense provision no longer depends on the level of premium. Under the actuary's all variable approach, a fixed cost expressed as a percentage of premium is overstated for policies with below-average premium and understated for those with above-average premium, and the whole provision drifts as the average premium moves — a real problem for a book whose premium is falling year over year. Splitting the components also allows a separate **expense trend** to be applied to the fixed piece, which matters when expense inflation differs from premium trend, as the rising annual ratios here suggest.

Two other alternatives that would receive credit:

- **The premium-based projection method.** Same fixed/variable split, but the fixed provision is stated as a ratio to premium computed at *current rate level* and trended, which corrects for rate changes and premium trend embedded in the historical ratios while still separating fixed from variable.
- **The all variable expense method with corrected denominators.** Keep the fully variable treatment but match each expense category to the premium base by which it is actually incurred — general expense and other acquisition over earned premium, commissions/brokerage and taxes, licenses and fees over written premium — and select a ratio per category from the annual results rather than aggregating three years. This is a smaller improvement but it at least fixes the timing mismatch and exposes the year-to-year pattern.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to name a specific alternative expense-provision method and state a relevant advantage it holds over the actuary's calculation. Full credit required both an applicable improvement — the exposure/policy-based projection method, the premium-based projection method, or the all variable method with corrected denominators — and a brief explanation of its benefit.

Common errors included proposing an alternative that was not actually an improvement on the actuary's approach, and naming a valid method without describing why it is better.
