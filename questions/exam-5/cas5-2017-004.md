Given the following information for an insurance company:

| (\$000) | |
|---|---|
| Written Premium | 15,000 |
| Earned Premium | 12,000 |
| Ultimate Losses and LAE | 10,000 |
| Commissions and Brokerage | 2,250 |
| Other Acquisition Costs | 750 |
| Taxes, Licenses, and Fees | 300 |
| General Expenses | 360 |

- All expenses are variable.
- Underwriting profit provision = -5%.

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the following expense ratios to premium and briefly justify the selection of the premium basis used in each calculation:

i. Commissions and brokerage
ii. General expenses

### Answer
i. 15% of written premium; ii. 3% of earned premium

### Explanation
The premium base is chosen to match the pattern in which the expense is *incurred*.

**i. Commissions and brokerage — written premium.** Commission is paid to the producer at policy inception and is set as a percentage of the premium written, so the expense is incurred at the same moment, and in the same proportion, as premium is written. Relating it to written premium matches cost to the base that drives it:

$$\text{C\&B ratio} = \frac{\text{Commissions and brokerage}}{\text{Written premium}} = \frac{2{,}250}{15{,}000} = \mathbf{15\%}$$

**ii. General expenses — earned premium.** General expenses are the ongoing costs of operating the company and servicing policies in force. They are incurred steadily over the policy term rather than at inception, which is the same pattern in which premium is earned:

$$\text{General expense ratio} = \frac{\text{General expenses}}{\text{Earned premium}} = \frac{360}{12{,}000} = \mathbf{3\%}$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the two underwriting expense ratios, select the appropriate premium base for each, and explain why that base was appropriate. The justification had to give a clear reason the base fits the expense — merely asserting that commissions are "more closely associated with" written premium received no credit, because it does not explain why.

Common errors included selecting the wrong premium base for one or both ratios, and vague or incorrect justifications.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Calculate the permissible loss and LAE ratio.

### Answer
80%

### Explanation
The permissible loss and LAE ratio is what is left of each premium dollar once all expense and profit provisions are removed:

$$\text{PLR} = 1 - V - Q_T$$

First complete the variable expense provision $V$. The two remaining expense items are both acquisition-type costs incurred when the policy is written, so both go over written premium:

$$\text{Other acquisition} = \frac{750}{15{,}000} = 5\% \qquad \text{Taxes, licenses and fees} = \frac{300}{15{,}000} = 2\%$$

All expenses are stated to be variable, so they simply add:

$$V = \underbrace{15\%}_{\text{C\&B}} + \underbrace{5\%}_{\text{other acq.}} + \underbrace{2\%}_{\text{TLF}} + \underbrace{3\%}_{\text{general}} = 25\%$$

The underwriting profit provision is **negative**, $Q_T = -5\%$, so subtracting it *raises* the permissible loss ratio:

$$\text{PLR} = 1 - 0.25 - (-0.05) = \mathbf{0.80}$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify the remaining components of the total underwriting expense ratio, calculate them on the correct premium bases, and combine them with the profit provision into the permissible loss and LAE ratio.

Common errors included selecting the wrong premium bases for other acquisition and for taxes, licenses and fees; omitting components from the expense provision; leaving the profit and contingencies provision out of the calculation, or entering it as +5% instead of -5%; calculating the actual loss and LAE ratio rather than the permissible one; and treating some expenses as fixed when the question states all expenses are variable.

## Part c (0.25 points)

Briefly explain how the company may return a profit with an underwriting profit provision less than 0%.

### Explanation
A negative underwriting profit provision only means the company expects premium to fall short of losses and expenses; it does not mean the company expects to lose money overall. Premium is collected before losses are paid, and the funds held in the interim are invested. On a sufficiently long-tailed line the **investment income** earned on those loss reserves and on unearned premium can more than offset a planned underwriting loss, leaving a positive total return.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain how *total* profit could be positive when the business was priced with a negative underwriting profit provision — essentially, investment income on the float.

Common errors included explaining ways the company might improve its underwriting profit, and explaining reasons a company might choose a negative underwriting profit provision, neither of which answers how a profit is nonetheless returned.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Demonstrate whether or not the company met underwriting profit expectations.

### Answer
No — the actual loss and LAE ratio of 83.3% exceeds the permissible 80%.

### Explanation
Compare the company's actual loss and LAE ratio against the permissible ratio from part b, using **earned** premium so that losses and premium cover the same exposure period:

$$\text{Actual loss \& LAE ratio} = \frac{10{,}000}{12{,}000} = 83.3\%$$

$$83.3\% > 80\% = \text{PLR}$$

The company **did not** meet underwriting profit expectations.

The shortfall can be quantified several equivalent ways. Backing the realized underwriting profit out of the fundamental insurance equation:

$$0.833 = 1 - 0.25 - Q_T \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_T = -8.3\%$$

which is worse than the -5% planned, a shortfall of 3.3 points of premium. Equivalently, the premium needed to hit the target was $10{,}000 / 0.80 = \$12{,}500$ against \$12,000 actually earned; or, by combined ratio, $0.833 + 0.25 = 1.083$ against the 1.05 implied by a -5% provision.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to perform a calculation and then state and justify a conclusion from it about whether underwriting profit expectations were met.

Common errors included completing the calculation but never stating a conclusion; using dollar amounts in the fundamental insurance equation instead of ratios; using written premium as the base for the loss and LAE ratio; stating that expectations were met when the calculation showed otherwise; and concluding expectations were missed merely because the realized profit was below 0%, when the expectation itself was -5%.
