A working paper by Martin Eling that summarizes the knowledge on market discipline in insurance and other financial service sectors. It defines market discipline as the ability of customers, investors, intermediaries (agents, brokers) and evaluators (analysts, auditors, rating agencies) to monitor and influence a company's management, reviews the extensive banking literature and the smaller insurance literature, and develops a framework for researching market discipline in insurance that includes its most important drivers and impediments.

## 1 Introduction
- Explicit reliance on market discipline — the influence of customers, investors, intermediaries and evaluators on firm behavior — is a central element of both Basel II and [[Solvency II]].
- The paper finds significant differences between banking and insurance, and between lines of business and legal forms within insurance, so that market discipline might be weak in some areas (personal lines with complex products, mutuals) and strong in others (commercial lines, stocks); a "one-model-fits-all" approach might be inappropriate.
- It is not meant as an argument for any particular type of regulation, but as an outline of potential impediments regulators may face in enhancing market discipline.

## 2 Definition and measurement of market discipline
- 2.1 Definition of market discipline
    - The banking literature divides market discipline into monitoring (market participants' ability to assess a firm's condition) and influencing (their ability to affect management action accordingly).
    - In insurance, intermediaries, evaluators and regulators monitor insurers' financial strength; guarantee schemes and insurers' opaqueness could limit that monitoring, and the small risk of a run and the small size of personal-lines policyholders limit the influencing component.
    - Harrington (2004) and Nocera (2005) distinguish investor-driven from customer-driven market discipline; the paper defines market discipline in insurance as the ability of customers, investors, intermediaries and evaluators to monitor and influence the management of insurance companies.
- 2.2 Measurement of market discipline
    - Customers and investors monitor and influence directly; intermediaries and evaluators both directly and indirectly — customers and investors react to the signals evaluators send, such as rating changes (Tables 1 and 2).
- 2.3 Discussion of market discipline in the context of other regulatory measures
    - Market discipline is the third pillar of both Basel II and Solvency II, whose Pillar 3 is composed of public disclosure and reporting requirements.
    - Safety nets (deposit insurance, guarantee funds) can create moral hazard, price and product regulation decrease efficiency and limit innovation, and capital adequacy rules might be subject to adverse incentives.
    - Combining capital adequacy (Pillar 1) and market discipline (Pillar 3) imposes a substantial cost on insurers, especially small ones; market discipline cannot completely replace regulation.

## 3 Evidence for market discipline (including facilitators and impediments)
- 3.1 Evidence for market discipline in banking
    - A review of 62 banking studies, dating back to the 1980s (Table 3).
- 3.2 Evidence for market discipline in insurance
    - The insurance research, which rarely uses non-U.S. data, in three categories: investor-driven market discipline, customer-driven market discipline, and other aspects such as the impact of guarantee funds and opaqueness (Table 4).
- 3.3 Derivation of trends, consensus, and notable conflicts in the subject areas
    - Almost all studies focus on the monitoring component, which is easier to measure than influencing; market discipline appears reasonably strong in most insurance markets, with variation by legal form, line of business and country.
    - Informational limitations and the regulatory environment play a major role: regulation is likely more appropriate where informational limitations exist, market discipline more effective where much information is available; a bad market signal typically has a greater downside than a good one has upside.
- 3.4 Derivation of facilitators and impediments to market discipline in insurance
    - The most important impediments: guarantee associations, implicit bailout schemes such as "too big to fail", the complexity of financial products and institutions, and the judgment-proof problem under compulsory insurance.
    - Market discipline is expected to differ by line of business — stronger in commercial than in personal lines, and in reinsurance than in insurance — and by legal form, being more effective with stock insurers than with mutuals.
    - The availability and quality of information is crucial to facilitating market discipline, and standardization, consistency and accessibility are important requirements for it to be effective.
    - Market discipline works only if stakeholders consider themselves at risk and can observe risk efficiently; it then shows as a price effect or a quantity effect.

## 4 Conclusions and future research
- Evidence from banking shows market discipline can work very efficiently, but not all banking findings may generalize to insurance; the few insurance studies suggest its strength varies between the German and U.S. markets.
- Future research needs include empirical tests of the risk sensitivity of policyholder demand outside the United States, analysis of credit default swap spreads and stock prices, comparisons across countries and legal forms, and more theoretical models.
