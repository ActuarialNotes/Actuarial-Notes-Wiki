A paper on the Canadian **cap on non-pecuniary general damages** — the ceiling on what a plaintiff can recover for pain and suffering — and its effect on how bodily-injury claims are litigated. Read for [[Exam 6C (CAS)|Exam 6C]] objective A2.

## The subject

Canada, unlike the United States, caps non-pecuniary damages judicially rather than by statute. The cap originates in the Supreme Court of Canada's 1978 **"trilogy"** — *Andrews v. Grand & Toy Alberta Ltd.*, *Thornton v. Prince George School District* and *Arnold v. Teno* — which set a rough upper limit of \$100,000 for the worst conceivable personal injury, indexed to inflation since. The reasoning was functional: money cannot restore what was lost, so an award for pain and suffering should buy solace rather than attempt compensation, and unbounded awards would price liability insurance beyond reach.

The paper takes up what follows from that:

- **Where the cap is going** — indexation, and the pressure on the ceiling from cases the trilogy did not contemplate.
- **What the cap does not reach** — pecuniary heads of damage (future care cost, loss of earning capacity) are uncapped, so the cap constrains a smaller share of a catastrophic claim than of a modest one.
- **The effect on litigation** — with the ceiling known, disputes shift to whether the claim clears a [[Tort Threshold and Deductible|threshold]] or a [[Minor Injury Guideline|minor injury]] definition at all, and to the pecuniary heads; and the cap interacts with provincial caps on minor-injury awards rather than replacing them.
- **[[Punitive Damages|Punitive damages]]** sit outside the cap entirely, which is what made *Whiten v. Pilot Insurance Co.* (see [[Landmark Legal]]) so significant.
