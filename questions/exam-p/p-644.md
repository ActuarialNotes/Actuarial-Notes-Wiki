A salesperson ships six computers, but insures only four of them. Each computer independently has probability 0.1 of being damaged during shipping.

Calculate the probability that neither of the uninsured computers is damaged during shipping, given that exactly three of the computers are damaged during shipping.

- A) $0.0029$
- B) $0.0036$
- C) $0.0146$
- D) $0.2000$
- E) $0.8100$

## Explanation

Every computer has the same damage probability, so once you are told that exactly three were damaged, all $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ choices of *which* three are equally likely — the value 0.1 drops out entirely. Counting the ways to place all three damaged units among the four insured ones is hypergeometric:

$$
P[\text{Uninsured damaged} = 0 \mid \text{Damaged} = 3] = \frac{\dbinom{2}{0}\dbinom{4}{3}}{\dbinom{6}{3}} = \frac{1(4)}{20} = \mathbf{0.20}
$$

The same answer comes out of the binomial route. The probability that exactly three of the six are damaged is $\binom{6}{3}(0.1)^3(0.9)^3 = 0.01458$; the probability that exactly three are damaged *and* both uninsured units are undamaged is $(0.9)^2\binom{4}{3}(0.1)^3(0.9) = 0.002916$. The ratio is 0.20.

Choice (E), 0.8100, is $(0.9)^2$ — the unconditional probability that neither uninsured computer is damaged. Conditioning on three damages makes that far less likely, since three failures among six units have to be crowded into the four insured slots.
