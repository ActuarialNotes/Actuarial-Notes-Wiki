Given the following information about an insurance product filing with annual policies:

- 2018 projected pure premium = \$350.
- Loss cost annual trend = 3%.
- Premium annual trend = 4%.
- Fixed expense per exposure, new business = \$50.
- Fixed expense per exposure, renewals = \$6.
- Variable expense ratio = 18%.
- Profit and contingencies provision = 6%.
- LAE provision = 10% of loss.
- Retention ratio = 80%.
- Discount rate = 5%.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Calculate the new business premium per exposure for 2018 indicated by a lifetime value analysis using a two-year time horizon.

### Answer
\$544.49

### Explanation
A lifetime value analysis sets the premium so that the policy breaks even — after the profit provision — over the whole expected life of the customer, not over the first year alone. Here that means the first year plus a second year weighted by the probability the customer renews and discounted back one year.

Let $P$ be the 2018 new business premium per exposure.

**Costs.** The LAE provision loads the pure premium; loss costs trend at 3% into the second year; the fixed expense drops from the new business \$50 to the renewal \$6:

| | Loss + LAE | Fixed expense | Total cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (2018) | $350 \times 1.10 = 385.00$ | 50 | **435.00** |
| Year 2 (2019) | $350 \times 1.03 \times 1.10 = 396.55$ | 6 | **402.55** |

**Premium and the loads.** The second year's premium carries the 4% premium trend, and variable expense and profit are percentages of premium in both years, so each year retains $1 - V - Q = 1 - 0.18 - 0.06 = 0.76$ of its premium to meet costs:

$$\text{Year 1 net} = 0.76P - 435.00$$

$$\text{Year 2 net} = 0.76(1.04P) - 402.55 = 0.7904P - 402.55$$

**Weighting the second year.** The customer renews with probability 80%, and the second year's cash flows are discounted one year at 5%:

$$w = \frac{0.80}{1.05} = 0.761905$$

Note the weight applies to the second year's *entire* net position — premium, losses and fixed expenses alike — not to the losses alone.

**Solve for break-even over the two years:**

$$(0.76P - 435.00) + 0.761905\,(0.7904P - 402.55) = 0$$

$$1.362210\,P = 741.70 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad P = \$544.49$$

**Cross-check** by discounting costs and premium separately. The present value of costs is $435.00 + 0.761905(402.55) = 741.70$; grossing that up for variable expense and profit gives $741.70 / 0.76 = 975.93$; and the present value of premium is $P(1 + 1.04 \times 0.761905) = 1.792381P$. Then $P = 975.93 / 1.792381 = \$544.49$.

The premium sits far above the first year's own break-even because the \$50 new business acquisition cost is recovered across both years — the renewal year, with only \$6 of fixed expense and a trended premium, subsidizes the first.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to apply the LAE factor to the loss cost, set up the required premium formula, and apply the loss trend, retention ratio, discount factor and premium trend correctly to the second year's premium and its components.

Common errors included not loading LAE into the loss cost; failing to apply the retention ratio or the discount to the second year's present value premium; failing to apply the retention ratio or discount to the fixed expenses; including the premium trend in the second year present value premium calculation where it did not belong; and omitting the premium trend when calculating the first year premium.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Fully justify the use of lifetime value analysis in a rate indication using the *Statement of Principles Regarding Property and Casualty Ratemaking*.

### Explanation
Each of the four CAS ratemaking principles supports the approach:

**Principle 1 — "A rate is an estimate of the expected value of future costs."** Lifetime value analysis is explicitly forward-looking: it projects loss costs, expenses and retention over the whole expected life of the policy rather than a single term, so it estimates future costs over the period the rate will actually be earning.

**Principle 2 — "A rate provides for all costs associated with the transfer of risk."** The method captures costs a single-year indication would misallocate — in particular the large one-time acquisition cost of new business (\$50 against \$6 on renewal). By spreading that cost across the expected life of the customer, the rate provides for all the costs of the transfer rather than loading them entirely onto year one.

**Principle 3 — "A rate provides for the costs associated with an individual risk transfer."** The analysis is performed at the level of an individual policy's expected lifetime, using that policy's own retention and cost characteristics, so the rate reflects the cost of that individual risk transfer.

**Principle 4 — "A rate is reasonable and not excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory if it is an actuarially sound estimate of the expected value of all future costs associated with an individual risk transfer."** Lifetime value analysis is an actuarially sound estimate built on explicit, supportable assumptions — retention, discount rate, trend — applied consistently to all insureds. Because the differences in price reflect genuine expected cost differences over the customer's life, the resulting rates are not unfairly discriminatory.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the ratemaking principles and to connect this real-world example to them. Common errors were knowing the principles but failing to link them to the example, and describing what lifetime value analysis does for ratemaking without tying it back to the principles.
