An auditor is examining insurance policies for fraud. A policyholder can only file one claim. The probability of any given policy having a claim is 0.90, and the probability of a claim being fraudulent is 0.20. The auditor picks five policies at random and examines them in order until he finds two fraudulent claims. He then stops examining policies. If he doesn't find two fraudulent claims, he stops after examining the fifth policy.

Calculate the expected number of policies he will examine.

- A) $4.68$
- B) $4.73$
- C) $4.78$
- D) $4.83$
- E) $4.88$

## Explanation

A policy yields a fraudulent claim only if it has a claim *and* that claim is fraudulent:

$$
p = (0.9)(0.2) = 0.18
$$

Let $X$ be the number of policies examined. Stopping at the second fraud gives a negative binomial pattern: for the run to end at policy $x$, the $x$th policy must be fraudulent and exactly one of the previous $x-1$ must have been.

$$
\begin{align*}
p(2) &= (0.18)^2 = 0.0324 \\
p(3) &= 2(0.82)(0.18)^2 = 0.0531 \\
p(4) &= 3(0.82)^2(0.18)^2 = 0.0654
\end{align*}
$$

The truncation is the twist: the auditor stops at five policies no matter what, so $X = 5$ covers *both* finding the second fraud on the fifth policy and never finding it at all. Rather than enumerating those cases, take the complement:

$$
\begin{align*}
p(5) &= 1 - 0.0324 - 0.0531 - 0.0654 \\
     &= 0.8491
\end{align*}
$$

Now the expectation:

$$
\begin{align*}
E[X] &= 2(0.0324) + 3(0.0531) + 4(0.0654) + 5(0.8491) \\
     &= \mathbf{4.7312}
\end{align*}
$$

Because fraud is rare, $X = 5$ carries 85% of the probability and the answer sits just below 5. An untruncated negative binomial would have mean $2/0.18 = 11.1$ — far outside the answer range, which is a quick signal that the cap must be modelled.
