Given the following information for an insurance company as of December 31, 2016:

| Accident Year | Incremental Closed Claim Counts as of 48 months | 60 | 72 | 84 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2010 | 60 | 25 | 15 | 5 |
| 2011 | 60 | 30 | 15 | |
| 2012 | 100 | 25 | | |
| 2013 | 80 | | | |

| Accident Year | Incremental Paid Claims (\$000) as of 48 months | 60 | 72 | 84 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2010 | 1,400 | 2,500 | 2,000 | 400 |
| 2011 | 1,600 | 1,100 | 600 | |
| 2012 | 2,800 | 1,900 | | |
| 2013 | 2,100 | | | |

- Selected annual severity trend = 5%.
- Trended tail severity at 72 months at the accident year 2016 cost level = \$114,000.
- Trended tail severity at 84 months at the accident year 2016 cost level = \$107,000.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Calculate the trended tail severities at maturity ages 48 months and 60 months at the accident year 2016 cost level.

### Answer
48 months: \$50,116; 60 months: \$96,049

### Explanation
In the disposal-rate frequency-severity method, a **tail severity at age $m$** is the average cost of the claims that close at age $m$ *and later*. It is built by pooling every cell of the incremental triangle at maturity $m$ or beyond:

$$\text{Tail severity at } m = \frac{\sum_{\text{cells with maturity} \ge m} \text{incremental paid} \times (1 + t)^{2016 - \text{AY}}}{\sum_{\text{cells with maturity} \ge m} \text{incremental closed counts}}$$

Two things to keep straight. First, the trend factor is indexed by **accident year**, not maturity — an accident year's whole row is brought to the 2016 cost level by the same factor, so trend runs across maturities within a row rather than down a column. Second, the counts in the denominator are *not* trended.

Trend factors to the accident year 2016 cost level:

| AY | Years of trend | Factor $1.05^{n}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 2010 | 6 | 1.3401 |
| 2011 | 5 | 1.2763 |
| 2012 | 4 | 1.2155 |
| 2013 | 3 | 1.1576 |

**Tail severity at 48 months.** Every cell in both triangles qualifies.

$$\text{Numerator} = (1{,}400 + 2{,}500 + 2{,}000 + 400)(1.05)^6 + (1{,}600 + 1{,}100 + 600)(1.05)^5$$
$$+ (2{,}800 + 1{,}900)(1.05)^4 + (2{,}100)(1.05)^3 = 20{,}798.2 \text{ (\$000)}$$

$$\text{Denominator} = (60 + 25 + 15 + 5) + (60 + 30 + 15) + (100 + 25) + 80 = 415$$

$$\text{Tail severity at 48} = \frac{20{,}798.2 \times 1{,}000}{415} = \$50{,}116$$

**Tail severity at 60 months.** Drop the 48-month column.

$$\text{Numerator} = (2{,}500 + 2{,}000 + 400)(1.05)^6 + (1{,}100 + 600)(1.05)^5 + (1{,}900)(1.05)^4 = 11{,}045.6$$

$$\text{Denominator} = (25 + 15 + 5) + (30 + 15) + 25 = 115$$

$$\text{Tail severity at 60} = \frac{11{,}045.6 \times 1{,}000}{115} = \$96{,}049$$

*Alternatively*, the same numbers can be built recursively from the given 72-month tail severity, since the tail at age $m$ is a count-weighted blend of the severity of the cells closing at $m$ and the tail at the next age:

$$\text{Tail}_{60} = \frac{2{,}500(1.05)^6 + 1{,}100(1.05)^5 + 1{,}900(1.05)^4 + 114 \times (15 + 15 + 5)}{25 + 30 + 25 + (15 + 15 + 5)} \times 1{,}000 = \$96{,}118$$

$$\text{Tail}_{48} = \frac{1{,}400(1.05)^6 + 1{,}600(1.05)^5 + 2{,}800(1.05)^4 + 2{,}100(1.05)^3 + 96.118 \times 115}{60 + 60 + 100 + 80 + 115} \times 1{,}000 = \$50{,}135$$

The small differences from the direct calculation are rounding in the given \$114,000 — as a check, the direct method reproduces it: $(2{,}400)(1.05)^6 + (600)(1.05)^5 = 3{,}982.0$ over $20 + 15 = 35$ counts is \$113,772.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the incremental tail severities at 48 and 60 months by pooling all incremental paid claims and closed counts at that maturity and beyond, after trending the paid claims to the accident year 2016 cost level.

Common errors included calculating a simple or claim-weighted average severity using only the data at age 48 and 60; computing the 48-month tail from data at 60+ and the 60-month tail from data at 72+, i.e. shifting the pool by one column; incorporating both the given 72- and 84-month tail severities into the same calculation; and trending mistakes — trending to 2013 rather than 2016, or applying trend across accident years at a fixed maturity instead of across all maturities within an accident year.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Discuss at which maturity age the data should be combined for the purpose of selecting an incremental tail severity to be used in a frequency-severity method for this insurance company.

### Answer
Combine at 60 months

### Explanation
The decision is a trade-off: combining early throws away usable development detail, while combining late leaves the selection resting on a handful of claims. The way to make it for *this* company is to look at its own trended incremental severities by cell and find the maturity at which they stop being stable.

$$\text{Trended incremental severity} = \frac{\text{incremental paid} \times (1.05)^{2016 - \text{AY}}}{\text{incremental closed counts}}$$

| AY | 48 | 60 | 72 | 84 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2010 | \$31,269 | \$134,010 | \$178,679 | \$107,208 |
| 2011 | \$34,034 | \$46,797 | \$51,051 | |
| 2012 | \$34,034 | \$92,378 | | |
| 2013 | \$30,388 | | | |

At **48 months** the four accident years give \$30,400 to \$34,000 — tight, and supported by 300 of the 415 closed claims in the triangle. That column is credible and predictive, so combining at 48 would discard a full period of genuine development information for no gain.

At **60 months and beyond** the picture breaks down: \$134,010, \$46,797 and \$92,378 across three accident years at the same maturity, on 25 to 30 claims each, and the 84-month severity (\$107,208) is *lower* than the 72-month severity (\$178,679), which incremental severities should not do in a maturing tail. That volatility is small-sample noise, not signal.

So combine at **60 months**: keep the actual 48-month severity as its own step in the method, and pool everything from 60 on into a single tail severity, which raises the claim volume behind the selection to 115 and smooths the erratic later cells. (Combining at 72 is also defensible on the grounds that 60-month volume is still meaningful and the erratic behavior is worst at 72 and 84; combining at 48 or at 84 is not.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to select an appropriate age at which to combine the data and to justify it using this company's own data, comparing the age before their selection with the age after it.

Common errors included giving general considerations for selecting a tail without making an actual selection for this company or referencing any company-specific figures; selecting 48 or 84 months, neither of which is appropriate; and failing to notice the stability of the severities at age 48.
