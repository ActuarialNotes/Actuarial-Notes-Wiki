![[Media/Figures/Fault_Determination_Rules.svg|340]]

The **Fault Determination Rules** are a regulation prescribing, by accident scenario, the percentage of fault assigned to each driver in an automobile collision. They apply to first-party coverages, [[Direct Compensation Property Damage|DCPD]] and collision, and are applied mechanically from the physical facts, without regard to weather, road conditions, driver skill or evidence about who was actually careless.

Ontario's Fault Determination Rules (R.R.O. 1990, Reg. 668) set out numbered scenarios, each with a fixed apportionment:

1. Rear-end collisions.
2. Left turns across traffic.
3. Lane changes.
4. Parking lots.
5. Uncontrolled intersections.

Fault is assigned in increments of $0$, $25$, $50$, $75$ and $100$ per cent, and the usual apportionments are $0/100$, $50/50$ and $25/75$.

Mechanical application is the point of the rules. In a high-volume, low-severity coverage, certainty and speed are worth more than accuracy. Two adjusters looking at the same collision reach the same answer without negotiation, which is what makes DCPD's low expense ratio achievable.

> [!example]- Under the rules, how is fault apportioned when B rear-ends A at a red light, claiming A stopped abruptly and unnecessarily? {Example}
> - The rear-end scenario assigns full fault to the following driver, so B is $100\%$ at fault.
> - The rules direct that fault be determined "without reference to the circumstances in which the incident occurs" beyond the scenario itself, so B's explanation is not a defence.
> - B's insurer pays B's collision claim, subject to the deductible, and B is recorded at fault.
> - A's insurer pays A's damage under [[Direct Compensation Property Damage|DCPD]], and A is not at fault for rating.

## Individual justice and efficiency

The rules can be wrong in the individual case. A driver who rear-ends another because that driver braked maliciously is still $100\%$ at fault under the rules. The regulation trades individual justice for administrative efficiency.

## Fault in a tort action

The rules govern first-party recovery and rating. They do not bind a court in a [[Tort Litigation|tort]] action for bodily injury, where common-law negligence and contributory negligence apply. The same accident can therefore be $100/0$ for property damage and, say, $80/20$ for bodily injury, if B proves in court that A braked without cause.

## Rating consequences

An at-fault determination under the rules typically triggers a surcharge and the loss of a claims-free discount, so the rules directly affect the driver's premium. That is why a determination that a driver considers unjust reaches the regulator as a complaint.

> [!trap] Applying the rules to bodily injury
> The rules settle fault for DCPD, collision and rating only. A tort claim for bodily injury on the same facts is decided by a court on negligence, and can apportion fault differently.
