An actuary is estimating unpaid claims as of December 31, 2025 for a private passenger automobile bodily injury liability book. Over the past two years, court decisions in the jurisdiction have expanded the coverage the policies are interpreted to provide, and the statute of limitations for filing bodily injury claims has been lengthened. Claims management reports no change in case reserving practice or in the speed of settlement.

Which approach for the most recent accident year is most appropriate?

- A) Give weight to the expected claims technique, with an expected claim ratio reflecting the new legal environment
- B) Rely on the reported development technique, since case reserving practice and settlement speed are unchanged
- C) Rely on the paid development technique, since court decisions do not affect payments until claims settle
- D) Adjust case outstanding for a change in adequacy, then rely on the reported development technique
- E) Rely on the development technique unadjusted, since the trend in the historical data already reflects legal change

## Explanation

The development technique assumes that historical claim development is predictive of future development. Friedland (Chapter 7) notes that **environmental** changes, not only changes inside the insurer, can invalidate that assumption: after a major change in the legal environment the actuary may no longer be able to assume that historical development will predict future development, and should consider alternative techniques or at least adjust the selected development factors.

Friedland (Chapter 8) names exactly this situation as a use of the expected claims technique: an insurer may rely on an expected claim ratio for the latest year after major changes in the legal environment, citing an increase in the statute of limitations and expanded coverage due to court decisions. The condition is that the actuary can estimate a reliable expected claim ratio that reflects the changed environment. The answer is **A**.

**Why not the others**

- **B** — unchanged case reserving and settlement speed satisfy the development technique's *internal* assumptions only. A longer filing window and broader coverage change how many claims arrive and what they cost, so historical reported development understates what is to come.
- **C** — the paid technique shares the same premise that history repeats. Expanded coverage and late-filed claims change ultimate payments as well; waiting for settlement does not make the paid pattern stable.
- **D** — a case adequacy adjustment (such as Berquist-Sherman's) addresses a shift in case reserve strength, which the stem rules out. It does nothing for a change in the legal environment.
- **E** — an annual trend restates claim *costs* from year to year; it does not restate a development pattern changed by a longer reporting window or by coverage that did not exist in the historical years.
