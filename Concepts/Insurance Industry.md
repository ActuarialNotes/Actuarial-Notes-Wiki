The **Insurance Industry** is the set of firms that accept the transfer of risk from households and businesses for a premium and pay claims when insured events occur. It includes [[Insurer|insurers]] and [[Reinsurance|reinsurers]], and the brokers, agents and adjusters around them.

An insurer's underwriting result is its [[Earned Premium|earned premium]] less its losses and loss adjustment expenses (LAE) and its other expenses:

> $$\text{Underwriting result} = \text{Earned premium} - \text{Losses and LAE} - \text{Expenses}$$

Adding [[Investment Income|investment income]] to the underwriting result gives operating income, which is

> $$\text{Operating income} = \text{Underwriting result} + \text{Investment income}$$

## Insurance as a vital service

Property and casualty insurance is a vital service. It finances recovery from loss, makes lending, trade and ownership possible, and supplies coverage the law makes compulsory:

1. A mortgage requires property insurance.
2. A car loan requires physical damage cover.
3. A contractor cannot bid without liability insurance and bonds.
4. Auto liability and workers' compensation are compulsory.

Because an insurer's failure harms third parties who never chose the insurer, the public interest justifies [[Insurance Regulation|regulation]] of solvency, rates and conduct, with [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty funds]] behind it. This is why the business is regulated far more heavily than most.

## The inverted production cycle

An insurer sets its price before its cost is known, and pays claims long after it collects the premium. The gap creates investment income from the float and the [[Underwriting Cycle|underwriting cycle]]. It also makes the insurer's reported profit depend on reserve estimates, which are the actuary's contribution.

## Structure of the industry

Insurers are organised as stock companies, mutuals and reciprocals. They distribute through brokers and independent agents or through direct writers, and reinsurers stand behind them. In Canada, insurers are federally or provincially incorporated or operate as branches of foreign insurers. In the U.S., admitted insurers are licensed state by state, and [[Excess and Surplus Lines|surplus lines]] carriers and [[Risk Retention Groups|risk retention groups]] operate outside that system.

## Exposure to court decisions

A [[Court Case|court decision]] can change the cost of policies already written. In Canada the syllabus decisions on the [[Duty to Defend|duty to defend]], [[Bad Faith Damages|bad faith damages]], [[Punitive Damages|punitive damages]], [[Vicarious Liability|vicarious liability]], [[Limitation Period|limitation periods]], [[Prejudgment Interest|prejudgment interest]] and [[Collateral Benefits|collateral benefits]] each reprice exposure retroactively.

> [!example]- What does this court decision cost this insurer, and what must it do? {Example}
> An appellate court rules that a common water-damage exclusion in homeowners' wordings is ambiguous and must be read in favour of the insured. Over the past two years insurers denied about $12{,}000$ claims under that exclusion, averaging $\$18{,}000$ each. The insurer holds $8\%$ of the market.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The industry exposure is $12{,}000 \times \$18{,}000 = \$216\text{M}$.
> > - The insurer's share is $0.08 \times \$216\text{M} = \$17.28\text{M}$, an upper bound that assumes every denied claim is reopened.
> > - The decision states the law as it always was, so reserves for past accident years must be re-estimated, and for a valuation not yet signed it is a [[Subsequent Events|subsequent event]] to recognise, not merely disclose.
> > - Current rates are inadequate for the in-force book, and every insurer will redraft the wording.
> > - The industry absorbs retroactively a cost it never priced.

## Latent liability in the U.S.

[[Latent Liability|Latent liability]] is injury that manifests decades after exposure, as with [[Asbestos|asbestos]] and other [[Mass Tort|mass torts]]. It reaches occurrence policies written long before the hazard was understood. Courts' trigger and allocation rules can bring many policy years, and their limits, into play. Coverage litigation adds cost, and development triangles have no history to project. The resulting reserve strengthening has impaired or bankrupted insurers.

> [!example]- How much does this insurer pay under a manifestation trigger, and under a continuous trigger allocated pro rata by time on the risk? {Example}
> A manufacturer bought annual occurrence general liability policies from one insurer from 1972 through 1985, each with a $\$1$ million per-occurrence limit. A claimant exposed in 1972 is diagnosed with an asbestos disease in 2010 and settles for $\$7$ million.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Under a manifestation trigger, the injury is deemed to occur on diagnosis in 2010, when no policy was in force, so the insurer pays nothing.
> > - Under a continuous trigger, running from exposure through manifestation, every policy from 1972 through 1985 is triggered, which is $14$ policy years.
> > - The available limit is $14 \times \$1\text{M} = \$14\text{M}$.
> > - The pro rata share is $\$7\text{M} / 14 = \$0.5\text{M}$ per year, within each year's limit.
> > - The insurer pays the full $\$7$ million out of policies priced in the 1970s for a risk nobody then associated with the product.

> [!trap] Treating a court decision as affecting only future policies
> A decision that reinterprets a wording changes the cost of claims under policies already written. The reserves for past accident years must be re-estimated as well as the rates for new business.
