Given the following information:

| Claim ID | Accident Date | 2013 CY Payments | 2013 Ending Case O/S | 2014 CY Payments | 2014 Ending Case O/S | 2015 CY Payments | 2015 Ending Case O/S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | January 1, 2013 | 80 | 150 | 25 | 100 | 100 | - |
| 2 | June 1, 2013 | 20 | 50 | 25 | 50 | 100 | - |
| 3 | May 1, 2014 | | | 100 | 50 | 50 | 50 |
| 4 | December 15, 2014 | | | 50 | 250 | 150 | 100 |
| 5 | April 1, 2015 | | | | | 50 | 50 |

## Part a (0.75 points)

Construct an accident year cumulative paid claims triangle.

### Answer
AY 2013: 100, 150, 350; AY 2014: 150, 350; AY 2015: 50

### Explanation
Group the claims by accident year — claims 1 and 2 are accident year 2013, claims 3 and 4 are 2014, claim 5 is 2015 — then accumulate calendar year payments across the diagonals. Age 12 for accident year 2013 is the end of calendar year 2013, age 24 the end of 2014, and so on.

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 100 | 150 | 350 |
| 2014 | 150 | 350 | |
| 2015 | 50 | | |

- **AY 2013 @ 12** $= 80 + 20 = 100$; **@ 24** $= 100 + (25 + 25) = 150$; **@ 36** $= 150 + (100 + 100) = 350$.
- **AY 2014 @ 12** $= 100 + 50 = 150$; **@ 24** $= 150 + (50 + 150) = 350$.
- **AY 2015 @ 12** $= 50$.

The figures are *cumulative paid*, so each cell adds that calendar year's payments to the prior cell — the case reserve column plays no part here.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to build an accident year paid claim triangle from transactional claim data, grouping each claim by its accident date and accumulating payments along the calendar year diagonals.

A common mistake was accumulating case reserves instead of payments.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Construct an accident year cumulative reported claims triangle.

### Answer
AY 2013: 300, 300, 350; AY 2014: 450, 500; AY 2015: 100

### Explanation
Reported claims are cumulative paid plus the case reserve standing at the valuation date:

$$\text{Reported} = \text{Cumulative paid} + \text{Ending case outstanding}$$

The case figure is a *balance*, not an increment, so each cell takes that evaluation's ending case for every claim in the accident year — not a running sum of the case column.

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 300 | 300 | 350 |
| 2014 | 450 | 500 | |
| 2015 | 100 | | |

- **AY 2013 @ 12** $= 100 + (150 + 50) = 300$; **@ 24** $= 150 + (100 + 50) = 300$; **@ 36** $= 350 + 0 = 350$ (both claims closed).
- **AY 2014 @ 12** $= 150 + (50 + 250) = 450$; **@ 24** $= 350 + (50 + 100) = 500$.
- **AY 2015 @ 12** $= 50 + 50 = 100$.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to build an accident year reported claim triangle from the same transactional data, adding each evaluation's outstanding case reserves to cumulative payments.

A common mistake was omitting some of the claims at a given evaluation date.

## Part c (0.5 points)

In 2015 the claims department began paying claims faster without changing the adequacy of case reserves. Produce and briefly discuss a triangle that demonstrates that this action has been successfully executed.

### Explanation
The diagnostic is the **ratio of paid to reported claims**, cell by cell from parts a and b:

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 0.333 | 0.500 | 1.000 |
| 2014 | 0.333 | 0.700 | |
| 2015 | 0.500 | | |

Read down each *column*, which holds maturity constant so that only the calendar year changes:

- **At 12 months**, the ratio holds at 0.333 for accident years 2013 and 2014, then jumps to **0.500** for accident year 2015.
- **At 24 months**, the ratio rises from 0.500 for accident year 2013 to **0.700** for accident year 2014.

Both increases sit on the **latest diagonal**, which is calendar year 2015 — exactly where the claims department changed its practice. A higher paid-to-reported ratio at the same maturity means a larger share of each reported dollar has already been paid out.

Only two things can raise that ratio: claims are being settled faster, or case reserves are being set lower relative to their eventual value. The question states that case adequacy is unchanged, which rules out the second, so the movement confirms faster payment.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to create a paid-to-reported diagnostic triangle and to comment on how the increased ratio along the latest diagonal demonstrates faster payment. Full credit was also given for a reported-to-paid triangle with a comment on its decreasing latest diagonal.

Common mistakes included producing some other diagnostic — paid-to-case, average paid severity, or average case outstanding; using paid loss development factors as the diagnostic; and building the correct triangle but never explaining that the rise in the latest diagonal is what evidences the faster payments.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Recommend and briefly justify a technique for calculating ultimate claims for this dataset, given the change noted in part c. above.

### Explanation
**Use the reported claim development technique.**

The paid development technique extrapolates the historical payment pattern forward, and that pattern has just changed: paid loss development factors selected from accident years 2013 and 2014 reflect the old, slower settlement rate, so applying them to the accelerated 2015 payments would overstate the remaining development and therefore overstate ultimate claims.

The reported technique is the right substitute for two reasons, and **both** must be stated:

1. Reported claims are paid plus case, so shifting a dollar from case reserve to payment leaves the reported total unchanged — the technique is simply **not affected by a change in settlement rate**.
2. Reported development does depend on case reserve adequacy, and the question tells us **case adequacy has not changed**, so that assumption still holds.

Any technique built on reported claims is equally defensible on the same reasoning — the expected claims method, the reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson method, or the Cape Cod method.

Alternatively, the **paid Berquist-Sherman technique** may be used: it restates the historical paid triangle onto the *current* settlement rate before development factors are selected, so the adjusted paid data and the new payment pattern are consistent.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know how to adjust the data or the estimation technique when the claims handling process changes, to state a specific technique appropriate to the new process, and to give sufficient reasoning why it produces an accurate ultimate.

Full credit went to any technique relying on reported claims — reported development, expected claims, reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson or Cape Cod — provided the candidate explained that it is unaffected by the change in settlement rates, and to the paid Berquist-Sherman technique provided the candidate said the historical paid triangle must be restated for the faster payment pattern.

Common mistakes included insufficient reasoning for the technique chosen; naming an inappropriate technique; selecting paid Berquist-Sherman without mentioning the restatement of the paid triangle; and selecting a reported technique but justifying it only by unchanged case adequacy, without also stating that reported claims are unaffected by settlement rate changes.
