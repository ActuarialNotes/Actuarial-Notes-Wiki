Given the following information for accident year 2015:

**Accident Year 2015** — a bar chart of ultimate claims (\$ millions) for accident year 2015, estimated by four techniques (Reported Development, Reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson, Reported Cape Cod, and Frequency/Severity), with each group of bars showing the estimate as of a successive year-end evaluation.

Reading the chart approximately:

| AY2015 evaluated as of | Reported Development | Reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson | Reported Cape Cod | Frequency / Severity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2015 | ~138 | ~165 | ~96 | ~190 |
| 2016 | ~141 | ~171 | ~130 | ~191 |
| 2017 | ~166 | ~172 | ~161 | ~184 |
| 2018 | ~177 | ~178 | ~178 | ~180 |

- Tort reform enacted January 1, 2015, resulted in a drastic increase in severity.

Discuss the year-over-year progression of each technique in response to the law change.

### Explanation
All four methods converge on roughly \$178–180 million by 2018. What separates them is **how quickly each recognizes the severity increase**, which follows directly from how much each relies on pre-reform history.

**Reported Development method — starts low, rises steadily.** This method applies age-to-age factors derived entirely from historical development to the reported claims. Those factors come from accident years before the tort reform, when severity was lower, so they are too low for a post-reform year: the 2015 estimate of ~\$138M badly understates the ultimate. Nothing in the method adjusts for the law change, so the higher severity is recognized only as it emerges in the reported claims themselves and works its way into the factors. The estimate therefore climbs each year — ~138, ~141, ~166, ~177 — with the sharpest jump between 2016 and 2017 as post-reform experience finally dominates the data, levelling out only once the year is nearly fully developed.

**Reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson — starts high and moves very little.** BF splits the estimate into actual reported claims plus expected unreported claims, the latter being an *a priori* expected claims ratio applied to the unreported percentage. Because the a priori is selected judgmentally, the actuary could and did build the anticipated severity increase into it immediately, so the very first estimate (~\$165M) already reflects the reform. As the year matures, weight shifts from the a priori to actual reported claims, which were themselves still catching up to the new severity level — hence the modest, steady rise to ~\$178M. BF sits between the development method (which under-projects) and frequency/severity (which reflects the change fully at once), exactly as a credibility weighting of a development estimate and an expected estimate should.

**Reported Cape Cod — starts lowest and moves the most.** Cape Cod resembles BF but derives its expected claims ratio *from the experience itself* rather than from judgment. That expected claims ratio is computed from historical, largely pre-reform years, so it is far too low — producing the lowest initial estimate of all four methods (~\$96M). It is therefore the **least responsive** to the law change: with both the reported claims and the estimated claims ratio understating post-reform severity, the method is doubly anchored to the old level. It corrects only as higher-severity experience enters both components, giving the largest year-over-year movements — roughly \$20M and \$30M jumps — before converging.

**Frequency/Severity — accurate immediately, essentially flat.** This technique estimates claim counts and severity separately, so the actuary can adjust the **severity** assumption directly for the tort reform without waiting for data to emerge. That is exactly the kind of change the method is built to absorb, and it produced an estimate close to the final answer from the very first evaluation (~\$190M against an eventual ~\$180M). It is the most responsive of the four, slightly overestimating at first and drifting down as actual experience refines the severity selection.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand both the mechanics and the assumptions of the four techniques, and the effect of the tort reform on each estimate over time.

Common mistakes included stating the direction of change in ultimate claims for a method without explaining why; asserting that a method was or was not responsive to the tort reform without further explanation; claiming the expected claims ratio in the BF method was unaffected by the reform without explanation; confusing the a priori expected claims ratio used in the BF method with the one derived in the Cape Cod method; stating that the reported development technique does not take the tort change into account without explaining why; and defining each method without ever addressing the impact of the tort reform.
