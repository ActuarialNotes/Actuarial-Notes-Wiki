## Part a (0.5 point)

Evaluate whether there has been a change in the adequacy of case outstanding over the experience period.

### Answer

Yes — case outstanding adequacy has been strengthened.

### Explanation

Compute average case outstanding per open claim (\$000):

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 140 | 110 | 130 | 120 |
| 2015 | 135 | 150 | 144 | |
| 2016 | 180 | 152 | | |
| 2017 | 180 | | | |

Reading down each column, the average case outstanding rises much faster than the 7.5% selected severity trend would explain. At 12 months it goes 140 → 135 → 180 → 180 (33% in one year from 2015 to 2016); at 24 months 110 → 150 → 152 (36% from 2014 to 2015); at 36 months 130 → 144 (10.8%).

Since the increases along the columns exceed the underlying severity trend, the difference is attributable to a **strengthening of case reserve adequacy** over the experience period, not to claim cost inflation alone.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to calculate the average case outstanding triangle and evaluate it to identify that the adequacy of case outstanding has increased over time.

Common mistakes included concluding that case reserve adequacy was decreasing, and examining only a single evaluation (for example the 12-month column alone) — a change at one evaluation is not sufficient evidence of a change in case reserve adequacy.

## Part b (1.25 points)

Estimate the ultimate claims for accident year 2017 using the Berquist-Sherman adjustment.

### Answer

Approximately \$344,700 thousand

### Explanation

**Step 1 — Restate the average case outstanding.** Take the latest diagonal as the current adequacy level and detrend it backwards at 7.5% per year (\$000):

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | $180/1.075^3 = 144.9$ | $152/1.075^2 = 131.5$ | $144/1.075 = 134.0$ | 120 |
| 2015 | $180/1.075^2 = 155.8$ | $152/1.075 = 141.4$ | 144 | |
| 2016 | $180/1.075 = 167.4$ | 152 | | |
| 2017 | 180 | | | |

**Step 2 — Adjusted case outstanding** = adjusted average × open claim counts, and **adjusted reported** = paid + adjusted case (\$000):

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 62,961 | 82,762 | 165,767 | 285,600 |
| 2015 | 62,758 | 79,800 | 161,000 | |
| 2016 | 66,443 | 88,400 | | |
| 2017 | 72,600 | | | |

**Step 3 — Development factors from the adjusted triangle:**

| | 12–24 | 24–36 | 36–48 | 48–ult |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 1.314 | 2.003 | 1.723 | |
| 2015 | 1.272 | 2.018 | | |
| 2016 | 1.330 | | | |
| Selected | 1.305 | 2.010 | 1.723 | 1.050 |

$$\text{CDF}_{12-\text{ult}} = 1.305 \times 2.010 \times 1.723 \times 1.05 = 4.748$$

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2017} = 72{,}600 \times 4.748 = 344{,}700$$

Weighted-average factor selections also received full credit.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to apply the Berquist-Sherman case outstanding adjustment to the given data and use the adjusted data to calculate the ultimate loss for accident year 2017.

Common mistakes included restating only the 2016-and-prior diagonals off the 2016 diagonal while leaving 2017 unrestated, failing to apply the given 48-to-ultimate tail factor, and applying the trend incorrectly (multiplying rather than dividing, or using 7% instead of 7.5%).

## Part c (0.25 point)

Briefly explain the effect of the Berquist-Sherman adjustment in part b. above when compared to the result using unadjusted data.

### Explanation

Because case outstanding adequacy has been strengthened, the unadjusted triangle mixes historical development factors — computed when case reserves were weaker and therefore developed further — with a current, stronger reported figure. Applying those inflated factors to accident year 2017's reported claims **overstates** the ultimate.

The Berquist-Sherman adjustment restates the history to the current adequacy level, producing lower development factors and therefore a **lower**, more appropriate ultimate estimate for AY 2017 than the unadjusted data would give.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to identify that the unadjusted loss development method overstates ultimate losses when case reserve adequacy increases.

Common mistakes included concluding that the unadjusted method would understate the ultimate, and describing the mechanics of the adjustment without actually comparing the result to the unadjusted one.

## Part d (0.25 point)

Briefly describe a potential limitation to the Berquist-Sherman adjustment in part b. above.

### Explanation

The adjustment rests entirely on the selected severity trend, which is a judgmental choice: the restated case outstanding — and hence the ultimate estimate — is highly sensitive to it, so an incorrect trend selection (or a trend that changed over the historical period) can shift the reserve estimate substantially.

A related limitation is that the method attributes the *entire* difference between the observed change in average case outstanding and the severity trend to a change in case adequacy, when it could arise from other causes — a shift in the mix between large and small claims, or a change in which claims are prioritized for settlement.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to understand the limitations of the Berquist-Sherman case outstanding adjustment.

Common mistakes included identifying an assumption of the adjustment that could be violated rather than a limitation of the adjustment itself, and identifying when the technique is not appropriate rather than a limitation.
