Two different models of televisions, A and B, have exponentially distributed lifespans, measured in years. The probability that television A and television B are still working $T$ years from now is 0.49 and 0.70, respectively.

The variance of television A's lifespan is 5.60.

Calculate the variance of television B's lifespan.

- A) $1.40$
- B) $1.80$
- C) $2.80$
- D) $11.20$
- E) $22.40$

## Explanation

The shared but unknown time $T$ is the link between the two televisions, so find it from A first.

An exponential distribution has variance equal to the square of its mean, so A's mean is

$$
\lambda_1 = \sqrt{5.60} = 2.3664
$$

Its survival probability at $T$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
0.49 &= e^{-T/2.3664} \\
-\frac{T}{2.3664} &= \ln(0.49) = -0.7133 \\
T &= 1.6881
\end{align*}
$$

Now use the same $T$ for television B:

$$
\begin{align*}
0.70 &= e^{-1.6881/\lambda_2} \\
-\frac{1.6881}{\lambda_2} &= \ln(0.70) = -0.3567 \\
\lambda_2 &= 4.7332
\end{align*}
$$

so

$$
\begin{align*}
\text{Var}(Y) &= \lambda_2^2 \\
              &= 4.7332^2 \\
              &= \mathbf{22.40}
\end{align*}
$$

There is a shortcut hiding here: $0.49 = (0.70)^2$, so $e^{-T/\lambda_1} = e^{-2T/\lambda_2}$, giving $\lambda_2 = 2\lambda_1$ exactly. Doubling the mean quadruples the variance, so $\text{Var}(Y) = 4(5.60) = 22.40$ with no logarithms at all. Choice (D), 11.20, is $2(5.60)$ — doubling the variance instead of the mean, which forgets that variance scales with the *square*.
