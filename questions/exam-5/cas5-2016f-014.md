An insured purchases a \$400,000 policy on a property valued at \$500,000.

- The coinsurance requirement for the policy is 90% of property value.
- No deductible applies.

## Part a (0.25 points)

Calculate the coinsurance penalty for a \$300,000 loss.

### Answer
\$33,333

### Explanation
The coinsurance clause pays a loss in proportion to how much insurance the insured actually bought against how much the clause required. That proportion is the **apportionment ratio**:

$$a = \min\left(\frac{F}{c \times V},\ 1\right) = \min\left(\frac{400{,}000}{0.90 \times 500{,}000},\ 1\right) = \min\left(\frac{400{,}000}{450{,}000},\ 1\right) = \frac{8}{9} = 0.8889$$

where $F$ is the face amount, $c$ the coinsurance requirement and $V$ the property value. The insured carried \$400,000 where \$450,000 was required, so only 8/9 of any loss is paid.

$$\text{Payment} = a \times L = 0.8889 \times 300{,}000 = \$266{,}667$$

$$\text{Coinsurance penalty} = L - a \times L = (1 - 0.8889) \times 300{,}000 = \mathbf{\$33{,}333}$$

(The published sample rounds $a$ to 0.889 and reports \$33,300; carrying the exact 8/9 gives \$33,333.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know how to calculate a coinsurance penalty: form the apportionment ratio from the face amount over the required amount of insurance, then apply the shortfall to the loss.

The common mistake was a calculation error.

## Part b (0.25 points)

Calculate the maximum coinsurance penalty.

### Answer
\$44,444

### Explanation
The penalty does not grow without bound, because the policy never pays more than its face amount. Tracking the penalty as the loss $L$ grows:

- **For $L \le F = \$400{,}000$:** the insured would have collected $L$ in full without the clause but collects $aL$ with it, so the penalty is $(1-a)L$ — increasing in $L$.
- **For $F < L < c \times V = \$450{,}000$:** without the clause the payment is capped at $F$, while the coinsurance payment $aL$ is still rising toward $F$. The penalty $F - aL$ now *shrinks*.
- **For $L \ge \$450{,}000$:** $aL \ge F$, both payments equal the face amount, and the penalty is zero.

So the penalty peaks exactly where the loss equals the face amount:

$$\text{Maximum penalty} = F \times (1 - a) = 400{,}000 \times \left(1 - \frac{8}{9}\right) = \frac{400{,}000}{9} = \mathbf{\$44{,}444}$$

(The published sample's rounded ratio gives \$44,400.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that the penalty is largest when the loss equals the policy's face amount, and to apply the same apportionment shortfall to that amount.

The common mistake was a calculation error.

## Part c (0.25 points)

Calculate the coinsurance apportionment ratio, assuming the property is valued at \$425,000 instead of \$500,000.

### Answer
1.00 — there is no coinsurance penalty.

### Explanation
Revaluing the property lowers the amount of insurance the clause requires:

$$c \times V = 0.90 \times 425{,}000 = \$382{,}500$$

The insured carries \$400,000, which now **exceeds** the requirement, so the ratio is capped at one:

$$a = \min\left(\frac{F}{c \times V},\ 1\right) = \min\left(\frac{400{,}000}{382{,}500},\ 1\right) = \min(1.0458,\ 1) = \mathbf{1.00}$$

The cap is the point of the `min`: carrying more insurance than the clause requires earns no bonus, it simply removes the penalty. Losses are paid in full up to the face amount.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the apportionment ratio correctly, including recognizing that it is capped at 1.

A common mistake was stating that there would be no coinsurance penalty without actually writing that the apportionment ratio equals 1, which is what the question asked for.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Briefly describe two issues associated with underinsured properties.

### Explanation
Any two of the following:

- **The insured is not made whole.** A property insured below its value cannot be restored to its pre-loss condition out of the policy proceeds — the payment is reduced by the apportionment ratio and is capped at a face amount that is itself below the property's value.
- **Premium is inequitable across insureds.** Rates are built per unit of insurance, but partial losses do not scale down with the amount of insurance purchased: an underinsured property is exposed to nearly the same expected loss as a fully insured one while paying for fewer units. Underinsured risks are therefore subsidized by fully insured risks.
- **Premium is inadequate for the underinsured business.** The same mismatch means the premium collected on underinsured policies falls short of their expected losses, so the rate level is deficient for that segment.
- **Regulatory or judicial risk after a catastrophe.** Following a widespread event, an insurer may face pressure — or be compelled — to pay beyond what the coinsurance clause strictly requires, undermining the pricing assumption.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate an understanding of the issues underinsurance creates, and to state each issue with enough precision to show which party is affected and how.

Common mistakes included saying premium would be inequitable without specifying that this is inequity of underinsured properties *relative to fully insured* ones; writing that a loss is "not covered" for underinsured policies rather than "not *fully* covered"; and citing the effect of inflation on property values, which bears on fully insured and underinsured properties alike and so earned no credit.
