A brief on microinsurance products designed to protect poor microentrepreneurs from massive disasters by covering damage to income-generating assets and disruption to their livelihoods. It describes pilot livestock, asset and property, livelihood and work security, and Islamic (takaful) insurance schemes, how microinsurance products for natural disasters are designed, priced and delivered, and the conditions under which the brief finds microinsurance effective for natural disasters.

## Introduction
- Microinsurance intends to offer the poor protection against specific risks in return for regular premiums proportionate to the likelihood and cost of the risk; the available guidance covers life, loan and health insurance against personal calamities.
- A study of microinsurance in Bangladesh found health, life and loan insurance covering about one third of the poor, while disaster and livestock insurance were virtually unavailable (Ahmed et al., 2005).
- Information on microinsurance programs for asset and livelihood losses after major disasters is sketchy, so it remains unclear whether microinsurance is feasible or effective for highly destructive natural disasters.

## A Livestock Insurance
- Requiring insurance when livestock is financed by bank loans is practised in several countries; the Grameen Bank found insuring livestock in rainy months prohibitively costly, partly from limited staff skills and an inability to pool risks and achieve volume.
- MFIs now partner with specialised insurers: SHEPARD offers group-based livestock insurance with a local agency, and BASIX has offered livestock insurance with Royal Sundaram since 2002; neither scheme has been exposed to a major disaster.
- Index-based livestock schemes, using weather to gauge damage severity, are being considered to make claim triggers more transparent; the World Bank is piloting one in Mongolia.

## B Asset/Property Insurance
- La Equidad's 1996 property product for Colombian microenterprises was suspended after a financial crisis collapsed the cooperatives distributing it.
- Initiatives in India include the Gujarat State Disaster Management Authority's compulsory housing insurance for reconstruction-grant beneficiaries, Caritas India's insured jointly owned livelihood assets, the Swayam tsunami recovery program's compulsory asset insurance, and CARE India's packaged microinsurance with Royal Sundaram.
- Group microinsurance through established insurers can help with claim settlement and keep premiums affordable, but asset insurance is more complex than life insurance: asset values and losses are hard to verify, and many insurers exclude disasters even from products sold as disaster protection.

## C Livelihood and Work Security Insurance
- Livelihood protection or work security insurance can compensate for income lost when a disaster disrupts livelihoods, especially where disasters are frequent and predictable.
- SEWA's work security insurance, provided with the Life Insurance Corporation of India, covered about 32,000 members by 2005; its lessons were the importance of speedy claims processing and of collaborating with major insurers to reduce costs.

## D Islamic Insurance
- Takaful, a variation of mutual insurance based on mutuality, cooperation, shared responsibility and joint indemnity, is emerging in Islamic countries to comply with Shariah law; the first takaful company was established in Sudan in 1979.
- Demand exists for micro-takaful among the poor, but its outreach is limited by a lack of trained personnel, low awareness and a lack of appropriate regulation; Lebanon's Agricultural Mutual Fund provides subsidised health insurance to the rural poor.

## E Designing Microinsurance Products for Natural Disasters
- Steps in product design
    - Opportunity International's client-driven design process has five stages — initial visits to the MFI, product design and pricing, manuals and training materials, pilot testing, and a nationwide rollout — followed by monitoring and market research to refine and redesign products.
- Premiums
    - Premiums should reflect the cost of providing the product and the indemnities, but actuarially based premiums are often unaffordable to the poor, and a lack of data hampers pricing after a disaster; SEWA links premium payments to a savings plan.
- Coverage
    - The extent of coverage depends on scale, diversification and experience, the ability to cross-subsidise between products, retention levels and effective partnerships.
- Delivery method
    - An insurer may provide the product while a delivery organisation (a workers' union, MFI or NGO) sells and services it, or an MFI may act as an intermediary between the target population and an insurer.

## F Effectiveness of Microinsurance for Natural Disasters
- Microinsurance against natural calamities can be effective and efficient only if, among other things, outreach is large and diversified, transaction costs and premiums are low, renewal rates are around 75%, assets are well appraised, data support premium setting, and established insurers and reinsurers are available as partners.
- The brief concludes that a cautious approach is essential in advocating microinsurance as a major tool for the natural disaster management of the poor, though it can be one tool alongside savings, loans, leasing and remittances.
