**Government Program Evaluation** is the structured assessment of whether a government or insurance-industry program, such as a workers' compensation board, a public auto insurer, a residual market, a guaranty fund or an agricultural or disaster program, achieves its purpose at an acceptable cost and can go on doing so. Exam 6C names the criteria it uses:

1. **[[Solvency]]**: can the program pay what it already owes?
2. **Efficiency**: how much of each premium dollar reaches claimants, and at what transaction cost?
3. **Stability**: are rates and benefits predictable?
4. **Viability and long-term prospects**: is the funding model sustainable as exposure, demographics and costs change?
5. **External factors**: what outside the program moves its results?

Evaluation also asks whether the program meets its objective, and how it interacts with the [[Voluntary Private Insurance Market]] and the rest of the [[Social Insurance]] framework.

## Solvency

The measure of solvency differs by program. For a workers' compensation board it is the funded ratio, which is

> $$\text{Funded ratio} = \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Liabilities for benefits incurred}}$$

A [[Public Auto Insurance|public auto insurer]] measures capital against a legislated target, a catastrophe fund its fund balance against the [[Probable Maximum Loss|PML]], and [[PACICC]] its post-event assessment capacity. A program that is solvent only because it can tax or assess later should be described that way.

## Efficiency

Efficiency is measured by the expense ratio, which is

> $$\text{Expense ratio} = \frac{\text{Administrative expense}}{\text{Premium or assessment revenue}}$$

Transaction cost also includes litigation and disputes. Efficiency includes incentive efficiency as well: flat levies, subsidised premiums or benefits with no link to behaviour create [[Moral Hazard|moral hazard]] and [[Adverse Selection|adverse selection]] that raise the program's cost.

## Stability

Large year-to-year swings in assessment rates, politically timed rate freezes followed by catch-up increases, and benefit schedules rewritten every few years ([[Automobile Insurance Reform]]) are all instability, whatever the average level.

## Viability and long-term prospects

The warning signs of a program that is not viable are a growing unfunded liability, falling voluntary participation as good risks leave, a growing [[Residual Market|residual market]], and a funding model that works only while the program is young.

## External factors

The external factors that move a program's results include interest rates (the discount rate on long-tail liabilities), wage and medical [[Inflation|inflation]], the economy (the payroll or premium base, claim duration), [[Climate Risk|climate]] (flood, crop and catastrophe frequency), court decisions widening coverage, and federal cost-sharing formulas.

## Evaluating a program

A good evaluation takes each criterion in turn, gives evidence for each, and ends with a recommendation.

> [!example]- How does this workers' compensation board measure up against each criterion? {Example}
> A provincial [[Workers Compensation Insurance|workers' compensation]] board reports:
>
> | Item | Value |
> | :--- | ---: |
> | Assets | $\$12.6$ billion |
> | Benefit liabilities | $\$14.0$ billion |
> | Duration of liabilities | $8$ years |
> | Assessable payroll | $\$80$ billion |
> | Average assessment rate | $\$1.85$ per $\$100$ of payroll |
> | Administrative expense | $\$190$ million |
>
> Its average rate changes over five years were $+2\%$, $+14\%$, $-9\%$, $+11\%$ and $-6\%$. Its funding policy retires any unfunded liability over $10$ years at $5\%$.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Solvency: the funded ratio is $12.6/14.0 = 90\%$, so the board is $10\%$ underfunded, a shortfall of $\$1.4$ billion.
> > - The funding policy retires the shortfall at $\$1.4\text{B}/a_{\overline{10}|0.05} = \$1.4\text{B}/7.7217 = \$181.3$ million a year.
> > - That is $\$0.23$ per $\$100$ of payroll, a $12\%$ surcharge on the $\$1.85$ average rate.
> > - Efficiency: revenue is $0.0185 \times \$80\text{B} = \$1.48\text{B}$, so the expense ratio is $190/1{,}480 = 12.8\%$.
> > - The expense ratio means little alone, so the evaluation asks what it buys (return-to-work programs lower claim duration) and compares cost per claim with similar boards.
> > - Stability: rate changes swinging between $-9\%$ and $+14\%$ are poor stability for employers who budget payroll costs.
> > - External factors: with duration $8$, a one-point fall in the discount rate raises liabilities by about $8\%$, to $\$15.12$ billion.
> > - If the assets are mainly equities that do not respond, the funded ratio falls to $12.6/15.12 = 83.3\%$.
> > - A recession would also shrink payroll, the assessment base, while lengthening claim durations.
> > - Viability: the board is viable, because the funding policy is credible and the surcharge affordable. Its weaknesses are the interest-rate mismatch and the rate volatility.
> > - Recommendation: match part of the liabilities with duration-matched bonds, and adopt a funding corridor that phases in surcharges and rebates rather than swinging rates each year.
