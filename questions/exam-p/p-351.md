In a group of four employees, two are high-risk and two are low-risk. This year, each high-risk employee has probability 0.6 of having no accidents; each low-risk employee has probability 0.9 of having no accidents.

The occurrences of accidents among employees are independent events.

Calculate the probability that at most one employee has one or more accidents this year.

- A) $0.4536$
- B) $0.5184$
- C) $0.6804$
- D) $0.7084$
- E) $0.7452$

## Explanation

Write $H$ for a high-risk employee and $L$ for a low-risk employee. A high-risk employee has an accident with probability $0.4$ and a low-risk employee with probability $0.1$.

**No employee has an accident.** All four must be accident-free, and independence lets the probabilities multiply:

$$
\begin{align*}
P[\text{0 have accidents}] &= (0.6)^2(0.9)^2 \\
                           &= 0.2916
\end{align*}
$$

**Exactly one employee has an accident.** This happens either when one of the two high-risk employees has an accident and neither low-risk employee does, or when one of the two low-risk employees has an accident and neither high-risk employee does. These two cases are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add:

$$
\begin{align*}
P[\text{1 has accidents}] &= \binom{2}{1}(0.4)(0.6)(0.9)^2 + (0.6)^2\binom{2}{1}(0.1)(0.9) \\
                          &= (2)(0.4)(0.6)(0.81) + (0.36)(2)(0.1)(0.9) \\
                          &= 0.3888 + 0.0648 \\
                          &= 0.4536
\end{align*}
$$

**At most one.** Add the two disjoint cases:

$$
\begin{align*}
P[\text{at most 1}] &= 0.2916 + 0.4536 \\
                    &= \mathbf{0.7452}
\end{align*}
$$

The factor of $\binom{2}{1} = 2$ in each term is easy to lose — it counts *which* of the two employees in that risk class is the one having accidents. Dropping both factors gives choice (A)'s value as the "exactly one" term alone.
