A single retrospectively rated policy has the following adjustments:

| Adjustment | Incremental Losses | Incremental Premium |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$100,000 | \$150,000 |
| 2 | 50,000 | 25,000 |
| 3 | 15,000 | 0 |
| 4 | 20,000 | 5,000 |
| 5 | 5,000 | -1,250 |

## Part a (0.5 points)

Discuss why incremental premium can be greater than incremental losses at the first adjustment.

### Explanation
Premium at the first adjustment includes the **basic premium**, which is charged even if no losses emerge. Also, few losses have been capped by the first adjustment, so multiplying the losses by the **loss conversion factor** and the **tax multiplier** can make the incremental premium exceed the incremental losses (a PDLD ratio above 1.0).

Accepted reasons: the tax multiplier, the loss conversion factor, the basic premium, and the fact that few losses are already capped by the first adjustment. Full credit needed two briefly described, or one explained in detail.

### Examiner Report
Candidates did best on this part of the question. Candidates needed to know how the incremental premium is calculated at the 1st adjustment, and be able to explain how portions of that calculation could cause the PDLD ratio to be above 1.0 at that point. To get full credit, the candidate needed to either go into detail on one of the potential reasons, or briefly describe more than one. The most common error was just naming one element of the formula, without discussing what it is or why it contributes to PDLD ratio being greater than 1.0.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Explain how a positive fourth adjustment to the incremental premium could follow a third adjustment of \$0.

### Explanation
The \$15,000 incremental loss at the 3rd adjustment could be on a claim that has already breached the per-accident limit, so it generates no capped loss and therefore no incremental premium. The loss at the 4th adjustment could be on another claim (new, or one that has not yet reached the limit), so it does generate incremental premium.

Alternatively:

If all development in the 3rd period occurred outside the loss limits, the incremental premium would be 0. A positive incremental premium in the 4th period means that some of its development was within the loss limits.

### Examiner Report
Candidates struggled a bit on this part. Candidates needed to understand the various limits that could prevent the incremental losses from influencing the premium. To get full credit, the candidate needed to provide an explanation both of what could be causing the phenomenon at the 3rd adjustment, and what would have changed about the 4th adjustment. The most common error was not explaining the 4th adjustment at all.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Explain how the observed premium development to loss development (PDLD) ratio could become negative for the fifth adjustment.

### Explanation
Three points were needed:

- Losses on claims below the cap decreased.
- Losses on claims above the cap increased.
- The increase above the cap was greater than the decrease below it, so total incremental losses were positive while capped losses — and so the incremental premium — fell, making the PDLD ratio negative.

### Examiner Report
Candidates performed worst on this part. The required understanding for this part was very similar to what was needed for part b. To get full credit, the candidate needed to explain which types of loss (relative to the caps) were increasing, and which were decreasing. They also needed to point out the relationship between the two types (which was greater). One of the more common errors was not pointing out that the increases had to outweigh the decreases. We accepted many ways of stating or even implying this relationship, but some candidates did not point it out at all. Other candidates ignored the caps completely, only stating that "losses went down, so premium was returned."
