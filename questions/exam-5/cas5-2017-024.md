For each of the following insurers, briefly describe why the classical technique is not optimal and briefly discuss an alternative technique that addresses the problem identified.

- There is no inflation.

## Part a (0.5 points)

An auto insurer expanding its operations by writing policies in two new states.

### Explanation
The classical technique estimates unpaid ULAE from a calendar-year **paid-to-paid ratio**, calendar-year paid ULAE over calendar-year paid claims, and assumes the two move together.

In a book that is growing quickly, they do not. Much of the ULAE spend is incurred as claims are *reported and set up* — staffing, systems and intake for the new states — so it rises immediately with the new volume. Paid claims lag: the claims from the new states have barely begun to settle, so the denominator still reflects the smaller, older book. The ratio is therefore distorted upward, and applying it to unpaid claims overstates the ULAE reserve.

**Kittel's refinement** corrects this by replacing the denominator with the *average of paid and incurred claims*:

$$R_{\text{Kittel}} = \frac{\text{Paid ULAE}}{0.5(\text{Paid claims}) + 0.5(\text{Incurred claims})}$$

Incurred claims recognize the new states' claims as they are reported, at the same time the ULAE is being spent, so the numerator and denominator grow together and the ratio stabilizes.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to diagnose why the classical ULAE technique produces a poor result for a rapidly growing book and then name an alternative that corrects that specific deficiency. The syllabus and examination committee noted afterward that "classical technique" was read by some candidates as the loss development method; full credit was given under either reading, so an answer citing thin data in the new states and an expected claims technique borrowing a loss ratio from established states was also accepted.

Common errors included naming an alternative technique without explaining why it fixes the problem, and proposing Mango-Allen — the issue with a growing book is not volatility in the paid amounts but the classical method's assumption that paid and reported activity are equivalent, which Mango-Allen does not address.

## Part b (0.5 points)

A homeowners insurer located in a hurricane prone area.

### Explanation
A hurricane drives an enormous spike in calendar-year paid claims that ULAE does not match. Catastrophe claims are large in dollars but are not proportionately expensive to administer in unallocated terms, and much of the response cost is allocated or outsourced. The paid-to-paid ratio is therefore artificially **low** in a catastrophe year and **high** in the quiet years in between — the ratio swings with the weather rather than measuring the cost of handling a claim.

The **Mango-Allen bootstrap** addresses exactly this. Instead of dividing by volatile actual calendar-year paid claims, it divides by *expected* paid claims — accident year ultimate estimates run through a payment pattern — so the denominator is smoothed and a single catastrophe no longer dictates the ratio.

A count-based approach such as Conger-Nolibos, which relates ULAE to claim counts rather than claim dollars, is also defensible: a hurricane's distortion is largely one of severity, and counts are far less volatile than dollars.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain why catastrophe exposure makes the classical paid-to-paid ratio unstable and to select an alternative that removes that instability. Answers under the loss development reading were also accepted.

For candidates answering on ULAE, separating catastrophe from non-catastrophe ULAE earned no credit — ULAE cannot be split by claim type without heavy assumptions, unlike claim payments, which can. Under the loss development reading, catastrophe modeling or an average catastrophe load also earned no credit, since a company does not reserve for an "average" catastrophe in a year when none occurred. The main error was choosing an alternative that does not actually damp the volatility: both Kittel's refinement and the generalized approach still rest on ratios of paid ULAE to paid claims, which remain volatile in a catastrophe-prone book.

## Part c (0.5 points)

An insurer writing a long-tail line of business.

### Explanation
The classical technique's reserve formula rests on the **50/50 assumption** — that half of a claim's ULAE is spent when it is reported and half when it is settled — which is what produces

$$\text{ULAE reserve} = R \times \left(0.5 \times \text{Case outstanding} + 1.0 \times \text{IBNR}\right)$$

On a long-tail line that assumption fails. Claims stay open for years and consume ongoing **maintenance** cost — diary reviews, litigation management, periodic payments — so far more than half the ULAE is spent after the claim is opened. Loading only 50% onto reported-but-unpaid claims understates the reserve.

The **Conger-Nolibos generalized approach** drops the 50/50 split entirely: ULAE is attributed to the events that generate it — opening, maintaining and closing a claim — with separately selected weights, and each weight is applied to the corresponding unpaid counts or dollars. That flexibility is what a long-tail line needs, because it lets the maintenance share be set to whatever the company's own claim data supports.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain why a long-tailed line breaks the classical technique's assumption about *when* ULAE is spent over a claim's life, and to name an alternative that relaxes it. Answers under the loss development reading — highly leveraged development factors at immature ages, use Bornhuetter-Ferguson or a frequency-severity approach instead — were also accepted.

The common mistake was attributing the classical ULAE technique's weakness to the difficulty of estimating IBNR for the underlying claims. That is a problem with the unpaid claim estimate the ratio is applied to, not with the ULAE technique itself.
