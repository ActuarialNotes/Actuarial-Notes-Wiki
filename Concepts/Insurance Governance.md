**Insurance Governance** is the system by which an insurer is directed and controlled: board oversight, independent audit and risk functions, internal controls and [[Enterprise Risk Management|enterprise risk management]]. In the U.S. it also includes the state requirements that make that system visible to regulators.

There is no single U.S. governance code. Regulators layered process and disclosure requirements, mostly through the Solvency Modernization Initiative ([[History of Solvency Regulation]]):

1. **Model Audit Rule.** The auditor answers to the audit committee, independence scales with size, management reports on internal control, and an internal audit function has direct access to the board ([[Sarbanes-Oxley]]).
2. **Risk Management and ORSA Model Act** (effective 2015). The insurer maintains a risk-management framework, conducts an Own Risk and Solvency Assessment at least annually, and files a confidential ORSA Summary Report with the lead state. The report covers the framework, the insurer's assessment of its risk exposures under normal and stressed conditions, and group risk capital with a prospective solvency assessment. The idea is the one on the vault's [[ORSA]] page, written for Canada.
3. **Corporate Governance Annual Disclosure** (CGAD, NAIC models adopted 2014). A confidential annual disclosure, due June 1, of the governance framework, board and committee practices, policies for senior management (suitability, code of conduct, compensation, succession) and how the board oversees critical risk areas. It sets no standard. It makes the insurer describe and defend its own.
4. **Holding-company act.** Form A covers acquisition of control, Form B registration, and Form D prior notice of material affiliate transactions. Since 2010, Form F is the ultimate controlling person's report on enterprise risks outside the insurers that could harm them.

Compare the Canadian treatment on [[Corporate Governance]].

## Requirements by size

An insurer with annual direct written plus assumed premium above $\$500$ million must have:

1. an ORSA,
2. an internal audit function,
3. management's report on internal control over financial reporting, and
4. an audit committee at least $75\%$ independent.

The models differ in detail on affiliated business. ORSA and internal audit also apply to any insurer in a group above $\$1$ billion.

> [!example]- What governance problems does this mutual insurer have? {Example}
> A mutual P&C insurer writes $\$700$ million of direct and assumed premium. Four of its seven audit committee members are independent. The head of internal audit reports only to the CEO and has not met the audit committee this year. The ORSA Summary Report is drafted by consultants and tabled at the board without discussion. The chief risk officer reports to the CFO.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The audit committee is $4/7 = 57\%$ independent, below the $75\%$ required above $\$500$ million, so it breaches the model as enacted.
> > - The internal audit function must be organisationally independent, its head must have direct and unrestricted access to the board, and it must report to the audit committee at least annually. Reporting solely to the CEO breaches this, although a dual line to the CEO and the audit committee would be acceptable.
> > - The ORSA is the insurer's own assessment, and the summary report is attested by the CRO or the executive responsible for ERM. A board that never discusses it cannot show it uses it, so an examiner will pursue this weakness.
> > - A CRO reporting to the CFO is not prohibited, but the CGAD must describe it, and the examiner will test whether risk management can challenge the finance function it reports to.
> > - The numeric thresholds are easy to fix. The ownership and challenge failures are what the examination report will dwell on.

## Examination and the regulators' own governance

Risk-focused financial examinations assess the board, management oversight and the risk-management system, and a regulator can order governance deficiencies corrected ([[Solvency Monitoring]]).

Most commissioners are appointed by the governor, and about a dozen states elect theirs. The NAIC is governed by those commissioners, and its models bind no one until enacted. Accreditation is the peer review that keeps baseline solvency standards uniform ([[State and Federal Insurance Regulation]]).
