A regulator wants to benchmark the underwriting profit provisions between companies.

For Company A's rate filing, the following is assumed:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Projected total fixed costs | \$50,000 |
| Projected total loss and LAE | \$600,000 |
| Projected exposures | 2,000 |
| Indicated rate per exposure | \$500 |

For Company B's rate filing, the following is assumed:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Projected total fixed costs | \$50,000 |
| Projected total loss and LAE | \$600,000 |
| Projected premium at current rates | \$900,000 |
| Indicated rate change | 16.5% |

- The variable expense ratio is the same for each company.

## Part a (1.25 points)

Determine which company's filing includes the higher underwriting profit provision.

### Answer
Company B, by about 3 percentage points.

### Explanation
Neither filing states its profit provision, so each must be recovered by running the company's own indication formula backwards. Both formulas carry the profit provision inside the same denominator $1 - V - Q$, so backing that denominator out of each filing and comparing is enough — the common variable expense ratio $V$ cancels.

**Company A — pure premium (rate per exposure) form.** Company A gives an indicated *rate*, so it used

$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Pure premium} + \text{Fixed expense per exposure}}{1 - V - Q_A}$$

The numerator is the projected loss and LAE plus fixed cost, per exposure:

$$\frac{600{,}000 + 50{,}000}{2{,}000} = \frac{650{,}000}{2{,}000} = 325$$

$$500 = \frac{325}{1 - V - Q_A} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 - V - Q_A = \frac{325}{500} = 0.65$$

**Company B — loss ratio form.** Company B gives an indicated rate *change*, so it used

$$1 + \Delta = \frac{(\text{Loss} + \text{LAE} + \text{Fixed})/\text{Premium at current rates}}{1 - V - Q_B}$$

$$1.165 = \frac{(600{,}000 + 50{,}000)/900{,}000}{1 - V - Q_B} = \frac{0.72222}{1 - V - Q_B}$$

$$1 - V - Q_B = \frac{0.72222}{1.165} = 0.61993$$

**Compare.** The variable expense ratio is the same for both, so subtracting the two denominators isolates the difference in profit provisions:

$$Q_B - Q_A = (1 - V - Q_A) - (1 - V - Q_B) = 0.65 - 0.61993 = 0.0301$$

Company B's filing carries the higher underwriting profit provision, roughly **3 percentage points** higher than Company A's. Note the fixed costs must stay *inside* the numerator of each indication — they are a projected cost like loss and LAE, not part of the variable-expense denominator.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to set up the overall rate indication for each company — the pure premium form for Company A, the loss ratio form for Company B — and solve each for the permissible loss and fixed expense provision $1 - V - Q$, then compare the two given that the variable expense ratio is common.

Common errors included computing a total projected profit dollar amount rather than a profit *provision*; mishandling the fixed expenses, typically by treating them as a percentage in the denominator instead of a projected cost in the numerator; and mishandling Company B's indicated rate change, most often by setting the indication equal to 0.165 rather than to $1 + 0.165$.

## Part b (0.5 points)

List two reasons the underwriting profit provision might differ between companies with the same loss, LAE and expense experience.

### Explanation
Two companies with identical underwriting results can still target different underwriting profit because the underwriting profit provision is set to complement *investment* income and to reflect *risk*, business strategy and regulation — none of which the loss and expense experience reveals. Any two of:

- **Different investment income.** A company writing longer-tailed business holds the premium longer and earns more investment income on the reserves, so it can accept a lower underwriting profit provision and still hit its total return target.
- **Different risk profile.** A company writing more volatile or higher-severity risks requires a larger return for the capital supporting them, and so builds in a higher profit provision.
- **Growth or market-share strategy.** A company trying to grow may deliberately reduce its profit provision to price competitively and win business.
- **Regulatory environment.** Jurisdictions differ in what profit provision they will approve, so companies filing in different states face different allowable maxima.
- **Different cost of capital / leverage.** A company holding more surplus per dollar of premium needs a higher underwriting margin to earn the same return on equity.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify two reasons that would cause two companies to *target* different profit provisions, given that loss, LAE and expense experience is held equal.

Common errors included naming reasons that act on expected losses or expenses — which the question has already equalized — and naming reasons that change the rate ultimately implemented (competition on the filed rate, judgment in selection) rather than the target profit provision itself.
