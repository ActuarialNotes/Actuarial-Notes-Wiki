A business's property policy, by endorsement, settles losses to its machinery at book value: cost less accumulated straight-line depreciation on the insured's books, with no salvage value. A machine bought six years ago for \$70,000 and written off over a 10-year accounting life is destroyed. A machine of like kind and quality costs \$90,000 today, with a physical useful life of 15 years. With a \$1,000 deductible, what does the insurer pay?

- A) \$27,000
- B) \$41,000
- C) \$53,000
- D) \$89,000

## Explanation

Book value starts from the insured's purchase price and depreciates over the accounting life.

$$
\begin{align*}
\text{BV} &= 70{,}000 \times \left(1 - \frac{6}{10}\right) \\
&= 28{,}000 \\
\text{Payment} &= 28{,}000 - 1{,}000 \\
&= 27{,}000
\end{align*}
$$

The answer is **A**. Accounting depreciation follows a schedule, not the machine's condition, which is why book value usually falls well below actual cash value for property still in use.

**Why not the others**

- **B** — \$41,000 depreciates the purchase price over the physical life, nine fifteenths of \$70,000 less the deductible, mixing the two bases.
- **C** — \$53,000 is actual cash value, nine fifteenths of \$90,000 or \$54,000, less the deductible.
- **D** — \$89,000 is replacement cost less the deductible.
