**Market Conduct Regulation** is provincial supervision of how insurers *behave* toward customers — sales and advertising, product suitability, claims handling, complaint resolution, privacy and the licensing of agents and adjusters. It is the counterpart to [[Solvency Regulation]] and is what a provincial [[Superintendent of Insurance]] spends most of its time on.

- The organising principle across Canada is now the **fair treatment of customers**, set out in joint [[Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators|CCIR]]/CISRO guidance covering the whole product life cycle: design, distribution, disclosure, claims and complaints.
- **Claims handling** is the highest-stakes area. An insurer owes a [[Duty of Good Faith|duty of good faith]] in handling a claim, and a breach is actionable in court as well as before the regulator — see [[Bad Faith Damages]].
- **Distribution conduct** covers agent licensing and conduct, disclosure of compensation and conflicts, and the [[Take-All-Comers Rule|take-all-comers]] obligation in regulated auto markets.
- Insurers file an annual **Market Conduct Annual Statement** with provincial regulators (a CCIR product, distinct from OSFI's prudential [[Canadian Annual Return]]) reporting complaint counts, claims-denial rates and distribution data, which regulators use to target examinations.
- The industry's self-regulatory layer sits alongside: the [[Insurance Bureau of Canada]]'s [[Code of Consumer Rights and Responsibilities]] and the General Insurance OmbudService for complaint escalation.
- Market conduct increasingly reaches **actuarial** work: whether a rating variable is fair ([[Unfair Discrimination]], [[Bias in Actuarial Practice]]) and whether an algorithm can be explained to the customer are now conduct questions, not just pricing questions.

> [!example]- Is This a Conduct Problem or a Pricing Problem? {Example}
> An insurer's model charges materially more to policyholders who shop around less, using a "price optimisation" factor built from renewal-response data rather than expected loss.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > It is both, and the conduct dimension is the binding one.
> >
> > - **Actuarially**, the factor is not a measure of expected cost. It prices *elasticity*, so two policyholders with identical risk pay different premiums for the same coverage.
> > - **In conduct terms**, that is charging on the basis of a customer's inattention. Canadian regulators have taken the position that rates must be justified by risk and expense, so the variable is not permitted in filings.
> > - The **fair treatment of customers** framing makes it worse than a technical filing defect: the practice targets the customers least able to protect themselves, which is precisely the outcome the guidance is written to prevent.
> >
> > The actuary's professional position is straightforward. Supporting a filing whose classification plan is not risk-based conflicts with the [[Standards of Practice]] and with the duty to the public interest, whatever the commercial case for it.
