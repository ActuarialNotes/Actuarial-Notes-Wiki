Match each random variable to the distribution that describes it. An option may be used more than once or not at all.

## Part a (1 point)
An underwriter reviews applications one at a time; each, independently, is declined with probability 0.2. $X$ is the number of applications reviewed up to and including the third one declined.

- A) Binomial
- B) Geometric
- C) Negative binomial
- D) Poisson
- E) Hypergeometric

### Answer
C

### Explanation
The number of independent trials needed to reach a fixed number of successes (here 3 declines) is **negative binomial**. **A** fixes the number of trials and counts successes — here it is the other way round. **B** is the special case of waiting for the *first* success only; waiting for the third is not geometric. **D** and **E** have no sequence of independent trials stopping at a target count.

## Part b (1 point)
A claims file holds 20 claims, 6 of which were paid in error. An auditor selects 5 of the 20 at random, without replacement. $X$ is the number of selected claims paid in error.

- A) Binomial
- B) Geometric
- C) Negative binomial
- D) Poisson
- E) Hypergeometric

### Answer
E

### Explanation
Drawing a fixed number of items **without replacement** from a finite population with a known number of "successes" is **hypergeometric**: $P(X = k) = \binom{6}{k}\binom{14}{5-k} / \binom{20}{5}$. **A** would be right only with replacement (or a population so large that the draws are effectively independent); here each draw changes the chance of the next, so the draws are not independent trials with a constant probability. **B**, **C** and **D** do not count successes in a fixed-size sample.

## Part c (1 point)
Claims are reported to a branch office independently of one another, at an average rate of 3 per week. $X$ is the number of claims reported in a given week.

- A) Binomial
- B) Geometric
- C) Negative binomial
- D) Poisson
- E) Hypergeometric

### Answer
D

### Explanation
A count of independent events occurring at a constant average rate over a fixed interval, with no fixed upper limit, is **Poisson** with mean 3. **A** needs a fixed number of trials $n$, and nothing here caps the count. **B** and **C** count trials until a target, not events in a fixed period. **E** needs a finite population sampled without replacement.

## Part d (1 point)
A small group plan covers 12 employees; each, independently, files a disability claim during the year with probability 0.05. $X$ is the number of employees who file a claim.

- A) Binomial
- B) Geometric
- C) Negative binomial
- D) Poisson
- E) Hypergeometric

### Answer
A

### Explanation
A fixed number of independent trials (12 employees), each with the same success probability (0.05), counting the successes: **binomial** with $n = 12$, $p = 0.05$. **D** is the tempting one — a Poisson with mean $\lambda = 12(0.05) = 0.6$ is an *approximation* to this binomial when $n$ is large and $p$ small, but the count here cannot exceed 12 and the exact distribution is binomial. **E** would apply only if the 0.05 were a fixed number of claimants drawn without replacement. **B** and **C** count trials until a target, not successes in fixed trials.

Option **B** (geometric) is used by none of the four.
