**The Insurance Companies Act** (ICA) is the federal statute governing federally regulated insurers in Canada: it creates them, defines what business they may carry on, sets their governance and disclosure duties, and gives [[OSFI]] its supervisory powers. It is the source of the [[Appointed Actuary]] role and of the requirement to value [[Insurance Contract Liabilities|policy liabilities]] in accordance with [[Accepted Actuarial Practice|accepted actuarial practice]].

- **The actuary's appointment (s. 357).** The *directors* appoint the actuary — not management — and the appointment may be revoked only by the directors, with notice to OSFI. That reporting line is the structural protection behind the role.
- **Valuation (s. 365).** The actuary values the [[Insurance Contract Liabilities|actuarial and other policy liabilities]] as at the year end in accordance with accepted actuarial practice, with any changes OSFI may specify. This is the hook that makes the [[Standards of Practice|CIA Standards of Practice]] effectively binding on federally regulated insurers.
- **Reporting duties (ss. 358–368).** The actuary must be given access to the records needed for the valuation, must report to the directors on the company's financial position (the [[FCT|financial condition testing]] report), and must report to the CEO and CFO — and if not adequately addressed, to the directors and then to OSFI — any matter that has *material adverse effects* on the financial condition. See [[Duty to Report]].
- **Civil immunity (s. 361).** The actuary is protected from civil liability for statements made in good faith in carrying out these duties, which is what makes the whistle-blowing duty workable.
- **Adequacy of assets (s. 515).** The company must maintain adequate capital and appropriate forms of liquidity, and OSFI may direct an increase; this is the statutory authority under which the [[MCT]] guideline operates.
- Provincially incorporated insurers are governed by the corresponding **provincial Insurance Act**, which generally mirrors these provisions — the actuary's duties do not disappear because the charter is provincial.

> [!example]- The Actuary Discovers a Reserve Deficiency {Example}
> During the year-end valuation, the Appointed Actuary of a federally regulated insurer concludes that a reserve strengthening of $\$40$ million is needed. The CFO instructs the actuary to book $\$15$ million now and "phase in the rest." Surplus is $\$120$ million.
>
> What does the ICA require?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The valuation is the *actuary's*, not management's. Under s. 365 the liabilities must be valued in accordance with accepted actuarial practice, so the actuary's report states the amount the actuary's work supports — $\$40$ million — regardless of what management prefers to carry.
> >
> > If management then carries $\$15$ million:
> >
> > 1. The actuary's [[Statement of Actuarial Opinion|opinion]] cannot say the amount carried makes appropriate provision. A $\$25$ million shortfall against $\$120$ million of surplus is plainly material.
> > 2. This is a matter with **material adverse effects on the financial condition** of the company, so the s. 368 escalation applies: report to the CEO and CFO, and if not suitably addressed, to the **directors**, and if still not addressed, to **OSFI**.
> > 3. Section 361 protects the actuary from civil liability for making that report in good faith.
> >
> > The wrong answer is to resign quietly. Resignation without reporting leaves the deficiency undisclosed, which is the exact outcome the statute is designed to prevent.

> [!example]- Who Can Fire the Appointed Actuary? {Example}
> A new CEO is unhappy with the Appointed Actuary's insistence on a higher [[Risk Adjustment for Non-Financial Risk|risk adjustment]] and tells the actuary they are being replaced effective immediately.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The CEO cannot do this. Under s. 357 the **directors** appoint and revoke the appointment. If the directors do revoke it, both the company and the actuary must notify OSFI, and the actuary may set out the reasons — so a dismissal over a valuation disagreement becomes visible to the regulator rather than invisible.
> >
> > A successor actuary is also expected, under the [[Standards of Practice]], to communicate with the predecessor before accepting the appointment. The combined effect is that "replace the actuary until you get the answer you want" is a strategy the framework is specifically built to defeat.
