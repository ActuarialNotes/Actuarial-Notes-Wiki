**Business-Level Strategy** is the integrated set of commitments and actions a firm uses to gain a competitive advantage in a particular product market, by exploiting its core competencies to serve chosen customers. A business-level strategy answers who the firm will serve, what needs it will satisfy, and how, meaning which core competencies it will use.

An insurer might serve young families, meet their need for cheap protection, and do it with automated underwriting. Business-level strategy is about how to compete in one market. Which markets and businesses to be in is [[Corporate-Level Strategy|corporate-level strategy]].

Hitt, Ireland and Hoskisson describe five such strategies, formed from the source of advantage (lowest cost or uniqueness) and the competitive scope (broad or narrow): cost leadership, differentiation, focused cost leadership, focused differentiation, and the integrated cost leadership/differentiation strategy.

| Competitive scope | Lowest cost | Uniqueness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Broad market | Cost leadership | Differentiation |
| Narrow segment | Focused cost leadership | Focused differentiation |

The integrated strategy pursues both sources of advantage at once.

## The five strategies and their risks

Each strategy defends against [[Porter's Five Forces]]. Low cost leaves margin when buyers or rivals push price down, and deters entrants who lack scale. Differentiation builds loyalty that weakens buyer power, substitutes and rivalry. The value chain shows where the cost or the uniqueness is created.

### Cost leadership

The firm sells a standard, acceptable product to a broad market at the lowest cost, through scale, a lean value chain and simple products. Its risks are that a competitor's new technology removes the cost edge, that cutting cost blinds the firm to changes in what customers want, and that rivals imitate its processes.

### Differentiation

The firm offers features customers value and will pay for, such as brand, service, advice, underwriting flexibility or riders, and recovers the extra cost through price. Its risks are that customers decide the premium over cheaper products is too large, that the feature stops being valued, that experience teaches customers they can do without it, and that imitations narrow the difference.

A differentiating feature counts only if customers will pay more for it than it costs.

### Focused cost leadership and focused differentiation

Focused cost leadership and focused differentiation apply the same logic to a narrow segment, such as a region, an occupation or high-net-worth clients. They fail if a competitor focuses on a narrower part of the segment, if a broad competitor decides the segment is worth serving, or if the segment's needs converge with the broad market's.

### Integrated cost leadership/differentiation

The firm pursues low cost and some differentiation at once, often with flexible technology and processes. Done well it serves a wide range of customers. Its risk is ending up "stuck in the middle", with neither the lowest cost nor a difference customers will pay for.

## Profit under cost leadership and differentiation

A firm selling $q$ units, such as policies in force, at a price $P$ per unit (the premium) and a full cost $C$ per unit (claims, expenses and the cost of capital) earns a profit of

> $$\Pi = q \times (P - C)$$

> [!example]- How do the profits of these two term life insurers compare, and what volume does D need to match L? {Example}
> Two insurers sell 20-year [[Term Life Insurance|term life]]. Insurer L is a cost leader that sells online with automated underwriting. Insurer D is a differentiator that sells through advisers with flexible underwriting and conversion riders.
>
> | Insurer | Premium per policy | Full cost per policy | Policies |
> | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
> | L | $\$400$ | $\$370$ | 100,000 |
> | D | $\$520$ | $\$460$ | 45,000 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - L earns $100{,}000 \times (400 - 370) = \$3{,}000{,}000$.
> > - D earns $45{,}000 \times (520 - 460) = \$2{,}700{,}000$.
> > - L earns $\$30$ a policy on a large volume, and D earns twice the margin on less than half the volume.
> > - To match L, D needs $3{,}000{,}000 / 60 = 50{,}000$ policies, about 11% more than it has.
> > - Both strategies can be profitable because they serve different customers: price-sensitive buyers who are comfortable online, and buyers who value advice and flexibility.

The firm's margin is $\pi = P - C$. Against the industry's average price $\bar{P}$, average cost $\bar{C}$ and average margin $\bar{\pi}$, the firm's excess margin is

> $$\pi - \bar{\pi} = (P - \bar{P}) + (\bar{C} - C)$$

A cost leader earns its advantage through $\bar{C} - C$, and a differentiator through $P - \bar{P}$.

> [!example]- What does each insurer earn after the shock that hits its strategy? {Example}
> Insurer L, the cost leader above, has 100,000 policies at a full cost of $\$370$. An insurtech with a full cost of $\$350$ enters and prices at $\$380$, and L must match it to keep its policies. Insurer D, the differentiator, has 45,000 policies at a full cost of $\$460$. Customers stop valuing D's riders and advice, so D can charge no more than L's old $\$400$ and still keep its policies.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - L earns $100{,}000 \times (380 - 370) = \$1{,}000{,}000$.
> > - L stays profitable but loses two-thirds of its profit, which is the cost leader's risk of a rival with better technology.
> > - D earns $45{,}000 \times (400 - 460) = -\$2{,}700{,}000$.
> > - D loses money, because its cost base was built to deliver a difference that customers no longer pay for.
> > - D must find a feature customers will still pay for or cut its cost. Failing at both leaves it stuck in the middle.

> [!trap] Low price is not cost leadership
> Cost leadership means the lowest cost, not the lowest price. A firm that cuts price without a cost advantage is buying volume at a loss, and in insurance that invites [[Adverse Selection|adverse selection]] as well.
