**The Standards of Practice** are the rules the [[Canadian Institute of Actuaries (CIA)]] sets for how actuarial work in Canada must be done. They comprise **General Standards**, applying to all practice, and **Practice-Specific Standards**, including those for insurance contract valuation. Compliance is mandatory for CIA members, and the [[Insurance Companies Act]]'s requirement to value liabilities in accordance with [[Accepted Actuarial Practice]] gives them legal force for federally regulated insurers.

- **The General Standards** cover the work an actuary does regardless of practice area: the **appropriateness of the work** to its use, **data** sufficiency and reliability, **assumptions and methods** and the basis for selecting them, **[[Materiality]]**, **[[Subsequent Events]]**, **approximations**, **reliance on others**, and **communication** — including the requirement that a report state its user, its purpose and its limitations.
- **The Practice-Specific Standards** for P&C insurance address the valuation of [[Insurance Contract Liabilities]] under [[IFRS 17]]: the measurement of [[Fulfilment Cash Flows|fulfilment cash flows]], the [[Risk Adjustment for Non-Financial Risk|risk adjustment]], [[IFRS 17 Discount Rates|discount rates]], and the [[Appointed Actuary]]'s reports and opinions.
- **They are principles-based**, not a recipe. They tell the actuary what must be considered and disclosed, not which development factor to select — which places the burden of judgement, and of documenting it, on the actuary.
- **The hierarchy of professional obligation:** the CIA's **Rules of Professional Conduct** (integrity, competence, disclosure of conflicts, cooperation with other actuaries) sit above the Standards of Practice; **[[Educational Note|educational notes]]** sit below them as non-binding guidance on how to comply.
- **Deviation is permitted but must be disclosed.** An actuary may depart from the standards where circumstances justify it, provided the deviation and its reason are stated. Silent deviation is a discipline matter.
- **Enforcement** runs through the CIA's discipline process, and — for an [[Appointed Actuary]] — through [[OSFI]], which can find the work inadequate and require it to be redone.

> [!example]- Which Standard Is Engaged? {Example}
> Identify the standard at issue in each situation and what it requires.
>
> 1. Claim data for one line cannot be reconciled to the general ledger, with a $\$4$ million unexplained difference on $\$300$ million of liabilities.
> 2. A court decision after the valuation date but before the report is signed widens a benefit definition.
> 3. The actuary uses a reserve estimate for a small subsidiary prepared by that subsidiary's own actuary.
> 4. The actuary selects a tail factor materially different from last year's with no explanation in the report.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. **Data.** The standard requires the actuary to assess whether the data are sufficient and reliable for the purpose. A $\$4$ million difference on $\$300$ million is about $1.3\%$ — likely immaterial, but the actuary must **investigate it, form a conclusion, and disclose** it. Ignoring it because it is small is not the same as concluding it is immaterial, and only the second satisfies the standard.
> > 2. **[[Subsequent Events]].** The decision provides evidence of conditions that existed at the valuation date, so the liability must be **adjusted**, not merely disclosed. A subsequent event that creates a *new* condition after the date would be disclosed instead.
> > 3. **Reliance on others.** Permitted, but the actuary must be satisfied the other actuary's work is appropriate for this purpose, and must **disclose the reliance** in the [[Appointed Actuary's Report]] and the opinion. The Appointed Actuary remains responsible for the opinion given.
> > 4. **Communication, and it is a clear failure.** A material assumption change must be identified, its effect on the liability quantified, and the reason given. Otherwise a reader cannot separate the effect of experience from the effect of the actuary's own judgement — which is exactly the distinction the report exists to make.
