For each of the following insurers, briefly describe why the classical technique is not optimal and briefly discuss an alternative technique that addresses the problem identified.

- There is no inflation.

## Part a (0.5 points)

An auto insurer expanding its operations by writing policies in two new states.

### Explanation
The classical technique estimates unpaid ULAE from a calendar-year **paid-to-paid ratio**, calendar-year paid ULAE over calendar-year paid claims, and assumes the two move together.

In a book that is growing quickly, they do not. Much of the ULAE spend is incurred as claims are *reported and set up* — staffing, systems and intake for the new states — so it rises immediately with the new volume. Paid claims lag: the claims from the new states have barely begun to settle, so the denominator still reflects the smaller, older book. The ratio is therefore distorted upward, and applying it to unpaid claims overstates the ULAE reserve.

**Kittel's refinement** corrects this by replacing the denominator with the *average of paid and incurred claims*:

$$R_{\text{Kittel}} = \frac{\text{Paid ULAE}}{0.5(\text{Paid claims}) + 0.5(\text{Incurred claims})}$$

Incurred claims recognize the new states' claims as they are reported, at the same time the ULAE is being spent, so the numerator and denominator grow together and the ratio stabilizes.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge of weaknesses in the classical method of ULAE analysis and recommend an alternative technique that accounts for the deficiency in the classical technique in each of three given situations common to an insurer's operations.

As noted above, responses that provided appropriate alternatives to the loss development technique were also given credit.

Candidates were expected to diagnose why the classical method of ULAE analysis may provide a poor result when applied to a company writing business in two new states, then provide an alternative method of analysis that corrects for the deficiency in the classical method. Credit was given for candidates who assumed the "classical method" referred to the loss development method.

Common errors included:

- Providing an alternative method without a supporting explanation as to why the alternative was appropriate.
- Suggesting Mango-Allen as an alternate technique, as the issue with a growing book of business is not instability in the paid amounts, but rather the assumption that paid and reported amounts are equivalent.

## Part b (0.5 points)

A homeowners insurer located in a hurricane prone area.

### Explanation
A hurricane drives an enormous spike in calendar-year paid claims that ULAE does not match. Catastrophe claims are large in dollars but are not proportionately expensive to administer in unallocated terms, and much of the response cost is allocated or outsourced. The paid-to-paid ratio is therefore artificially **low** in a catastrophe year and **high** in the quiet years in between — the ratio swings with the weather rather than measuring the cost of handling a claim.

The **Mango-Allen bootstrap** addresses exactly this. Instead of dividing by volatile actual calendar-year paid claims, it divides by *expected* paid claims — accident year ultimate estimates run through a payment pattern — so the denominator is smoothed and a single catastrophe no longer dictates the ratio.

A count-based approach, which relates ULAE to claim counts rather than claim dollars, is also defensible: a hurricane's distortion is largely one of severity, and counts are far less volatile than dollars.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to diagnose why the classical method of ULAE analysis may provide a poor result when applied to a company writing business in a catastrophe-prone area, then provide an alternative method of analysis that corrects for the deficiency in the classical method. Credit was given for candidates who assumed the "classical method" referred to the loss development method.

For candidates that answered with an alternate ULAE method, responses that involved separating catastrophe from non-catastrophe ULAE did not receive credit for an alternate approach. ULAE from different types of claims is generally difficult to separate, requiring some significant assumptions. This approach was accepted for candidates who assumed the question referred to the loss development method, as catastrophe and non-catastrophe claim payments can be separated much more readily than ULAE payments.

For candidates that assumed the question related to the loss development method, responses involving catastrophe modeling or average catastrophe loads did not receive credit for an alternative approach, as a company would not reserve for an "average" catastrophe in years when a catastrophe did not occur.

Common errors included:

- Selecting alternate methods that did not appropriately account for the volatility in the underlying loss payments. The Kittel Refinement and the Generalized approach both rely on ratios of paid ULAE to paid claims that are volatile in a catastrophe-prone area.

## Part c (0.5 points)

An insurer writing a long-tail line of business.

### Explanation
The classical technique's reserve formula rests on the **50/50 assumption** — that half of a claim's ULAE is spent when it is reported and half when it is settled — which is what produces

$$\text{ULAE reserve} = R \times \left(0.5 \times \text{Case outstanding} + 1.0 \times \text{IBNR}\right)$$

On a long-tail line that assumption fails. Claims stay open for years and consume ongoing **maintenance** cost — diary reviews, litigation management, periodic payments — so far more than half the ULAE is spent after the claim is opened. Loading only 50% onto reported-but-unpaid claims understates the reserve.

The **Conger-Nolibos generalized approach** drops the 50/50 split entirely: ULAE is attributed to the events that generate it — opening, maintaining and closing a claim — with separately selected weights, and each weight is applied to the corresponding unpaid dollars. That flexibility is what a long-tail line needs, because it lets the maintenance share be set to whatever the company's own claim data supports.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to diagnose why the classical method of ULAE analysis may provide a poor result when applied to a company writing a long-tailed line of business, then provide an alternative method of analysis that corrects for the deficiency in the classical method. Credit was given for candidates who assumed the "classical method" referred to the loss development method.

A common mistake was attributing the deficiencies of the ULAE classical method to difficulties in establishing the IBNR for the losses themselves.
