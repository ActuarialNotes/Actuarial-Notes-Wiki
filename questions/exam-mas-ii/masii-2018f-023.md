A linear regression is fit with the model below:

$$
\begin{align*}
y_i &\sim \mathit{Normal}(\mu_i,\ \sigma) \\
\mu_i &= b_0 + b_1 x_{1i} + b_2 x_{2i} \\
b_0 &\sim \mathit{Normal}(10,\ 50) \\
b_1 &\sim \mathit{Normal}(2,\ 10) \\
b_2 &\sim \mathit{Normal}(2,\ 10) \\
\sigma &\sim \mathit{Uniform}(0,\ 10)
\end{align*}
$$

It is known that $X_1$ and $X_2$ are both highly correlated with $Y$.

Three posterior plots of $b_1$ and $b_2$ are provided below.

![Three stacked posterior plots. Plot I, "Marginal Posterior Estimates and 95% Posterior Intervals": b1 has a point estimate of about 1.2 with an interval from about -0.5 to 2.9, and b2 has a point estimate of about 0.8 with an interval from about -0.7 to 2.5, on a Value axis from -1 to 3. Plot II, "Joint Posterior Density": posterior draws of b1 (vertical axis, -1 to 4) against b2 (horizontal axis, -2 to 3) form a thin straight band sloping downward from about (b2 = -1.5, b1 = 3.5) to about (b2 = 3, b1 = -0.8). Plot III, "Posterior Density of b1 + b2": a narrow bell-shaped density centred at about 2.0, running from about 1.8 to 2.2, with peak density about 6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ActuarialNotes/Actuarial-Notes-Wiki/main/Media/Attachments/masii-2018f-q23-posterior-plots.png)

Determine the best interpretation regarding multicollinearity from these plots.

- A) It is present in the data because $x_1$ and $x_2$ are highly positively correlated
- B) It is present in the data because $x_1$ and $x_2$ are highly negatively correlated
- C) It is present in the data because $b_1$ and $b_2$ are highly positively correlated
- D) It is present in the data because $x_1$ and $x_2$ have low correlation with each other
- E) It is not present in the data

## Explanation

Read the three plots together.

- **Plot I**: both marginal 95% intervals are very wide (about $-0.5$ to $2.9$ for $b_1$ and $-0.7$ to $2.5$ for $b_2$) and both straddle zero — even though $X_1$ and $X_2$ are each strongly related to $Y$.
- **Plot II**: the joint posterior is a thin ridge with a **negative** slope. Whenever $b_1$ is high, $b_2$ is low.
- **Plot III**: the sum $b_1 + b_2$ is pinned down tightly, between about 1.8 and 2.2.

This is the signature of multicollinearity: the data determine the combined effect of the two predictors precisely, but cannot split it between them. If $x_1 \approx x_2$, then

$$
\mu_i \approx b_0 + (b_1 + b_2)\,x_{1i}
$$

so only the sum is identified. Any trade of $b_1$ against $b_2$ that keeps the sum near 2 fits equally well, which is what makes the two coefficients negatively correlated in the posterior. A negative ridge between the **coefficients** therefore comes from a strong positive correlation between the **predictors**.

- **(B)** would give the opposite picture: with $x_1 \approx -x_2$, only the difference $b_1 - b_2$ is identified, and the posterior ridge slopes upward.
- **(C)** misreads Plot II, which slopes down, and in any case the correlation of the coefficients is a symptom in the posterior, not the property of the data.
- **(D) and (E)** contradict the ridge and the wide marginals.

The answer is **A**.
