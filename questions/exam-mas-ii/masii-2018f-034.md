The Metropolis algorithm is used to estimate the posterior distribution of a parameter $\theta$, which represents the probability of a flipped coin landing on heads.

Assume the following:

- The prior for $\theta$ is beta(1, 1)
- Two heads were observed in five flips
- Each proposed step in the Metropolis algorithm is the sum of the current position of the sampler and a draw from the proposal distribution

Three proposal distributions for the Metropolis algorithm are considered.

- I. beta(2, 5)
- II. uniform(0, 1)
- III. uniform(current $\theta$ - 0.1, current $\theta$ + 0.1)

Determine which of the proposal distributions above are valid for this analysis.

- A) I only
- B) II only
- C) III only
- D) I, II and III
- E) The answer is not given by (A), (B), (C), or (D)

## Explanation

The Metropolis algorithm needs a **symmetric** proposal: jumping from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ must be exactly as likely as jumping back from $\theta'$ to $\theta$. That is what lets the acceptance probability be the plain ratio of posterior densities, $\min\left(1,\ p(\theta' \mid y)/p(\theta \mid y)\right)$. Here each proposed position is the current position plus a draw from the proposal distribution.

**I is not valid.** A beta(2, 5) draw always lies in $(0, 1)$, so every proposal is a step *upward*. The sampler can never move to a smaller $\theta$, the proposal is not symmetric, and the chain drifts to the top of $[0, 1]$ instead of exploring the posterior.

**II is not valid.** A uniform(0, 1) draw is also always positive, which gives the same one-directional drift.

**III is valid.** A uniform jump of up to 0.1 either side of the current $\theta$ is the standard symmetric random-walk proposal: a move of $+d$ is exactly as likely as a move of $-d$. A proposal that falls outside $[0, 1]$ has posterior density zero and is simply rejected. The chain then settles on the posterior, which is beta(3, 4) here: the beta(1, 1) prior updated with 2 heads and 3 tails.

Only III, so the answer is **C**.

*Note: the CAS answer key accepted both **C** and **E** for this question.* E was also credited because, read literally, III adds a draw from uniform(current $\theta$ - 0.1, current $\theta$ + 0.1) to the current position. That would give a proposal of $2\theta + U(-0.1, 0.1)$, which is not centred on the current $\theta$, so none of the three would be valid.
