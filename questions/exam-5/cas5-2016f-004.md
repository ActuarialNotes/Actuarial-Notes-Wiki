Given the following information:

| Accident Year | Frequency | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 0.100 | \$25,000 |
| 2012 | 0.090 | \$27,250 |
| 2013 | 0.081 | \$30,248 |
| 2014 | 0.082 | \$33,423 |
| 2015 | 0.080 | \$36,599 |

| Accident Year | Ultimate Losses (\$000) |
|---|---|
| 2013 | 48,000 |
| 2014 | 55,000 |
| 2015 | 60,000 |

- Exposures are constant.
- The company only writes semi-annual policies.
- The rate filing will be effective on January 1, 2017.
- Rates will be in effect for one year.

## Part a (2.5 points)

Calculate the average annual trended ultimate losses that should be used to determine the indicated rate change. Briefly justify the frequency trend and severity trend selections.

### Answer
\$72,341 thousand (\$72,341,000)

### Explanation
**Step 1 — year-over-year changes.**

| Change | Frequency | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| 2011 to 2012 | $0.090/0.100 = 0.900$, i.e. $-10.0\%$ | $27{,}250/25{,}000 = 1.090$, i.e. $+9.0\%$ |
| 2012 to 2013 | $0.081/0.090 = 0.900$, i.e. $-10.0\%$ | $30{,}248/27{,}250 = 1.110$, i.e. $+11.0\%$ |
| 2013 to 2014 | $0.082/0.081 = 1.012$, i.e. $+1.2\%$ | $33{,}423/30{,}248 = 1.105$, i.e. $+10.5\%$ |
| 2014 to 2015 | $0.080/0.082 = 0.976$, i.e. $-2.4\%$ | $36{,}599/33{,}423 = 1.095$, i.e. $+9.5\%$ |

**Step 2 — selections, with justification.**

*Frequency:* the series breaks in two. Two consecutive $-10.0\%$ declines through 2013, then near-flat movement of $+1.2\%$ and $-2.4\%$. The old regime is not representative of what will happen during the policy period, so select from the recent years only: the average of the 2013-to-2014 and 2014-to-2015 changes,

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1.2\% + (-2.4\%)\right) = -0.6\%$$

Selecting a flat $0\%$ on the grounds that recent frequency is stable is equally defensible; the calculation below uses $-0.6\%$.

*Severity:* the four changes are $9.0\%$, $11.0\%$, $10.5\%$ and $9.5\%$ — tightly clustered with no drift, so all four years are credible and the straight average is the natural selection:

$$\tfrac{1}{4}(9.0 + 11.0 + 10.5 + 9.5) = +10.0\%$$

**Step 3 — the trend period.** Rates are effective January 1, 2017 and in effect for one year, so the average written date of a policy under the new rates is **July 1, 2017**. Policies are semi-annual, so losses occur on average a quarter of a year after writing:

$$\text{Average accident date of the forecast period} = 7/1/2017 + 0.25\text{ yr} = \mathbf{10/1/2017}$$

The historical years are full accident years, with average accident date July 1 of each year. Trend lengths:

| Accident year | From | To | Years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 7/1/2013 | 10/1/2017 | 4.25 |
| 2014 | 7/1/2014 | 10/1/2017 | 3.25 |
| 2015 | 7/1/2015 | 10/1/2017 | 2.25 |

**Step 4 — apply the combined pure premium trend.** With exposures constant, losses trend at the product of the frequency and severity trends, $1.100 \times 0.994 = 1.0934$ per year:

| AY | Ultimate losses (\$000) | Trend factor | Trended (\$000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 48,000 | $1.0934^{4.25} = 1.4615$ | 70,154 |
| 2014 | 55,000 | $1.0934^{3.25} = 1.3367$ | 73,518 |
| 2015 | 60,000 | $1.0934^{2.25} = 1.2225$ | 73,351 |

**Step 5 — average.** The question asks for the average annual trended ultimate losses, and exposures are constant, so a straight average of the three years is appropriate:

$$\frac{70{,}154 + 73{,}518 + 73{,}351}{3} = \mathbf{72{,}341 \text{ (\$000)}}$$

Note how the trending works: 2013 is by far the smallest untrended year but ends up close to the other two, which is the point of the exercise — once the years are put on a common cost level they should be broadly comparable, and averaging then dampens the random noise in any single year.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute frequency and severity trends from the historical series, make and *justify* selections, determine the correct trending period from the effective date and the semi-annual policy term, apply the selected trends to each ultimate loss year, and average the results.

Common errors included miscalculating the trend period — most often forgetting the quarter-year shift the semi-annual term produces in the average accident date; making selections with no justification, which the question explicitly asked for; and stopping at the three trended years without computing the average.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Discussions with the underwriting team reveal that changes in underwriting guidelines in the 2012 policy year resulted in lower claim counts. Describe how this information may change the estimate in part a. above without performing any additional calculations.

### Explanation
The information explains the frequency series rather than adding to it. The two $-10\%$ drops through 2013 are now attributable to a one-time tightening of underwriting guidelines in policy year 2012, not to an ongoing downward frequency trend. A one-time level shift should not be projected forward — once the new guidelines are fully reflected in the experience, frequency stops falling for that reason.

Because the selection in part a already came from the post-change years 2013 to 2015, the effect is to **confirm** that selection rather than change it: the answer is unchanged, and can now be defended on causal grounds rather than on the shape of the data alone.

If the part a selection had instead been drawn from all four year-over-year changes, it would have embedded the guideline shift as a permanent trend and been badly too negative. Correcting it would **raise** the selected frequency trend, raise the pure premium trend, and therefore **increase** the trended ultimate losses and the indicated rate change.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that an underwriting-guideline change producing lower claim counts is a one-time shift rather than a trend, and to say how that affects the frequency trend selection and, through it, the trended ultimate losses. Full credit required tying the response back to the specific selection made in part a and stating the direction of the impact on trended ultimate loss.

Common errors included discussing the guideline change in the abstract without relating it to the part a selection, and stating the effect on the frequency trend but never carrying it through to the trended ultimate losses.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Discussions with the underwriting team reveal that the company has been writing fewer high deductible policies, starting in policy year 2014. Fully describe how this information may change the estimate in part a. above without performing any additional calculations.

### Explanation
A shift away from high deductible policies changes the mix of business, and it pulls frequency and severity in opposite directions.

**Frequency rises.** A high deductible suppresses reported claim counts, because losses below the deductible are never presented to the insurer. Writing fewer such policies means more small claims are now reported, so observed frequency increases.

**Severity falls.** Those newly reported small claims are exactly the ones that used to be eliminated by the deductible. A high deductible book has an artificially high average severity precisely because its nuisance claims are screened out; adding them back drags the average down.

**Net effect on the estimate.** If the mix shift continues into the policy period, the selected **frequency trend should be increased** and the selected **severity trend decreased**. Those two adjustments partially offset, but they do not cancel — a book with lower deductibles carries more total loss dollars per exposure than the same book at high deductibles, so the pure premium trend rises on balance and the **trended ultimate losses in part a increase**, raising the indicated rate change.

A caveat worth stating: this is a distortion in the *historical* data as well as a forward-looking change. If instead the mix is expected to stabilize at the new level, the correct response is to restate the historical years to a common deductible mix rather than to build the shift into the trend.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to work out the effect of a shift away from high deductible policies on frequency and on severity separately, and then to state the combined effect on the trended ultimate losses.

A common error was analyzing the frequency and severity impacts correctly but never addressing how the change flows through to the trended ultimate loss estimate the question asked about.
