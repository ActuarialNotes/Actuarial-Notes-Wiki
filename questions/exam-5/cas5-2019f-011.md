The graph below shows frequency model results for industry types. The same data and assumptions are used in both models.

**Model Results** — bars show the number of exposures by industry type (left axis, 0 to 250,000); two lines show the estimated frequency relativity (right axis, "Factor", 0 to 1.6): a dotted line with round markers for the **One-Way Result**, and a dashed line with square markers for the **Generalized Linear Model Result**.

Reading the chart approximately:

| Industry Type | Exposures | One-Way Result | GLM Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~75,000 | ~0.50 | ~0.35 |
| 2 | ~85,000 | ~0.60 | ~0.40 |
| 3 | ~90,000 | ~0.80 | ~0.65 |
| 4 | ~128,000 | ~0.90 | ~0.82 |
| 5 | ~230,000 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 6 | ~217,000 | ~1.08 | ~1.12 |
| 7 | ~113,000 | ~1.10 | ~1.20 |

Industry type 5 carries the most exposures, and the two lines coincide there. Below industry 5 the GLM factors sit below the one-way factors; above it they sit above.

## Part a (0.25 points)

Briefly discuss why the relativity for both line graphs match for industry 5.

### Explanation
Industry type 5 is the **base level** for the industry type variable, so both models set its relativity to 1.00 by construction. Relativities are expressed relative to the base, so whatever else the two models disagree about, they must agree at the base level.

Industry 5 was chosen as the base because it has the largest number of exposures, which makes it the most stable and credible level to measure the other levels against — but it is being the *base*, not merely being the largest, that forces the two factors to be equal.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the differences between GLM and one-way models and explain how using less sophisticated models could produce inaccurate pricing which could lead to adverse selection and ultimately deteriorating profitability.

Candidates were expected to know that both the one-way model and the GLM set Industry 5 as the base level.

Common mistakes Included:

- Not indicating that Industry 5 was set as the base level.
- Only stating that the industry 5 had the most exposures and therefore the most credibility, but not indicating it was the base level.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Explain why the models in part a. above produce different results.

### Explanation
The two models differ in how they handle the **other rating variables**.

A **one-way (univariate) analysis** measures each level of industry type using only the raw experience of the risks in that level. It therefore attributes to industry type any differences that are really caused by other variables. If the distribution of some other rating characteristic is not the same across industry types — say, the low-numbered industries happen to contain a disproportionate share of risks that are low-risk on some other dimension — the one-way result silently folds that other effect into the industry relativity. This is **exposure correlation**, or distributional bias.

A **GLM** estimates all variables simultaneously. Each variable's coefficients are fitted holding the others constant, so the industry type relativity reflects the effect of industry type *alone*, with the effects of the correlated variables stripped out and assigned where they belong. The GLM can also capture interactions between variables, which a one-way analysis cannot represent at all.

The systematic pattern in the graph is what exposure correlation looks like: the two curves agree only at the base, and diverge further the further a level sits from it, because the one-way result is double-counting a correlated effect that the GLM has removed.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain the main differences between one-way and GLMs, i.e. that GLMs consider all variables and account for exposure correlation and variable interactions; and the one-way models do not.

A common mistake was stating that the results were different because of exposure correlations, but not explaining how the models treat them differently.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Describe how the use of one-way results might impact profitability for an insurance company.

### Explanation
Taking the GLM as the more accurate estimate of expected loss, the one-way relativities misprice both ends of the industry-type scale — and in opposite directions, which is what makes the effect corrosive rather than self-cancelling.

- For industries **below** the base (1 through 4) the one-way factor is **higher** than the GLM factor, so those risks are **overcharged**. Competitors pricing with a multivariate model will quote them more accurately and win them, and the insurer loses that business — both new business it fails to write and renewals it fails to retain.
- For industries **above** the base (6 through 8) the one-way factor is **lower** than the GLM factor, so those risks are **undercharged**. They are attractively priced relative to the market, so the insurer writes more of them than expected — at rates that do not cover their expected losses.

The insurer is therefore **adversely selected against on both sides**: it sheds the business it prices too high and attracts the business it prices too low. The mix shifts toward the underpriced segments, the overall loss ratio deteriorates, and profitability declines over time even though the *average* rate level may have been adequate when the rates were filed.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand that the one-way model would produce less accurate results which would lead to adverse selection and deteriorating profitability.

Common mistakes included:

- Failing to explain that inaccurate pricing could lead to adverse selection.
- Only stating that it would result in overcharging/undercharging risks without explaining how it would impact the profitability of company.
- Failing to state that it would negatively impact profitability.
- Indicating that one-way is always higher or lower than GLM.
- Comparing one-way analysis to not doing anything at all rather than comparing to the GLM.
