Given the following:

| Stock | Current Price (time=0) | Investors' Forecast Price (time=1) | Beta | Covariance with Market Portfolio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 80 | 85 | 0.2 | Not given |
| B | 60 | 63 | Not given | 0.03 |

- The market risk premium is 6%.
- The market variance is 5%.
- The risk-free rate is 4%.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Explain which stock is the superior investment strategy according to the capital asset pricing model (CAPM).

### Explanation
CAPM expected returns, with $R_M$ the 6% market risk premium and B's beta its covariance with the market over the market variance:

$$E(A) = r_f + \beta(R_M) = 0.04 + 0.2(0.06) = 0.052$$

$$E(B) = r_f + \beta(R_M) = r_f + \frac{\text{Covariance}}{\text{Market variance}}(R_M) = 0.04 + \frac{0.03}{0.05}(0.06) = 0.076$$

Alphas, from the forecast holding-period returns:

$$\alpha(A) = \left(\frac{85}{80} - 1\right) - 0.052 = 0.0105$$

$$\alpha(B) = \left(\frac{63}{60} - 1\right) - 0.076 = -.026$$

A is the better buy if short sales are not allowed, but B is the better strategy if short sales are allowed, as the absolute value of its alpha is greater than that of A.

Alternatively:

Compare prices instead of returns. With the same CAPM expected returns, the CAPM price of A at time 1 is $80(1 + 0.052) = 84.16 < 85$ and of B is $60(1 + 0.076) = 64.56 > 63$. (Equivalently, at time 0: $85/(1 + 0.052) = 80.80 > 80$ and $63/(1 + 0.076) = 58.55 < 60$.) A is underpriced, so it is the better buy if short sales are not allowed, but B is the better strategy if short sales are allowed, as the absolute difference between its forecast price and its expected price is greater than that of A.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the CAPM expected return calculation and evaluate the superior investment strategy according to CAPM.
A common error was using the market return risk premium as the market return

## Part b (1 point)

Compare and contrast CAPM and the single-index model with respect to the optimal risky portfolio.

### Explanation
Under CAPM, all investors apply the Markowitz optimization process to a common set of investable assets and input list (expected returns and covariance matrix). The optimal risky portfolio that all investors hold will, by definition, be the market portfolio.

According to the single-index model, when forming a portfolio, investors will relentlessly pursue positive-alpha stocks, or take short positions in negative-alpha stocks, to increase the risk premium. Consequently, prices of positive-alpha stocks will rise and prices of negative-alpha stocks will fall. This will continue until all alpha values are driven to zero. At this point, investors will be content to minimize risk by completely eliminating unique risk, that is, by holding the broadest possible portfolio, the market portfolio. When all stocks have zero alphas, the market portfolio will be the optimal risky portfolio.

So both CAPM and the single-index model suggest that the market portfolio is the optimal risky portfolio, but do so from different starting assumptions.

Alternatively:

Under CAPM, the optimal risky portfolio is the market portfolio. Under the single-index model, investors search for stocks with non-zero alphas and create a portfolio of passive (market) and active (non-zero-alpha) investments. In this process, investors will bring the alphas to zero, and the optimal portfolio will also be the market portfolio.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the differences between CAPM and the single-index model and how they would result in differences in the optimal risky portfolios.

Common errors include:

- Incorrectly stating that CAPM optimal risk portfolio leads to Market index (incorrect, leads to Market Portfolio)
- Incorrectly stating that the single index model optimal risk portfolio is an index
- Not explaining how both models will lead to the market portfolio as the optimal risky portfolio
- Including true statements that do not directly tie to the question, such as:
    - CAPM investors all are mean-variance investors with the same input list (failing to specify that this results in the Market portfolio being the optimal portfolio)
    - CAPM has more variables, leading to more parameter estimation risk
    - CAPM looks at expected return, but single-index model looks at actual return
    - CAPM assumes an unobservable market
