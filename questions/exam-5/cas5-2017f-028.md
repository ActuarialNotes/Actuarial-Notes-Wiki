Given the following ultimate claim ratio estimates based on reported claims as of December 31, 2016:

| Accident Year | Development Technique | Bornhuetter-Ferguson Technique | Frequency-Severity Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 61.9% | 61.9% | 61.9% |
| 2013 | 61.6% | 60.3% | 61.2% |
| 2014 | 61.8% | 57.1% | 61.7% |
| 2015 | 61.5% | 52.4% | 61.8% |
| 2016 | 72.4% | 50.7% | 61.7% |

- The initial expected loss ratio used in the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique is the same in all accident years.
- There has been no change to the mix of business written by the company.
- The company has not experienced any unusually large losses.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Assess the selected claim development factors used in the techniques.

### Explanation
Read the table by **column against column**, one accident year at a time. Because the mix of business has not changed and there are no unusually large losses, the true underlying claim ratio should be roughly level across years — and the frequency-severity technique, which does not depend on the reported development factors, confirms that it is: 61.2% to 61.9% in every year.

For accident years 2012 through 2015 the development technique agrees closely with frequency-severity (61.5% to 61.9%), so the selected development factors at 24 months and beyond appear reasonable.

Accident year 2016 is the exception. At 12 months it comes in at **72.4%**, about 10 points above every other year and above what both other techniques produce for the same year. Accident year 2016's development estimate is the only one that relies on the **12-24 month development factor**, so that factor is where the anomaly must sit: it is too high, and being the most leveraged factor in the pattern it amplifies a small amount of reported claims into a badly overstated ultimate. The 12-24 factor should be re-examined and lowered; the remaining selected factors look appropriate.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that the 12-24 month development factor was high relative to the other methods and years, by comparing the ultimate claim ratios produced by the various techniques.

Common errors included attributing the high 2016 ultimate to case reserve strengthening; not mentioning development factors at all; comparing the method ultimates without connecting them back to the factors; claiming that the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique used different development factors than the development technique; and assessing the reserving techniques generally rather than the development factors the question asked about.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Assess the initial expected loss ratio used in the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

### Explanation
The Bornhuetter-Ferguson estimate is a credibility weighting of the development estimate and the expected claims, with the weight on expected claims equal to the percent unreported:

$$\text{Ultimate}_{BF} = \frac{1}{\text{CDF}} \times \text{Ultimate}_{Dev} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}}\right) \times \text{Expected claims}$$

At mature ages $1/\text{CDF} \approx 1$, so Bornhuetter-Ferguson collapses onto the development estimate; at immature ages the initial expected loss ratio dominates. That is exactly the pattern in the table: Bornhuetter-Ferguson equals the development technique at 2012 (61.9%), then peels away further with every less mature year — 60.3%, 57.1%, 52.4%, 50.7%.

A one-directional drift like that is the signature of an **initial expected loss ratio that is too low**. The other two techniques put the true claim ratio around 61-62% in every year, including 2016, so as more weight shifts to the a priori assumption the Bornhuetter-Ferguson result is pulled steadily *away* from a stable reality rather than toward it. Nothing in the given facts — mix of business unchanged, no large losses — offers an alternative explanation for recent years genuinely running 10 points better.

The initial expected loss ratio should be raised toward the ~62% indicated by the mature years and by the frequency-severity technique.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that the initial expected loss ratio was too low, either by comparing the Bornhuetter-Ferguson results to the other techniques or by observing that the Bornhuetter-Ferguson ultimates decline as more weight is placed on the expected loss ratio.

Common mistakes included concluding the expected loss ratio was too high, and discussing the development factors used within the Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique rather than the expected loss ratio.
