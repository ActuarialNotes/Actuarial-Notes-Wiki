Given the following information as of December 31, 2016:

**Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 18,000 | 19,800 |
| 2014 | 11,000 | 16,500 | 19,800 | |
| 2015 | 12,650 | 18,975 | | |
| 2016 | 14,500 | | | |

**Cumulative Paid Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 4,000 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 18,000 |
| 2014 | 4,400 | 11,000 | 16,000 | |
| 2015 | 4,840 | 12,100 | | |
| 2016 | 5,324 | | | |

**Reported Claims Development Technique Ultimate Claims (\$000)**

| Accident Year | Ultimate Claims (\$000) |
|---|---|
| 2013 | 20,790 |
| 2014 | 22,869 |

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the ultimate claims for accident years 2015 and 2016 as of December 31, 2016, using the reported claims development technique.

### Answer
AY 2015: \$26,299 thousand; AY 2016: \$30,146 thousand

### Explanation
**Step 1 — age-to-age factors on the reported triangle.** Every diagonal gives the same factor, so the selection is unambiguous:

| AY | 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | $15{,}000/10{,}000 = 1.500$ | $18{,}000/15{,}000 = 1.200$ | $19{,}800/18{,}000 = 1.100$ |
| 2014 | $16{,}500/11{,}000 = 1.500$ | $19{,}800/16{,}500 = 1.200$ | |
| 2015 | $18{,}975/12{,}650 = 1.500$ | | |
| **Selected** | **1.500** | **1.200** | **1.100** |

**Step 2 — back out the tail from the given ultimates.** This is the point of supplying the 2013 and 2014 ultimates. Accident year 2013 is the only year at age 48, so

$$\text{Tail}_{48\text{-ult}} = \frac{20{,}790}{19{,}800} = 1.050$$

Check it against 2014, which is at age 36: $CDF_{36} = 1.100 \times 1.050 = 1.155$ and $19{,}800 \times 1.155 = 22{,}869$ — exactly the given ultimate, confirming the tail.

**Step 3 — cumulative development factors.**

$$CDF_{36} = 1.100 \times 1.050 = 1.155$$
$$CDF_{24} = 1.200 \times 1.155 = 1.386$$
$$CDF_{12} = 1.500 \times 1.386 = 2.079$$

**Step 4 — apply to the latest diagonal of *reported* claims.**

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2015} = 18{,}975 \times 1.386 = \$26{,}299 \text{ thousand}$$
$$\text{Ultimate}_{2016} = 14{,}500 \times 2.079 = \$30{,}146 \text{ thousand}$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how to develop ultimate losses using triangles, how triangles can be used as a means to identify internal/operational changes, and how underreserving could impact different aspects of a company.

Candidates were expected to know how to calculate ultimate losses for 2015 and 2016 based on reported losses triangles.

Common errors included:

- Applying LDFs to paid losses to calculate ultimate losses
- Not including a tail factor (some assumed tail factor to be 1)

## Part b (0.5 points)

Produce a diagnostic that shows an operational change in the insurer's history. Briefly describe a scenario that could result in the observed diagnostic.

### Explanation
The natural diagnostic here is the **ratio of cumulative paid to cumulative reported claims**, read *down* each column (same maturity, successive accident years):

| AY | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 0.400 | 0.667 | 0.833 | 0.909 |
| 2014 | 0.400 | 0.667 | 0.808 | |
| 2015 | 0.383 | 0.638 | | |
| 2016 | 0.367 | | | |

The ratio is flat for 2013 and 2014 and then falls in 2015 and again in 2016 at every maturity available — 0.400 to 0.383 to 0.367 at 12 months, 0.667 to 0.638 at 24 months, 0.833 to 0.808 at 36 months. Something changed operationally beginning with accident year 2015.

**A scenario producing it:** the company **strengthened its case reserves** starting in 2015 — a tightening of case-setting guidelines, a move to more conservative initial reserves, or a re-review of open files. Case adequacy rises, so reported claims (paid plus case) grow relative to paid claims at the same maturity and the paid-to-reported ratio falls, even though nothing about the underlying claims has changed.

The alternative consistent story is a **slowdown in the settlement rate** — claims taking longer to close, so less is paid at each maturity while the case reserves remain in place. Either scenario is acceptable, but it must be the one that matches the direction of the ratio: a *weakening* of case reserve strength would push the paid-to-reported ratio *up*, not down.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to produce a triangle of paid/reported ratio, to identify the lowering ratios, and to understand why such a situation could happen.

Common errors included:

- Producing the right diagnosis (lower settlement rate), but providing a wrong scenario (weakening case reserve strength)
- Producing a case reserve triangle to show reserve strengthening

## Part c (0.75 points)

Briefly describe an issue that could arise for each of the following parties that relies on accurate unpaid claims estimates if unpaid claims are understated by the insurer.

i. Investors

ii. Regulators

iii. Internal management

### Explanation
Understating unpaid claims understates incurred losses, which overstates income, surplus and apparent profitability. Each party is then misled in a different way:

**i. Investors.** Reported earnings and book value look better than they are, so investors buy or hold the stock at a price the true results do not support. When the deficiency emerges and reserves are strengthened, earnings are hit and the investment loses value — investors will have made a capital allocation decision on financial statements that were wrong.

**ii. Regulators.** The insurer's surplus and its solvency ratios look stronger than they are, so the regulator does not identify the company as financially troubled and takes no supervisory action at a point where intervention could still help. Understated reserves can also make the business look more profitable than it is, which may lead the regulator to press for rate decreases or to deny rate increases the company actually needs.

**iii. Internal management.** Management prices and plans off its own indicated loss costs. Understated reserves make the book look adequately or over-priced, so rate increases are not taken, unprofitable segments are expanded rather than corrected, and incentive compensation and expense budgets are set on profits that do not exist. Reinsurance purchasing and capital planning are similarly built on an understated liability.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate consequences of under-reserving on people in different roles.

Common errors included:

- Confusing regulators with credit agencies, and provided answers that the regulators would "downgrade", "de-grade" the company
- Providing answers that were logically wrong (e.g. investment return looked better than it actually is so investors might leave)
- Providing answers that were vague (e.g. investors will be unhappy)
- Discussing the importance of having appropriate reserve estimates as opposed to the issues of having understated reserves
