The CIA's March 2024 paper on **relying on work the actuary did not do**, in a P&C pricing context. Read for [[Exam 6C (CAS)|Exam 6C]] objective A1.

## Contents

- **1. Introduction** — *1.1 Purpose*, *1.2 Application*. Pricing work now routinely incorporates vendor models, third-party data, catastrophe models and the output of other teams; the actuary signing the work still owns it.
- **2. Standards of practice** — what the [[Standards of Practice|Consolidated Standards of Practice]] already require when an actuary uses another's work: the actuary must either take responsibility for it or disclose the reliance, and cannot do both halfway.
- **3. Other considerations**
  - *3.1 [[Materiality|Materiality]]* — the depth of review scales with how much the borrowed input could move the answer.
  - *3.2 Understanding the environment* — knowing what the input was built for, and whether this use is the one it was built for.
  - *3.3 Review and validation* — reasonableness checks, benchmarking, and sensitivity testing the result to the borrowed assumption.
  - *3.4 Documentation and reporting* — recording what was relied on, from whom, and what review was performed.
- **4. Applications in advanced modelling techniques** — machine-learning models and external data feeds, where the actuary may not be able to inspect the mechanism and must rely on behaviour instead.
- **5. Conclusion**

## Why it is on the syllabus

Reliance is the professional-responsibility half of the modelling material. [[CIA Models]] governs the model itself and [[FSRA Risk Management]] the operational framework around it; this paper governs the actuary's position when the model, the data, or the parameters came from somebody else. The same reliance question recurs on the financial-reporting side of the syllabus — see [[OSFI Memorandum]] §4.12 and [[CIA Appointed Actuary]] §2 on the [[Appointed Actuary|appointed actuary]] taking responsibility for items set by others.
