Whether a football player is injury-free in any one season is independent of whether the player is injury-free in any other seasons. The player's probability of being injury-free for a season is the same for each season. Let $x$ represent the player's probability of being injury-free for exactly one of the next two seasons.

Determine the player's probability of being injury-free for exactly two of the next four seasons.

- A) $0.5x$
- B) $2x$
- C) $x^2$
- D) $1.5x^2$
- E) $3x^2$

## Explanation

Let $p$ be the per-season probability of being injury-free. Both quantities are binomial.

**Exactly one of two seasons:**

$$
x = \binom{2}{1}p(1-p) = 2p(1-p)
$$

**Exactly two of four seasons:**

$$
\binom{4}{2}p^2(1-p)^2 = 6p^2(1-p)^2
$$

The second expression contains the square of the first. Writing $6p^2(1-p)^2$ as a multiple of $\bigl[2p(1-p)\bigr]^2 = 4p^2(1-p)^2$:

$$
\begin{align*}
6p^2(1-p)^2 &= \frac{6}{4}\bigl[2p(1-p)\bigr]^2 \\
            &= \mathbf{1.5x^2}
\end{align*}
$$

The factor 1.5 is the ratio of binomial coefficients, $\binom{4}{2}/\binom{2}{1}^2 = 6/4$. Choice (C), $x^2$, comes from treating the four-season event as two independent copies of the two-season event, which gets the powers of $p$ right but miscounts the arrangements — there are 6 ways to place two injury-free seasons among four, not the 4 that squaring implies.
