**Mergers and Acquisitions** (M&A) are the means of external, or inorganic, growth, in which a firm combines with or buys another business instead of building the capability itself (internal, or organic, growth). An acquisition creates value for the buyer only when the target's stand-alone value plus the present value of the improvements the combination makes possible exceeds everything the buyer pays for it.

With $V_A$ and $V_T$ the stand-alone intrinsic values of the acquirer and the target, the value of the combined firm is

> $$V_{AT} = V_A + V_T + \text{PV(synergies)} - \text{Integration costs}$$

- $\text{PV(synergies)}$ is the present value of the cost savings and revenue gains that the combination makes possible and that neither firm could achieve alone.
- $\text{Integration costs}$ are the costs of combining the two firms' operations.

The value the deal creates for the acquirer is

> $$\Delta V_{\text{acquirer}} = V_T + \text{PV(synergies)} - \text{Integration costs} - P$$

- $P$ is the price paid.

The price paid is

> $$P = \text{MV}_T + \text{Premium}$$

- $\text{MV}_T$ is the target's market value.
- $\text{Premium}$ is the premium for control.

Transaction costs are added to the price when they are material. The target's shareholders gain the premium, $P - \text{MV}_T$.

> [!example]- How much value does this acquisition create, and how is it shared? {Example}
> An insurer bids for a competitor whose shares trade at $\$800$ million, which is also its stand-alone intrinsic value. Combining the two claims and policy administration operations is expected to produce after-tax cost savings with a present value of $\$300$ million, and the integration will cost $\$40$ million. The insurer pays a 30% premium in cash.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The price is $800 + 0.30 \times 800 = \$1{,}040$ million.
> > - The acquirer gains $800 + 300 - 40 - 1{,}040 = \$20$ million.
> > - The target's shareholders gain $1{,}040 - 800 = \$240$ million.
> > - The deal creates $\$260$ million in total, and the target's shareholders take $\$240$ million of it through the premium.
> > - If the savings came in at $\$250$ million instead of $\$300$ million, the acquirer would lose $\$30$ million.
> > - Sellers usually capture most of the value, and a small overestimate of synergy turns a modest gain into a loss.

## Mergers, acquisitions and takeovers

In Hitt, Ireland and Hoskisson's terms:

- A **merger** is two firms agreeing to integrate their operations on a relatively equal basis.
- An **acquisition** is one firm buying a controlling interest in another to make it a business in its portfolio.
- A **takeover** is an acquisition the target did not solicit.

Horizontal acquisitions buy a competitor, vertical acquisitions buy a supplier or a distributor, and related acquisitions buy a firm in a highly related industry.

## Organic and inorganic growth

Organic growth through new products, new markets and internal development avoids a premium and keeps control of the culture, but it is slow and its outcome is uncertain. An acquisition buys an established position and capabilities quickly, at the cost of the premium and the risk of integration. Cooperative strategies, such as alliances and joint ventures, sit between the two.

Hitt lists these reasons for acquiring:

1. Increased market power.
2. Overcoming barriers to entry.
3. Avoiding the cost and delay of developing new products.
4. Lower risk than internal development.
5. More [[Corporate-Level Strategy|diversification]].
6. Reshaping the firm's competitive scope.
7. Learning new capabilities.

Koller's value-creating archetypes include improving the target's performance, removing excess capacity from an industry, giving the target's products faster access to market, and buying skills or technology faster than they can be built.

## Problems in achieving success

Hitt lists these problems:

1. Integration difficulties.
2. Inadequate evaluation of the target.
3. Large or extraordinary debt.
4. An inability to achieve synergy.
5. Too much diversification.
6. Managers who are overly focused on acquisitions.
7. A firm that becomes too large.

Most failures come back to two of them: paying too much, and not delivering the synergies the price assumed. Cost synergies, such as combining claims, policy administration or investment operations, are usually more reliable than revenue synergies, which depend on customers' behaviour. Integration costs are spent up front, while synergies take time to arrive and are often smaller than planned. Synergies are counted after tax and net of integration costs. The target's share price may already include a takeover premium or expected synergies, and adding the synergies again counts them twice.

## Paying in cash or in stock

In a cash deal the acquirer's shareholders bear the whole risk that the synergies fall short. In a stock deal the target's shareholders become owners of the combined firm and share that risk. An increase in earnings per share says nothing about value. A deal paid in high-P/E stock is accretive even when it destroys value, and a value-creating deal can be dilutive at first.

> [!example]- What does this stock deal do to earnings per share and to value per share? {Example}
> An acquirer has 100 million shares at $\$20$, earning $\$100$ million, so its earnings per share are $\$1.00$ and its price-to-earnings ratio is 20. It buys a target earning $\$30$ million, valued at $\$360$ million (a P/E of 12), for a 25% premium paid in new shares. The deal has no synergies.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The price is $1.25 \times 360 = \$450$ million, which needs 22.5 million new shares at $\$20$.
> > - Earnings per share become $130/122.5 = \$1.0612$, up 6.1%.
> > - Value per share becomes $2{,}360/122.5 = \$19.265$, down $\$0.735$.
> > - The acquirer's shareholders lose $\$73.5$ million and the target's gain the same amount.
> > - EPS rose only because the acquirer used high-P/E stock to buy low-P/E earnings, and with no synergies the premium was paid for nothing.
> > - The target's shareholders now own 18.4% of the combined firm, so they bore part of the $\$90$ million premium themselves.

## Mergers and acquisitions in insurance

Insurers grow inorganically by buying other insurers, books of business and distribution, such as brokers and agencies. Buying a closed block competes with [[Reinsurance|reinsurance]] as a way to transfer a book, and [[Private Equity|private equity]] has become a frequent buyer. A divestiture is the same test in reverse: a business should be sold when another owner values it at more than its value to the firm.

> [!trap] Value created in total versus value created for the acquirer
> A deal can create value in total and still destroy value for the acquirer, when the premium exceeds the net synergies. In the first example the deal creates $\$260$ million, and the acquirer keeps $\$20$ million of it. State whose value the question asks about before answering.
