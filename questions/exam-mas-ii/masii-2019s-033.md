An MCMC sampling algorithm with 10,000 samples post warm-up is employed to estimate the posterior distribution of two parameters. The kernel density estimate of the posterior distribution of the correlation, $\rho$, between the two parameters is plotted below:

![Kernel density plot of rho over the range -1.0 to -0.55. The mass is concentrated in a large ragged peak near -0.93 that falls away unevenly with visible shoulders through -0.85 and -0.80, plus two small separate bumps near -0.72 and -0.59](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ActuarialNotes/Actuarial-Notes-Wiki/main/Media/Attachments/masii-2019s-q33-rho-density.png)

The effective number of samples from the sampling algorithm is 202 for the first parameter and 331 for the second parameter.

Determine the most likely MCMC sampling algorithm employed for this analysis.

- A) Gibbs
- B) Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
- C) Metropolis
- D) Metropolis-Hastings
- E) Quadratic Approximation

## Explanation

Two facts drive the choice: the parameters are **very strongly correlated** ($\rho$ concentrated near $-0.9$), and the sampler is **badly inefficient** — 202 and 331 effective samples out of 10,000 draws is roughly a 2-3% efficiency, and the ragged, lumpy density is what a chain with that much autocorrelation produces.

Working through the options:

- **(E) Quadratic approximation** is not an MCMC algorithm at all — it fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode, so there are no draws and no effective sample size to report.
- **(B) Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** uses gradient information to travel along the posterior's contours, which is precisely what makes it robust to strong correlation. HMC routinely returns effective sample sizes close to (and sometimes above) the number of draws — not 2%.
- **(C) Metropolis and (D) Metropolis-Hastings** propose a move in all dimensions at once, so a well-tuned proposal can step *along* a correlated ridge rather than across it.
- **(A) Gibbs** updates one parameter at a time from its full conditional, holding the others fixed. Every move is therefore axis-aligned. When two parameters are correlated at $-0.9$ the posterior is a narrow diagonal ridge, and an axis-aligned move can only take a tiny step before leaving the high-density region. The chain crawls up the ridge in small zig-zags, which is exactly the highly autocorrelated, low-effective-sample behaviour observed.

The answer is **A**.
