Given the following for an insurance company that writes only annual policies:

| Policy | Effective Date | Annual Premium |
|---|---|---|
| A | July 1, 2014 | \$200 |
| B | October 1, 2014 | \$240 |
| C | January 1, 2015 | \$260 |
| D | July 1, 2015 | \$280 |

- Policy D was cancelled March 31, 2016.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Calculate the following for calendar year 2015:

i. Earned premium

ii. Written premium

### Answer
i. $680; ii. $540

### Explanation
**i. Calendar year 2015 earned premium.** Calendar year earning counts whatever portion of each policy's term falls inside 2015, regardless of when the policy was written — so the two policies written in 2014 contribute their 2015 portions:

| Policy | Policy period | Months earned in 2015 | Earned |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 7/1/2014 – 6/30/2015 | 6 | $200 \times \tfrac{6}{12} = 100$ |
| B | 10/1/2014 – 9/30/2015 | 9 | $240 \times \tfrac{9}{12} = 180$ |
| C | 1/1/2015 – 12/31/2015 | 12 | $260 \times \tfrac{12}{12} = 260$ |
| D | 7/1/2015 – 6/30/2016 | 6 | $280 \times \tfrac{6}{12} = 140$ |

$$\text{CY2015 EP} = 100 + 180 + 260 + 140 = \$680$$

**ii. Calendar year 2015 written premium.** Written premium is assigned in full to the calendar year containing the effective date. Policies C and D incept in 2015; A and B incepted in 2014:

$$\text{CY2015 WP} = 260 + 280 = \$540$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate earned and written premium under calendar year aggregation. Common errors included calculating only one of the two correctly, and omitting policy A's $200 \times \tfrac{6}{12}$ contribution from the earned premium — a policy written in the prior year still earns into the current calendar year.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Calculate the following as of December 31, 2016:

i. Policy year 2015 earned premium

ii. Policy year 2015 written premium

### Answer
i. $470; ii. $470

### Explanation
Policy year 2015 comprises the policies **effective** during 2015 — policies C and D — and follows each to the end of its term rather than stopping at a calendar boundary.

Policy D was cancelled on March 31, 2016, so it was in force from 7/1/2015 to 3/31/2016, which is **9 months** of its 12-month term. The cancellation removes the unearned final three months from both the earned and the written premium:

$$D = 280 \times \tfrac{9}{12} = \$210$$

**i. Policy year 2015 earned premium.** Policy C is fully earned by 12/31/2015, and D has earned all it ever will:

$$260 + 210 = \$470$$

**ii. Policy year 2015 written premium.**

$$260 + 210 = \$470$$

The two are **equal**, and necessarily so: valued at December 31, 2016 every policy in the 2015 policy year has expired or been cancelled, so the policy year is fully developed and there is no unearned premium left. Written and earned premium converge at that point.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate earned and written premium under policy year aggregation. Common errors included miscounting the months policy D was in force before cancellation — for example using $280 \times \tfrac{10}{12} = 493$ rather than $\tfrac{9}{12}$; failing to recognize that written and earned premium must be equal for a fully developed policy year; and general miscalculation of the two figures.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Briefly describe one advantage and one disadvantage of calendar year data aggregation.

### Explanation
**Advantage — the data is final as soon as the year closes.** Calendar year premium and losses are fixed at December 31 and do not develop afterward, so there is nothing left to estimate and the figures are available immediately. They are also the same figures already compiled for the financial statements, so they reconcile directly to financial data and cost nothing extra to assemble for ratemaking.

**Disadvantage — premium and losses are mismatched.** The premium earned in a calendar year comes from policies written across two different years, while the losses recorded in that year include payments and reserve changes on claims from *earlier* accident years. The two sides therefore do not describe the same exposures, which distorts the loss ratio. The same fixity that makes the data convenient also means it cannot be developed, so calendar year data is unsuitable for estimating ultimate claims or IBNR.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate their understanding of the calendar year method by giving one advantage and one disadvantage.

Responses rejected as advantages included "easy to compare with losses"; "uses most recent data" or "easy to use" without further detail; claims that calendar year aggregation is uncommon so benchmarks do not apply; "development doesn't take as long as underwriting aggregation" — the data does not develop *faster*, the method simply ignores the development of business written during the year; and "there is no development beyond 12 months so no need to develop", which misstates the mechanism.

Responses rejected as disadvantages included "takes longer to become available"; "may not be an accurate reflection of the actual data" or "calendar year data does not develop" without explaining why that matters; the assertion that calendar year loss data is uncorrelated with exposures; and answers confusing earned, written and in-force policies.
