An auto insurer is evaluating the variable "number of vehicles" for inclusion in a rating plan. Given the following Generalized Linear Model (GLM) output:

*Chart 1 — indicated relativity by number of vehicles (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7+), plotted against exposures, with upper and lower standard error bounds and a reference line at 1.0.* The indicated relativity is close to 1.0 for 1–4 vehicles and rises to roughly 2.0 at 6–7+ vehicles, but the standard error bounds are wide and the 1.0 reference line falls inside the error range at nearly every level. Exposure volume is concentrated at 1–3 vehicles and is very thin at 5, 6 and 7+.

*Chart 2 — consistency test: indicated relativity by number of vehicles for 2015, 2016 and 2017 data separately.* The three years track each other closely for 1–4 vehicles but diverge sharply at 5, 6 and 7+, where the 2017 indication rises to about 2.5, the 2016 indication to about 2.1, and the 2015 indication falls back toward 1.4.

- Number of vehicles chi-square percentage: 10%

## Part a (1 point)

Fully justify whether number of vehicles should be included in the rating plan.

### Explanation

Apply the four standard tests of a variable's predictive quality:

- **Statistical test (chi-square / p-value):** the chi-square percentage is 10%, above the usual 5% significance threshold, so the variable **fails** — it is not statistically significant.
- **Main effect test (standard errors):** at nearly every level the 1.0 reference line lies inside the standard error bounds, so the indicated relativities are not statistically distinguishable from unity. The variable **fails**.
- **Consistency test:** the year-by-year indications track each other only for the first few levels; at 5, 6 and 7+ vehicles the three years diverge widely and even move in opposite directions. The variable **fails**.
- **Judgment:** the levels where the indication is large (5, 6, 7+) carry very little exposure, which explains the wide error bounds and inconsistency and gives no reason to override the tests.

**Conclusion: number of vehicles should not be included in the rating plan.** It fails all three quantitative tests, and there is no judgmental basis for including it anyway.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge and proper application of the tests used to analyze the predictive quality of a variable from GLM output — the main effect test, the consistency test, the statistical test, and judgment — and to state a clear decision on inclusion based on those results.

No credit was given where a candidate incorrectly stated or implied that the variable passed the main effect test or the consistency test, or where a recommendation was given with no justification. Common mistakes included not clearly indicating whether the variable passed or failed each test, and not clearly stating whether the variable should be used.

## Part b (0.75 point)

Briefly discuss three challenges associated with performing GLM analysis on loss ratio data.

### Explanation

- **Premium must be on-leveled.** The loss ratio's denominator reflects historical rate levels, so the premium has to be brought to current rate level before modelling — otherwise the model measures past rate changes rather than risk.
- **There is no natural error distribution for loss ratios.** Frequency has a Poisson-family distribution and severity a Gamma; loss ratio has no such default assumption, so the modeller has no obvious choice of distribution and link function.
- **The model becomes obsolete when rates change.** A model fitted on loss ratios is tied to the rate level in force during the experience period, so a subsequent rate change invalidates it — unlike a pure premium model, which is independent of the rating structure.

Also acceptable: actuaries have no a priori expectation for the shape of a loss ratio relationship (unlike frequency and severity), and loss ratios do not have clean trend factors the way frequency and severity do.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to list three challenges specific to using loss ratio data in a GLM.

A common mistake was listing general challenges of GLMs without any reference to the particular issues raised by loss ratio data.

## Part c (0.25 point)

GLM analysis is widely accepted in classification ratemaking. Briefly discuss one reason that univariate analysis may be more appropriate than GLM analysis.

### Explanation

Univariate analysis is far more transparent and easier to explain — the result can be shown, understood and accepted by underwriters, management and regulators, whereas a GLM's output is harder to communicate and some regulators will not accept it. It is also the practical choice where data volume is too thin to support a credible multivariate fit, or where the company lacks the software, computing power or expertise to build and maintain a GLM, particularly for a simple rating algorithm where the added complexity and cost are not justified.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to discuss one reason why univariate analysis could be more appropriate than multivariate analysis.

A common mistake was describing a difficulty without specifying whether it applied to the multivariate or the univariate approach, so no credit could be awarded.
