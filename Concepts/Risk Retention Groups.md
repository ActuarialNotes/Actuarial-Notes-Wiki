A **Risk Retention Group** (RRG) is a liability insurance company owned by its own insureds, who share similar or related liability exposures, formed under the federal **Liability Risk Retention Act of 1986** (LRRA). An RRG is chartered and licensed as a liability insurer in one state, its domicile, and may then write in every other state after only registering there, because the LRRA **preempts** most non-domiciliary state insurance law.

An RRG is defined by four features:

1. Its owners are its insureds.
2. It writes **liability coverage only**, for its members' businesses or activities. It may not write property insurance, workers' compensation or personal lines.
3. Its members are engaged in similar or related businesses, so their liability exposures are alike: a physicians' RRG, a trucking RRG, a nursing-home RRG.
4. Its domiciliary state alone licenses, examines and supervises its solvency.

The LRRA expanded the Product Liability Risk Retention Act of 1981 to all commercial liability after the liability-insurance availability crisis of the mid-1980s, when buyers such as physicians, contractors and municipalities could not find coverage at any price. Pooling within a homogeneous group of owners is a way to self-insure a line the commercial market has left.

> [!example]- Which of these coverages can the contractors' RRG write? {Example}
> Forty regional building contractors form an RRG. They want it to cover their (a) general liability, (b) commercial auto liability, (c) workers' compensation, (d) equipment and building property, and (e) the owners' personal auto policies.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - (a) General liability, yes. Commercial liability arising from the members' common business is what the LRRA covers.
> > - (b) Commercial auto liability, yes, as liability arising from the business. Physical damage to the vehicles is property and is not.
> > - (c) Workers' compensation, no. The LRRA excludes it.
> > - (d) Property, no. An RRG writes liability only.
> > - (e) Personal auto, no. Personal lines are excluded.
> > - For (c) to (e) the contractors need the admitted market, a surplus lines placement, or another vehicle such as a captive.

## What a non-domiciliary state may require

A non-domiciliary state may require only a limited set of things:

1. Registration.
2. Premium taxes.
3. Compliance with its unfair claims practices laws.
4. Participation in its residual market mechanisms.
5. A copy of the annual financial statement, certified by an independent accountant and including an opinion on loss and LAE reserves by an actuary or qualified loss reserve specialist.
6. Compliance with a court injunction if the RRG is found to be in hazardous financial condition.

It cannot require a separate licence, impose its rate and form approval, or make the RRG join a guaranty fund.

> [!example]- What must a Vermont-domiciled RRG do to write in New York and Texas? {Example}
> - Only Vermont licenses and supervises the RRG.
> - New York and Texas can require it to register, pay premium tax, file its annual statement and actuarial opinion, follow their unfair claims practices laws, and join their residual market mechanisms.
> - They cannot require a separate licence, impose their rate and form approval, or make it join a guaranty fund.

## No guaranty fund

State law may not make an RRG a member of a state insurance guaranty fund, so an RRG's insureds are unprotected if it fails. Every RRG policy must carry a prominent notice stating that the RRG may not be subject to all of the state's insurance laws and that state insolvency guaranty funds are not available.

> [!example]- A physicians' RRG fails with $\$40$ million of unpaid claims and $\$15$ million of assets. What protection do claimants have? {Example}
> - The $\$15$ million of assets is distributed in liquidation under the domiciliary state's supervision.
> - No guaranty fund responds in any state where the physicians practise.
> - The $\$40 - \$15 = \$25$ million shortfall falls on the physicians, who face their claims personally, and on the injured claimants.
> - The members are both owners and insureds, so in a failure they lose their capital and their coverage.

## Purchasing groups

**Purchasing groups** are the LRRA's other vehicle: a group of similar buyers that purchases liability insurance *from* an insurer, rather than owning one. The group bears no risk, and the insurer does.

## Regulatory concerns

Solvency rests on one domicile, often a captive-friendly state, so regulators worry about domicile shopping, thin capitalisation and governance by service providers rather than owners. The NAIC's response has been to apply its solvency accreditation standards to a state's supervision of the multi-state RRGs it domiciles, including those formed as captives. [[Excess and Surplus Lines]] is the other route by which a buyer reaches coverage outside the admitted market, and [[Specialized Insurance Topics]] covers the wider setting.

> [!trap] Assuming a guaranty fund stands behind an RRG
> An RRG is a licensed insurer, but no state guaranty fund covers its policies. If it fails, claimants recover only from its assets.
