An article in the May 2016 issue (no. 80) of the Society of Actuaries Health Section's Health Watch on level funding, a self-funded product for small groups. It introduces the market the products serve, how they are designed and priced, why they had yet to gain significant market share, the regulatory and market considerations, and the risks to both insurers and small groups.

## Introduction
- Under the Affordable Care Act, groups with 50 or fewer employees will eventually be subject to modified community rating rules; some will see lower premiums, others significant increases, and many of the latter kept transitional coverage at renewal in 2014.
- Self-funding has historically been realistic only for groups with at least a few hundred employees; the premium disruption has led insurance carriers and third-party administrators to develop an alternative self-funded product for small groups, sometimes called "Level Funding".

## Self-Funding Basics
- Self-funding is a spectrum of funding methods in which the group bears a significant portion of the financial risk of its members' health coverage rather than the insurance company or health plan.
- Forms include administrative services only (ASO), ASO with stop loss, minimum premium arrangements, and coverage administered by a TPA with or without stop-loss arrangements.
- Advantages for groups: avoiding premium taxes, state mandates and certain ACA-related fees; benefiting directly from favourable claims experience; forgoing insurer risk charges.
- Disadvantages: less predictable cash flows, responsibility for unfavourable claims experience, the need to pay for professional advice, and the potential need to buy stop-loss insurance.

## Level Funding Basics
- Level funding is an ASO product with integrated stop-loss coverage offered by insurance companies, brokers and TPAs, giving the group fixed or level monthly costs.
- Five cost components: an ASO fee; aggregate stop-loss coverage; specific stop-loss coverage; a paid claims fund held by the issuer for the group's expected non-stop-loss claims in the projection period; and an incurred but not reported (IBNR) fund for claims incurred in the period but paid afterward.
- A sixth, unofficial component is an incurred claims cost projection, used to develop several of the official cost components.
- The paid claims fund is the product of the aggregate stop-loss corridor and the group's projected paid claims below the specific stop-loss deductible; if actual paid claims fall below the corridor, the group receives some portion of the fund's surplus as a refund.

## Level Funding and ACA Pricing Comparison
- The primary appeal for some small groups is price: ACA community rating builds in a "subsidy" for costlier groups, while stop-loss pricing is not subject to community rating.
- Two hypothetical 45-subscriber groups, Living Well Graphic Arts and Classic Cabs, are compared on small group ACA, small group transitional and level funding rates for the plan year beginning January 2017 (Tables 2 to 5).
- Classic Cabs' best interest is to migrate to small group ACA coverage as soon as it can; for it a level funding plan will probably be the most expensive choice.

## Considerations with Offering a Level Funding Product
- Insurers would ideally want better-risk small groups to migrate to their small group ACA blocks, so offering level funding to a good-risk group that would otherwise choose an ACA plan risks cannibalizing that block.
- Level funding products should be designed, administered and priced to closely resemble the fully insured products they replace, and may require the filing of rates and forms with the department of insurance in many states.
- Profit can be built into every component of level funding cost, and an insurer may retain a portion of the paid claims surplus as profit.
- Insurers should train sales staff and brokers and develop a target group list of transitional relief groups.

## The Future of Small Groups and Self-Funding
- The market for level funding is tied to the groups expected to migrate to the ACA market; the PACE Act, which removed the requirement that groups with 51 to 99 enrollees migrate to small group ACA plans, reduced the potential size of the market but did not eliminate it.
- More states could adopt the NAIC model stop-loss law or strengthen existing stop-loss laws, and some states or the federal government might outlaw self-funding for smaller groups.
- The article concludes that it is prudent for carriers to develop their own level funding product in 2016 and 2017 to be ready to offer it in earnest in 2018.
