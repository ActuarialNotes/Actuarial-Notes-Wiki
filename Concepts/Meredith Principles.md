**The Meredith Principles** are the five foundations of Canadian [[Workers Compensation Insurance|workers' compensation]], set out in Sir William Meredith's 1913 Ontario royal commission report and adopted, with local variation, by every province. They define the historic bargain: workers surrender the right to sue in exchange for guaranteed, no-fault benefits funded collectively by employers.

- **1. No-fault compensation.** Benefits are paid regardless of who caused the injury; neither the worker's carelessness nor the employer's diligence is investigated. This removes the three common-law defences that had defeated most injured workers' claims.
- **2. Collective liability.** Employers are collectively liable through an assessment pool rather than individually liable to their own workers. A single catastrophic claim does not bankrupt one employer, and no worker's benefit depends on their employer's solvency.
- **3. Security of payment.** A funded pool guarantees benefits will be paid for the life of the claim — the reason board funding ratios are a matter of statutory and political attention.
- **4. Exclusive jurisdiction.** The board alone decides claims; the courts have no role. This eliminates litigation cost and delay, and it is the principle most in tension with the modern expectation of judicial review.
- **5. An independent board.** The board is autonomous of government and of the parties, so that decisions are not politically driven.
- **The bargain cuts both ways** and the exam expects both halves: workers lose the possibility of full tort damages, including pain and suffering, and lose access to the courts; employers lose the ability to defend on fault and pay for injuries they did not cause.
- Modern strains on the principles: exclusive jurisdiction versus a right of appeal; [[Experience Rating|experience rating]] pulling against pure collective liability; and coverage gaps as work shifts to contractors and gig arrangements outside the definition of "worker."

> [!example]- Which Principle Is Under Pressure? {Example}
> Identify the Meredith principle each development strains.
>
> 1. A province introduces aggressive experience rating with large surcharges and rebates.
> 2. A board's funding ratio falls to $75\%$ and the government defers assessment increases to avoid burdening employers.
> 3. A worker seeks judicial review of a board decision denying entitlement.
> 4. Growth in gig and independent-contractor work leaves a large share of the workforce outside coverage.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. **Collective liability.** Experience rating moves cost back toward the individual employer, which is the opposite of collective liability. The defence is that it improves safety incentives — a genuine benefit — but it also gives employers a financial reason to contest claims, which pulls against no-fault. The tension is real and unresolved.
> > 2. **Security of payment.** A $75\%$ funding ratio means benefits already awarded are not fully funded; deferring assessments shifts the cost to future employers, who will pay for injuries that occurred before they existed. Actuarially this is the same intergenerational problem as an underfunded pension plan.
> > 3. **Exclusive jurisdiction.** Courts have generally deferred to boards, but the principle is in permanent tension with administrative law's insistence on review. Every successful review is a small erosion.
> > 4. **All five, by omission.** A worker outside the definition has no no-fault benefit, is not in the collective pool, has no security of payment — and retains the right to sue, which is worth little against a small employer. This is the largest live policy problem in Canadian workers' compensation, and it is a coverage-definition problem rather than a funding one.
