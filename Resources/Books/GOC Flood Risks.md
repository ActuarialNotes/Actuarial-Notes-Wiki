## The six objectives a solution had to meet

1. Provide **adequate and predictable financial compensation** for residents in high-risk areas.
2. Incorporate **risk-informed price signals** and other levers that promote risk-appropriate behaviour.
3. Be **affordable** to residents of high-risk areas, with specific consideration for the most vulnerable.
4. Provide coverage **widely available** to those at high risk across all regions.
5. **Maximise participation** of residents in high-risk areas.
6. Provide **value for money** for governments and taxpayers.

Objectives 2 and 3 pull against each other, and objective 5 against both — that tension is the report, and the exam question.

## The findings

- Total residential flood risk in Canada is estimated at about **\$2.9 billion per year**.
- **The risk is highly concentrated** in a small number of the highest-risk properties, which is why a purely private market prices those properties out and why a pooled or subsidised solution is even conceivable.
- **Some standardisation is needed** in the market — coverage definitions and limits currently vary enough that a homeowner cannot tell what they hold.
- **Participation is key.** Voluntary purchase in high-risk areas is low; without participation, neither the pool nor the price signal works.
- **Greater public intervention can close more of the protection gap, but at a cost** — the report's central trade-off, and the reason it sets out costed options rather than a single recommendation.

## Why it is on the syllabus

Flood is the live Canadian case of an [[Adverse Selection|adversely selected]], systemically correlated peril that the private market covers incompletely, and where the alternative to insurance is *ex post* government relief through the [[Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements|Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements]] — which pays without a price signal and without encouraging anyone to move or mitigate. Expect to be asked to evaluate a proposed scheme against the six objectives, and to compare with the U.K.'s Flood Re and the U.S. National Flood Insurance Program.
