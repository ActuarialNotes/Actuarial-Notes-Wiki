**The Duty of Good Faith** is the mutual obligation of insurer and insured to deal with each other honestly and fairly. It runs in both directions: the insured must disclose material facts truthfully, and the insurer must investigate, evaluate and pay claims promptly and fairly, giving the insured's interests equal consideration to its own.

- The duty arises because the insurance contract is one of **utmost good faith** (*uberrimae fidei*): the insurer relies on facts only the applicant knows, and the insured relies on a promise only the insurer can perform, years later, when they are least able to enforce it.
- **On the insured's side:** the duty to disclose material facts on application, to report claims honestly, and to cooperate in the investigation. Breach — material misrepresentation or fraud — can void the policy or forfeit the claim.
- **On the insurer's side:** to investigate fairly and promptly, to interpret the policy reasonably, to give reasons for a denial, to settle within limits where the insured's exposure warrants it, and not to use its superior position to force a discounted settlement from a vulnerable claimant.
- **The imbalance of power is the point.** A claimant who has just suffered a loss usually needs money immediately and cannot fund years of litigation. Canadian courts have repeatedly said that this vulnerability is what makes the insurer's duty more than a contractual formality — the breach of which supports [[Bad Faith Damages]] and, in serious cases, [[Punitive Damages]].
- The duty is **continuing**: it applies at application, through the policy term, and — most importantly — throughout claims handling and any litigation that follows.
- In Quebec the same obligation arises from the *Civil Code of Québec*'s general duty of good faith rather than from common-law insurance cases, with substantially similar effect.

> [!example]- Where the Line Falls {Example}
> Classify each insurer action as legitimate claims handling or a breach of the duty of good faith.
>
> 1. Denying a claim after an investigation concluding the loss is excluded, with written reasons.
> 2. Requiring a claimant to submit the same medical documentation four times over eighteen months while paying nothing.
> 3. Offering $\$30{,}000$ on a claim the adjuster's own file values at $\$95{,}000$, knowing the claimant is unable to pay their mortgage.
> 4. Reserving rights while investigating, and defending under a reservation of rights.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. **Legitimate.** An insurer is entitled to deny a claim it reasonably believes is not covered. The duty requires a fair process and reasons, not a particular outcome — a good-faith denial that turns out to be wrong is a breach of contract, not bad faith.
> > 2. **Breach.** Delay used as leverage. Repeated demands for material already provided, with no payment and no decision, is the classic pattern Canadian courts identify as bad faith.
> > 3. **Breach**, and the aggravating facts are on the record: the insurer's own valuation contradicts its offer, and it is exploiting known financial distress. This is the fact pattern that produces [[Punitive Damages]].
> > 4. **Legitimate**, and in fact the correct procedure where coverage is uncertain — it preserves the insurer's coverage position while it meets its [[Duty to Defend]]. Failing to reserve rights can waive the coverage defence entirely.
> >
> > The distinction running through all four: good faith regulates the **process and the motive**, not the answer. An insurer may be wrong in good faith; it may not be slow, opaque or coercive.
