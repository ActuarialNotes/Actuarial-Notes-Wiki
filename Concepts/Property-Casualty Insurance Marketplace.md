The **Property-Casualty Insurance Marketplace** is where buyers of property and liability insurance meet the [[Insurer|insurers]] that sell it, usually through producers, with reinsurers behind the insurers and state regulators overseeing the whole. Three features set the product apart from most others: its price is set before its cost is known, its price and policy wording are regulated, and its prices and availability swing through the underwriting cycle.

The marketplace is divided into three markets:

| Market | Who writes the risk | Which risks |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Admitted | Insurers licensed in the state, subject to its rate, form and solvency rules | Risks insurers choose to accept in the voluntary market |
| Surplus lines | Nonadmitted insurers, through a licensed surplus lines broker | Risks not available in the admitted market |
| Residual | Programs and pools of admitted insurers | Basic coverage for buyers no insurer will take voluntarily |

## Participants

Individuals and families buy personal lines such as auto and homeowners insurance. Businesses buy commercial lines, and larger ones employ a risk manager who decides what to retain and what to insure and buys through a broker ([[Risk Treatment]]).

The insurers include stock and mutual companies, reciprocal exchanges, Lloyd's syndicates, captives and government programs ([[Types of Insurers]]). The Insurance Services Office (ISO) collects statistics and acts as an advisory organization on rates and policy forms, which smaller insurers rely on.

Most property-casualty insurance is sold through agents or brokers, the [[Insurance Producer|producers]], paid by commission, with some sold by salaried employees or directly ([[Insurance Distribution Systems]]). An agent legally represents the insurer. A broker acts for the buyer when placing business, and in about half the states brokers are also required to be agents of the insurer.

[[Reinsurance]] is an arrangement by which an insurer, the ceding insurer, transfers all or part of its risk to another company, the assuming reinsurer.

Insurance in the United States is regulated mainly by the states. The state insurance commissioner licenses insurers, agents, brokers and adjusters, oversees solvency, approves policy forms and controls rates. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) works for uniformity across the states, and each state has guaranty funds that pay claims of insolvent licensed insurers.

## Admitted, surplus lines and residual markets

An admitted insurer is licensed in the state and subject to its rate, form and solvency rules. In the voluntary market, auto insurers and other admitted insurers are free to choose which policyholders to accept.

Nonadmitted insurers write risks that are not available in the admitted market, placed through a licensed surplus lines broker, who checks that the insurer is eligible and remits the surplus lines premium tax. New products with no loss history are often written here first, and coverage can move to the admitted market once data build up. Surplus lines policyholders have no guaranty fund protection ([[Excess and Surplus Lines]]).

Residual, shared or involuntary market programs make basic coverage available to high-risk buyers the standard market will not take. All states and the District of Columbia use systems, commonly assigned risk plans, that guarantee auto insurance is available, assigning high-risk drivers proportionally to the insurers doing business in the state. For property, FAIR Plans, beach and windstorm plans, and the state-run Citizens insurers of Florida and Louisiana operate as pools whose premiums, losses and expenses are shared among member insurers.

> [!example]- Which part of the marketplace serves each of these four accounts? {Example}
> A producer has four new accounts in one state:
>
> 1. A family with a clean driving record wants a personal auto policy.
> 2. A driver with several recent accidents has been declined by every voluntary insurer the producer represents, and the state requires auto liability insurance.
> 3. A start-up that rents electric scooters wants liability coverage for a new kind of exposure that admitted insurers will not write.
> 4. A building owner in an older urban area has been declined by several property insurers, and the building passes the state FAIR Plan's inspection.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The family goes to the admitted, voluntary market, where licensed insurers compete for the account and can choose to accept it.
> > - The driver goes to the residual market. The state's auto insurance plan assigns the driver to an insurer doing business in the state, because coverage is compulsory and the voluntary market declined.
> > - The start-up goes to the surplus lines market. A licensed surplus lines broker places it with an eligible nonadmitted insurer, and the policyholder will have no guaranty fund protection if that insurer fails.
> > - The building owner goes to the residual market. The FAIR Plan writes basic property insurance for buildings that pass its inspection but cannot get voluntary coverage.

## Price set before cost is known

Ratemaking is prospective, because the property-casualty rate must be developed before the risk is transferred. An insurer learns its true cost of goods sold only as claims are reported and settled, sometimes years later, so its prices rest on estimates of losses, trends and catastrophes. How the result compares with the plan is measured by the [[Combined Ratio|combined ratio]], which is

> $$\text{CR} = \frac{\text{Losses}}{\text{Premium}} + \frac{\text{Expenses}}{\text{Premium}}$$

- $\text{CR}$ is the combined ratio, the loss ratio plus the expense ratio.

Above 100% the insurer pays more than a dollar in losses and expenses for each premium dollar, though investment income on the funds it holds can still leave it profitable.

> [!example]- What are this line's planned and actual combined ratios, and its result after investment income? {Example}
> With illustrative figures, an insurer prices a line for next year expecting losses of \$65 million and expenses of \$30 million on premium of \$100 million. Storms and rising repair costs push incurred losses to \$78 million, and investment income on the line's funds is \$8 million.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The planned combined ratio is $65\% + 30\% = 95\%$.
> > - The actual combined ratio is $78\% + 30\% = 108\%$.
> > - The underwriting result is a loss of \$8 million instead of the planned \$5 million gain.
> > - Investment income of \$8 million brings the line back to break-even.
> > - The premium could not be changed after the policies were sold: the insurer committed to its price before it knew its costs.

## Regulation of rates and forms

Because the product is a promise of future payment, regulation aims first at insurers' solvency, and also at rates and policy forms. Most states regulate rates for auto, property, liability and workers compensation. Under prior approval, a rate must be approved before it is used. Under file-and-use, an insurer may use a new rate as soon as it is filed. Approval methods vary by state.

## The underwriting cycle

Property-casualty markets alternate between soft markets, when losses are low and prices are very competitive and underwriting standards loosen, and hard markets, when losses exceed expectations, insurers draw on capital, capacity falls and prices rise ([[Underwriting Cycle]]). Prices swing through the cycle even though rates are regulated. A buyer's best treatment can change with the cycle, since retention is more attractive when insurance is expensive.

> [!trap] Treating surplus lines as the residual market
> Surplus lines insurers choose to write risks the admitted market will not, at rates they set. The residual market is a mechanism, often a pool of admitted insurers, that provides coverage to buyers no insurer will take voluntarily.
