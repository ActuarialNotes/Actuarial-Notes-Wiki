Given the following policy year information:

| Effective Date | Overall Average Rate Change |
|---|---|
| October 1, 2015 | 5% |
| April 1, 2016 | 10% |
| October 1, 2016 | 5% |

- All policies are annual.
- Policy year 2016 written premium = \$100,000.
- Policy year 2016 earned premium = \$100,000.
- Policy year 2016 ultimate losses including LAE = \$80,000.
- Loss trend = 0%.
- Premium trend = 0%.
- There are no fixed expenses.

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the policy year 2016 earned premium at current rate level using the parallelogram method.

### Answer
\$106,085

### Explanation
Policy year 2016 contains every policy *written* between 1/1/2016 and 12/31/2016. That makes the on-level calculation simpler than the calendar-year version: because a policy year is defined by writing date, the "parallelogram" collapses to a rectangle and each rate level is weighted by the **fraction of the writing year it was in effect**, not by an area cut diagonally.

**Step 1 — cumulate the rate changes into a rate level index.** Setting the level in force before 10/1/2015 to 1.000:

| In effect from | Change | Cumulative index |
|---|---|---|
| before 10/1/2015 | — | 1.00000 |
| 10/1/2015 | +5% | 1.05000 |
| 4/1/2016 | +10% | $1.05 \times 1.10 = 1.15500$ |
| 10/1/2016 | +5% | $1.155 \times 1.05 = 1.21275$ |

Note the changes must be **cumulated multiplicatively** — the 10% increase applies on top of the 5% already taken.

**Step 2 — weight the levels over the policy year's writing period.**

| Writings | Portion of PY 2016 | Rate level index |
|---|---|---|
| 1/1/2016 – 3/31/2016 | $3/12 = 0.25$ | 1.05000 |
| 4/1/2016 – 9/30/2016 | $6/12 = 0.50$ | 1.15500 |
| 10/1/2016 – 12/31/2016 | $3/12 = 0.25$ | 1.21275 |

$$\text{Average rate level} = 0.25(1.05) + 0.50(1.155) + 0.25(1.21275) = 1.1431875$$

**Step 3 — on-level factor and on-level premium.** The current rate level is the level in force today, 1.21275:

$$\text{OLF} = \frac{1.21275}{1.1431875} = 1.06085$$

$$\text{PY 2016 EP at CRL} = 100{,}000 \times 1.06085 = \$106{,}085$$

The choice of base for the index is arbitrary — indexing the 1/1/2016 level to 1.000 instead gives levels of 1.0, 1.1 and 1.155 with a current level of 1.155, an average of $1.08875$, and the identical on-level factor $1.155/1.08875 = 1.06085$, since only the *ratio* matters.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate policy year earned premium at current rate level using the parallelogram method and then use this premium to determine the variable expense ratio. Candidates were also expected to understand the basic assumption of the parallelogram method and how a violation of that assumption would impact a rate level indication. Finally, candidates were expected to understand the reasons for policy year premium development.

Candidates were expected to calculate the appropriate on-level factor using the parallelogram method and apply to earned premium to develop policy year 2016 earned premium at current rate level.

Common errors included:

- Calculating an on-level factor for calendar year 2016 instead of policy year 2016.
- Calculating the average rate level without cumulating the rate changes.

## Part b (0.25 points)

Calculate the variable expense ratio that would earn an underwriting profit of 5% at the current rate level.

### Answer
19.59%

### Explanation
"At the current rate level" means the indicated rate change is zero, so the loss ratio indication formula must return a factor of exactly 1.0. With no fixed expenses, the indicated change factor is

$$\frac{\text{Loss \& LAE ratio}}{1 - V - Q_T} = 1.0$$

The loss ratio must be measured against the **on-level** premium from part a, not the unadjusted \$100,000:

$$\text{Loss \& LAE ratio} = \frac{80{,}000}{106{,}085} = 0.75411$$

Setting the indication to 1.0 with a profit and contingency provision $Q_T = 5\%$:

$$0.75411 = 1 - V - 0.05 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = 1 - 0.05 - 0.75411 = 0.19589$$

$$V = \mathbf{19.59\%}$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to estimate the variable expense ratio using the earned premium at current rate level calculated in part a. based on the loss ratio rate indication formula.

Common errors included:

- Using the 2016 earned premium not at current rate level.
- Mismatching between the premium used and the indicated rate level change.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Assume the company rapidly increased exposures throughout 2016. Explain whether the parallelogram method would overstate or understate a rate level indication.

### Answer
It would understate the indication.

### Explanation
The parallelogram method assumes premium is **written uniformly throughout the year**. Rapid exposure growth violates that assumption: disproportionately many policies are written late in 2016, when the higher rate levels (1.155 from April, 1.21275 from October) are in force.

Follow the error through the calculation:

1. The true exposure-weighted average rate level for policy year 2016 is **higher** than the 1.1431875 the uniform-writing assumption produces.
2. A higher true average rate level means the true on-level factor is **lower** than 1.06085 — the premium was already closer to current rates than the method believes.
3. The parallelogram method therefore **overstates** the on-level factor, and so **overstates** premium at current rate level.
4. An overstated premium denominator **understates** the loss ratio.
5. An understated loss ratio **understates** the indicated rate change.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the underlying assumption of the parallelogram method is that premium is written evenly throughout the year and that the growth in exposures violated this assumption. Candidates were expected to demonstrate the impact of the increase in exposures on the on-level factor/premium at current rate level that would then impact the loss ratio used in the rate level indication.

Common errors included:

- Commenting on the average rate level but not commenting on the impact this would have on the rate level indication.
- Stating that the parallelogram method assumes uniform writing of exposures but not explaining how this would impact the calculation underlying the indication.
- Pointing out changes to the loss experience or average accident date, as there is not enough information to discuss how this could be impacted by the growth in exposures.

## Part d (0.25 points)

Briefly describe a scenario in which policy year premium is not fixed at the completion of the policy year.

### Explanation
Policy year premium is normally final once every policy in the year has expired, but two common arrangements keep it moving afterward:

- **Premium audit.** On exposure bases that can only be measured after the fact — workers compensation payroll, general liability sales or receipts — the insurer audits the insured's records after the policy expires and books an additional or return premium, which can land well after the policy year closes.
- **Retrospectively rated policies.** Retro premium is a function of the policy's own emerged losses, so it is recalculated at successive adjustment dates for years after the policy year ends, as those losses develop.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to differentiate between different premium aggregations and state the reasons for premium development after the end of the policy year.

Common errors included:

- Discussing loss development (other than retro policies), which would not impact policy year premium development.
- Discussing the mechanics of earning premium over the course of the policy year.
- Commenting on cancellations that occur mid-term but in the next calendar year. These would occur before the end of the policy year, and would not cause changes after the policy year ends.
