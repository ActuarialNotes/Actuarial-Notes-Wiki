Match each reserving characteristic to the type of insurance it describes. An option may be used more than once or not at all.

## Part a (1 point)
Paid claims and case outstanding already make up a high proportion of ultimate claims at early maturities.

- A) Short-tail line, such as automobile physical damage
- B) Long-tail occurrence line, such as medical malpractice occurrence
- C) Claims-made coverage
- D) Low-frequency, high-severity group of claims

### Answer
A

### Explanation
Friedland (Chapter 5) describes automobile physical damage and property as short-tail lines precisely because of the short period over which claims are reported and settled, so paid and case outstanding are most of ultimate early on. **B** is the opposite case. **C** and **D** describe the coverage trigger and the claim size distribution, not the length of the reporting and settlement period.

## Part b (1 point)
Its experience should be kept separate from occurrence experience, and actuaries often prefer report-year aggregation for it.

- A) Short-tail line, such as automobile physical damage
- B) Long-tail occurrence line, such as medical malpractice occurrence
- C) Claims-made coverage
- D) Low-frequency, high-severity group of claims

### Answer
C

### Explanation
A claims-made policy covers claims *reported* during the policy period. Friedland (Chapter 3) notes that for such coverage actuaries often prefer report-year data, and the CAS Statement of Principles on loss and LAE reserves (reproduced as Friedland's Appendix A) says claims-made data should be segregated from occurrence experience. **B** is an occurrence line, grouped by accident date.

## Part c (1 point)
Reserve estimates for it tend to be less accurate, so its evaluation ordinarily requires more extensive analysis.

- A) Short-tail line, such as automobile physical damage
- B) Long-tail occurrence line, such as medical malpractice occurrence
- C) Claims-made coverage
- D) Low-frequency, high-severity group of claims

### Answer
D

### Explanation
The Statement of Principles (Friedland, Appendix A): the same total dollars can come from a few very large claims or many small ones, and estimates tend to be more accurate for a high-frequency, low-severity group than for a low-frequency, high-severity group, which therefore ordinarily needs more extensive analysis. Choosing **B** confuses severity with tail length: a long-tail line is uncertain because of its long development, a separate property.

## Part d (1 point)
IBNR is a large share of the unpaid claims at early maturities, and the cumulative development factors for the most recent accident years are highly leveraged.

- A) Short-tail line, such as automobile physical damage
- B) Long-tail occurrence line, such as medical malpractice occurrence
- C) Claims-made coverage
- D) Low-frequency, high-severity group of claims

### Answer
B

### Explanation
Friedland classifies medical malpractice occurrence as a long-tail line because of the lengthy reporting and settlement period, and her Chapter 5 comparison with automobile physical damage shows a far larger IBNR share for it. In Chapter 7 she notes that for long-tail lines the cumulative development factors for recent years can become very large, so projections are highly leveraged. **C** is tempting because medical malpractice is also written claims-made, but a claims-made policy covers only claims reported during its period, so claims not yet reported are not its liability; the large IBNR share belongs to the occurrence form.
