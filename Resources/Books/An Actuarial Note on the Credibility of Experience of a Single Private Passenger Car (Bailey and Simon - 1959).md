A six-page note, in the Proceedings of the Casualty Actuarial Society, that uses the experience of the Canadian merit rating plan for private passenger cars to evaluate the experience rating credibility of one car. The Canadian experience includes that of virtually every insurance company operating in Canada, collated by the Statistical Agency acting under instructions from the Superintendent of Insurance. The authors solve the experience rating formula for the credibility Z, by merit rating class and for experience periods of one, two and three years.

> - [[Exam 8 (CAS)|Exam 8]] — objectives A2–A4; including the discussion of the paper by Hazam (PCAS XLVII, 1960).

## The Canadian merit rating data and the credibility formula
- Merit ratings A, X, Y and B correspond to three or more, two, one, and no years since the most recent accident or since licensing; earned premiums are put on a common rate basis using the relationship A:X:Y:B = 65:80:90:100.
- Relative claim frequency is calculated on the basis of premium rather than car years, which avoids the maldistribution created by higher claim frequency territories producing more X, Y and B risks and higher territorial premiums.
- The experience rating formula is Modification = ZR + (1 − Z), where Z is the [[Credibility|credibility]] and R the ratio of actual losses to expected losses; setting the modification equal to the relative claim frequency allows the formula to be solved for Z, which for accident-free risks (R = 0) is 1 − Modification.

## Credibility by class and by length of experience period
- Credibilities for a private passenger car for periods of one, two and three years are shown in Table 2, with each class's average claim frequency; credibility for experience rating depends on the amount of variation of individual hazards within the class as well as on the volume of data.
- Table 3 gives the credibility of a two or three-year period relative to one year; the relative credibilities for two and three years are much less than 2.00 and 3.00, which the authors attribute partly to risks entering and leaving the class and otherwise to changes in an insured's chance of an accident or to skewness in the risk distribution.
- Experience rating finds the deviation of an individual risk from the average risk, unlike class ratemaking, where more data increases reliability only in proportion to the square root of the volume.
- For Class 1B risks, with an average of 1.044 accidents in the year prior to rating, a one-year credibility of .043 is found, confirming the .046 produced from the combined A + X + Y group.
- Tables 1, 2 and 3 are based on accident frequency to reduce chance fluctuations from the size of claims; Table 4 repeats the Class 1 data with losses in place of the number of claims.

## Conclusions
- The experience for one car for one year has significant and measurable credibility for experience rating.
- In a highly refined private passenger rating classification system there would not be much accuracy in an individual risk merit rating plan, but where a wide range of hazard is encompassed within a classification, credibility is much larger.
- Given one year's experience, adding a second year increases the credibility roughly two-fifths; given two years, a third year increases it by one-sixth of its two-year value.

## Appendix I
- Illustrates, with a model of 100,000 risks at a true claim frequency of .05, 100,000 at .10 and 50,000 at .20 and a Poisson approximation, that credibilities would vary approximately in proportion to the number of years for the first few years and typical frequencies.

## Appendix II
- Using the Poisson distribution as an approximation, the average number of claims produced by risks with one or more claims is m/(1 − e^(−m)); for the Class 1 claim frequency of .087 per car, risks that had one or more claims last year averaged 1.044 claims.

## Discussion by W. J. Hazam (PCAS XLVII, 1960)
- The paper's most provocative conclusion is that the experience for one car-year has significant and measurable credibility.
- A premium base eliminates maldistribution only if high frequency territories are also high premium territories and territorial differentials are proper; premium is nevertheless an improvement over exposure as a base for this type of study.
- The authors' statement that credibilities for one, two and three years vary approximately in proportion to the number of years holds largely true only for low credibilities; using the P/(P + K) formula with k = 2074 (derived from 100 claims per year giving a one-year credibility of .046), the theoretical relativities are 1.00, 1.91 and 2.74 against the observed 1.00, 1.48 and 1.74.
