A CAS study note (October 2012) on basic reinsurance accounting topics that other syllabus readings may omit. Taking the six principal functions of reinsurance in turn, it works a numerical example of each and shows how the ceded reinsurance affects the ceding company's surplus, loss reserves, unearned premiums, leverage ratios and income statement, with the financial statements following the statutory convention of offsetting ceded liabilities against gross liabilities.

> [!info] On the syllabus
> - [[Exam 6I (CAS)|Exam 6I]] — objectives D1, D4–D5; the whole study note.

## Ceded Reinsurance Impact on Financial Statements
- Lists the six principal functions of reinsurance given in Connor Harrison's *Reinsurance Principles and Practices*: increase large line capacity, provide catastrophe protection, stabilize loss experience, provide surplus relief, facilitate withdrawal from a market segment and provide underwriting guidance.
- Each example compares the ceding company "without" and "with" the reinsurance, and examines the impact on surplus, loss reserves, unearned premiums, leverage ratios and the income statement.

## 1 Increase large line capacity
- Example 1: XYZ, a homeowners writer unwilling to write homes insured over $500,000 without reinsurance, buys a [[Surplus Share|surplus share]] pro rata treaty ceding, for each policy, the excess of the home value over $500,000 divided by the total home value.
- Balance Sheet
    - Assets
    - Liabilities
    - Surplus
- Income Statement
- Other Financial Statistics
- Analysis of impact: gross and net loss reserves increase; net leverage ratios increase slightly, gross leverage ratios begin to differ materially from net, and reinsurance leverage becomes important.

## 2 Provide Catastrophe Protection
- Example 2: ABC buys a catastrophe treaty for 5% of gross premium that pays losses from a single event in excess of 10% of premium, and the example is shown with and without a $500,000 catastrophe.
- Balance Sheet
    - Assets
    - Liabilities
    - Surplus
- Income Statement
- Other Financial Statistics
- Analysis of impact: the reinsurance reduces surplus if no catastrophe occurs but mitigates the risk of a significant drop in surplus if one does; ceded reinsurance leverage can be significantly affected in the period after a major catastrophe.

## 3 Stabilize loss experience
- Example 3: DEF, facing the possibility of a high loss year at a 125% loss ratio, buys an [[Aggregate Excess of Loss|aggregate excess of loss]] treaty for the entire book.
- Balance Sheet
    - Assets
    - Liabilities
    - Surplus
- Income Statement
- Other Financial Statistics
- Analysis of impact: the expected value of surplus is lower, by the expected net cost of the reinsurance, but varies less from period to period; net loss reserves should be smaller and more stable.

## 4 Provide surplus relief
- Example 4: XYZ, with leverage ratios higher than desired, buys a 50% [[Quota Share|quota share]] with a 20% ceding commission.
- Balance Sheet
    - Assets
    - Liabilities
    - Surplus
- Income Statement
- Other Financial Statistics
- Analysis of impact: net leverage ratios improve significantly while ceded reinsurance leverage ratios increase, so the insurer's solvency becomes more reliant on its reinsurers'; ceding half the business does not halve the net leverage ratios, because the cession also changes surplus.

## 5 Facilitate withdrawal from a market segment
- Example 5: XYZ stops writing new business and buys prospective reinsurance ceding 100% of its remaining unearned premium and all losses occurring after the beginning of the year, with a ceding commission covering the commission it paid the previous year.
- Balance Sheet
    - Assets
    - Liabilities
    - Surplus
- Income Statement
- Other Financial Statistics
- Analysis of impact: net loss and unearned premium reserves disappear and net leverage ratios are zero, leaving reinsurance collectability as the only remaining insurance risk.

## 6 Provide underwriting guidance
- An insurer entering a new market heavily reinsures its writings there, relying on the reinsurer's expertise in pricing and underwriting it.
- No numeric example is given; the note calls it conceptually equivalent to Exhibit 1, in which reinsurance creates new business opportunities.
