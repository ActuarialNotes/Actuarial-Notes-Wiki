Given the following information for an insurer as of December 31, 2016:

- Company management strengthened outstanding case reserves on all open claims during 2016.
- Four unusually large claims that occurred in 2015 have been paid and closed. There are no other large losses in the company's history.
- No adjustments were made to historical claim development factors.

Briefly assess the appropriateness of each reserving technique provided for each accident year below.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Accident year 2013:

i. Paid development technique.
ii. Reported development technique.

### Explanation
The two facts have to be sorted by *what they touch* and *when*. The case reserve strengthening is a **calendar year 2016** action, so it lands on the latest diagonal of every accident year's reported triangle. The four large claims occurred in **accident year 2015**, so they cannot affect accident year 2013 or 2014 at all.

**i. Paid development technique — appropriate.** Paid claims are untouched by case reserve strengthening, and the large losses belong to a different accident year. Accident year 2013 is also nearly fully developed at 48 months, so the remaining paid development is small. Nothing in the given facts distorts this estimate.

**ii. Reported development technique — overstates ultimate.** Strengthening raises accident year 2013's reported claims on the 2016 diagonal, but the historical development factors were left unadjusted, so they still reflect the *old*, weaker case adequacy. Applying factors built on the assumption that more development is still to come to an already-inflated reported figure double-counts the strengthening and overstates ultimate. The distortion is modest for accident year 2013 because it is mature and its remaining cumulative development factor is close to 1.00, but the direction is unambiguously upward.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how large losses in a *future* accident year and a change in case reserving practice would affect the paid and reported development methods for accident year 2013.

Common errors included stating that the large losses affected either method — they occur in a later accident year and so have no impact — and stating that the reported development method would be understated, when strengthening case reserves without adjusting development factors overstates it.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Accident year 2014:

i. Disposal rate frequency-severity technique.
ii. Reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

### Explanation
**i. Disposal rate frequency-severity technique — appropriate.** This technique is built from closed claim counts and *paid* severities, so case reserve strengthening cannot reach it. Nothing in the given facts suggests the settlement rate changed, which is the assumption the method actually depends on, and the accident year 2015 large claims sit outside accident year 2014's severities. The estimate stands.

**ii. Reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique — overstates ultimate.** The Bornhuetter-Ferguson estimate is

$$\text{Ultimate} = \text{Reported claims} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}}\right) \times \text{Expected claims}$$

Strengthening raises the reported claims term, while the unadjusted historical CDF keeps the percent-unreported factor $1 - 1/\text{CDF}$ at its old, too-high level. In reality a strengthened case basis means *less* is left to be reported, so the true percent unreported is lower. Adding a too-large IBNR to an already-inflated reported figure overstates ultimate — though by less than the reported development method would, since the IBNR piece is anchored to expected claims rather than scaled off the inflated reported amount.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how large losses in a future accident year and a change in case reserving practice would affect the disposal rate frequency-severity technique and the reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

Common errors included stating that the large losses affected either method, when they fall in a later accident year; stating that the disposal rate frequency-severity technique is affected by case reserve strengthening, when it uses paid losses only; and stating that the reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson method would be understated rather than overstated.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Accident year 2015:

i. Paid development technique.
ii. Paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

### Explanation
Accident year 2015 is the year that contains the four unusually large claims, and they have been **paid and closed** — so they are in the paid triangle. The case reserve strengthening is irrelevant to both methods here, since neither uses case reserves.

**i. Paid development technique — not appropriate; overstates ultimate.** The historical paid development factors were selected from years with no large losses, so they are calibrated to a normal payment pattern. Applying them to accident year 2015's inflated paid claims multiplies the large losses by the full development factor, as if each large claim will keep developing in proportion. Since these claims are closed and will not develop further, ultimate is significantly overstated. The distortion is worst at immature ages where the factors are most leveraged.

**ii. Paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique — better, but still somewhat overstated.** The Bornhuetter-Ferguson method only develops the *unpaid* portion off an a priori expected claims figure, so the large losses enter the estimate at their actual amount rather than being leveraged by a development factor. That makes it clearly preferable to paid development. It is not perfect: the large claims cause more of accident year 2015 to be paid at this maturity than the historical pattern assumes, so the percent-unpaid factor $1 - 1/\text{CDF}$ is too high and adds too much unpaid claim on top. The overstatement is materially smaller than under the paid development method.

An unqualified case can also be made that paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson is *appropriate*, since it both captures the large claims at face value and derives the remaining unpaid amount from an a priori estimate that the large losses have not contaminated.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how large losses in the *current* accident year and a change in case reserving practice would affect the paid development method and the paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique.

Common errors included stating that the paid development method is not affected by the large losses; stating that either method is affected by the case reserve change, when both are paid methods; and stating that the large losses would cause either method to be understated.
