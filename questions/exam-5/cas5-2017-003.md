Given the following information about two claims:

| Claim Number | Accident Date | Transaction Date | Incremental Payment | Ending Case Reserves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | January 1, 2015 | January 1, 2015 | \$0 | \$20,000 |
| 1 | January 1, 2015 | January 1, 2016 | \$25,000 | \$0 |
| 2 | April 1, 2015 | July 1, 2015 | \$0 | \$50,000 |
| 2 | April 1, 2015 | October 1, 2015 | \$25,000 | \$75,000 |
| 2 | April 1, 2015 | April 1, 2016 | \$100,000 | \$20,000 |

## Part a (0.5 points)

Calculate the incurred losses for accident year 2015 as of May 1, 2016.

### Answer
\$170,000

### Explanation
Both claims have 2015 accident dates, so both belong to accident year 2015. Accident year incurred losses as of a valuation date are the cumulative amount paid on those claims through that date plus the case reserves standing open at it:

$$\text{Incurred} = \text{Cumulative paid} + \text{Case outstanding}$$

Every transaction listed occurs on or before 5/1/2016, so all of them count:

| | Cumulative paid | Case at 5/1/2016 | Incurred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim 1 | \$25,000 | \$0 | \$25,000 |
| Claim 2 | $25{,}000 + 100{,}000 = \$125{,}000$ | \$20,000 | \$145,000 |
| **Total** | **\$150,000** | **\$20,000** | **\$170,000** |

$$\text{AY 2015 incurred at 5/1/2016} = 150{,}000 + 20{,}000 = \$170{,}000$$

Note the case reserve to use is the **latest** one for each claim, not the sum of the ending case reserves down the column — each row restates the claim's outstanding balance rather than adding to it.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the definition of incurred loss and how to aggregate losses by accident year.

A common error was an incorrect calculation of the ending case reserve — typically summing every case figure in the exhibit instead of taking each claim's balance as of the valuation date.

## Part b (1 points)

Calculate the incurred losses for calendar year 2015 and calendar year 2016.

### Answer
CY 2015: \$120,000; CY 2016: \$50,000

### Explanation
Calendar year incurred losses count only the *transactions* that occur during the year, regardless of when the claim occurred:

$$\text{CY incurred} = \text{Payments during the year} + \text{Ending case} - \text{Beginning case}$$

**Calendar year 2015.** Both claims start the year with a case reserve of zero, since neither was reported before 2015.

| | Paid in 2015 | Case 12/31/2015 | Case 12/31/2014 | Incurred |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claim 1 | \$0 | \$20,000 | \$0 | \$20,000 |
| Claim 2 | \$25,000 | \$75,000 | \$0 | \$100,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **\$120,000** |

$$\text{CY 2015} = 25{,}000 + (95{,}000 - 0) = \$120{,}000$$

**Calendar year 2016.** Both claims now carry the case reserves they closed 2015 with, and both reserves are drawn down as payments are made — so the change in case is negative and partly offsets the payments.

| | Paid in 2016 | Case 12/31/2016 | Case 12/31/2015 | Incurred |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claim 1 | \$25,000 | \$0 | \$20,000 | \$5,000 |
| Claim 2 | \$100,000 | \$20,000 | \$75,000 | \$45,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **\$50,000** |

$$\text{CY 2016} = 125{,}000 + (20{,}000 - 95{,}000) = \$50{,}000$$

The two calendar years sum to \$170,000, matching part a — as they must, since accident year 2015 is the only accident year present and 5/1/2016 is after the last transaction.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the definition of incurred loss and how to aggregate losses by calendar year.

Common errors included calculating only one of the two calendar years; adding in the ending case reserve without subtracting out the prior case balance, which double-counts reserves carried into the year; and not recognizing that the beginning case reserve for both claims was zero at the start of the first calendar year.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Briefly describe one advantage and one disadvantage of calendar year aggregation.

### Explanation
**Advantage — the data is final and immediately available.** Calendar year figures are fixed at 12/31 and never develop afterward: any later transaction falls into a subsequent calendar year. That makes calendar year the most responsive aggregation, usable as soon as the year closes, and it is why it ties directly to year-end financial reporting.

**Disadvantage — it mismatches losses against premium.** A calendar year's incurred losses include transactions on claims from *all* prior accident years, while its earned premium comes only from the current year's exposures. Because losses are never allowed to develop within the aggregation, calendar year data is a poor basis for estimating IBNR and is especially unsuitable for long-tailed lines, where most of a year's transactions relate to older accident years.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to give one advantage and one disadvantage of calendar year aggregation.

Common errors included giving only one of the two; stating there was a mismatch of premium without saying what it was mismatched *with* (losses); and citing the absence of development as a disadvantage without describing why that is a problem.
