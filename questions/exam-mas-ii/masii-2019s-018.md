You are building the following model:

$$
\begin{align*}
y_i &\sim \text{Exponential}(\theta) \\
\theta &\sim \text{Uniform}(0, \omega) \\
\omega &\sim \text{Uniform}(7, 10)
\end{align*}
$$

Your buddy, Sarah Jane, tells you that the posterior distribution of $\theta \mid \omega$ is:

$$
p(\theta \mid \omega) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\frac{1}{\theta}e^{-\sum y_i/\theta}}{\int_0^{\omega} \frac{1}{\theta}e^{-\sum y_i/\theta}\,d\theta} & \text{if } 0 < \theta < \omega \\[2ex]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

Determine which of the following statements is correct.

- A) Sarah Jane is correct.
- B) Sarah Jane is incorrect because the bounds of the integral are incorrect.
- C) Sarah Jane is incorrect because she didn't include the prior density.
- D) Sarah Jane is incorrect because the variable of integration ($d\theta$) is incorrect.
- E) Sarah Jane is incorrect for another reason not listed in (B), (C), or (D).

## Explanation

Build the posterior from the pieces and compare.

The $n$ observations are independent exponentials, so the likelihood is

$$
\begin{align*}
L(\theta) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\theta}e^{-y_i/\theta} \\
          &= \frac{1}{\theta^{\,n}}e^{-\sum y_i/\theta}
\end{align*}
$$

The prior $\theta \mid \omega \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \omega)$ has density $1/\omega$ on $(0, \omega)$, so

$$
p(\theta \mid \omega, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{\theta^{-n}e^{-\sum y_i/\theta} \cdot \frac{1}{\omega}}{\int_0^{\omega} \theta^{-n}e^{-\sum y_i/\theta} \cdot \frac{1}{\omega}\,d\theta}, \qquad 0 < \theta < \omega.
$$

Now check each of the offered reasons:

- **The bounds are right.** The prior confines $\theta$ to $(0, \omega)$, so the normalizing integral runs over $(0, \omega)$ — (B) is not the error.
- **Omitting the prior is harmless here.** The uniform density $1/\omega$ is free of $\theta$, so it cancels between numerator and denominator — (C) is not the error.
- **The variable of integration is right.** We are normalizing over $\theta$, so $d\theta$ is correct — (D) is not the error.

The actual mistake is the exponent on $\theta$: Sarah Jane wrote $\frac{1}{\theta}$ where the likelihood of $n$ observations requires $\frac{1}{\theta^{\,n}}$. She used a single observation's density instead of the full sample likelihood.

That reason is not among (B), (C) or (D), so the answer is **E**.
