A **Risk Measure** $\rho$ assigns a single number $\rho(X)$ to a random loss $X$, such as the capital needed to support it or the premium to charge for it. A *capital* risk measure sets the assets $a$ an insurer holds against $X$, and a *pricing* risk measure sets the premium for the loss those assets can actually pay, $X \wedge a$.

## Basic measures of risk

[[Frequency]] is how often losses occur and [[Severity]] is how large they are. The [[Expected Value|expected value]] is the central tendency. The *range*, highest outcome minus lowest, is a rough measure that ignores how likely the extremes are, so two exposures can have the same range while one reaches its extreme far more often. [[Variance]] and [[Standard Deviation|standard deviation]] average the deviations from the mean, treating favourable and unfavourable surprises alike. *Semivariance* and downside standard deviation count only unfavourable deviations. *Shortfall risk* is the probability of falling below a target, with the probability of ruin the case where the target is the point capital runs out.

A standard deviation grows with the size of an exposure, so comparing the standard deviations of exposures with different means overstates the risk of the larger one. The [[Coefficient of Variation|coefficient of variation]] of a loss with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ is

> $$\text{CV} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu}$$

> [!example]- Which location's losses are relatively more variable? {Example}
> A retailer's annual property losses ($\$000$s) over five years were:
>
> | Location | Losses |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | A | 40, 55, 60, 45, 50 |
> | B | 70, 130, 100, 90, 110 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Location A has mean $50$, range $60 - 40 = 20$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{250/5} = 7.07$.
> > - Location B has mean $100$, range $130 - 70 = 60$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{2{,}000/5} = 20$.
> > - B's standard deviation is almost three times A's, but its mean is twice as large.
> > - The coefficients of variation are $7.07/50 = 0.14$ for A and $20/100 = 0.20$ for B.
> > - B's losses are relatively more variable.

The CAS's 2003 ERM overview splits ERM measures into two families. *Solvency-related* measures look at the adverse tail (probability of ruin, shortfall risk, VaR, EPD, TVaR) and concern customers and their proxies, regulators and rating agencies. *Performance-related* measures look at volatility near the mean (variance, standard deviation, semi-variance, below-target risk) and concern owners and their proxies, such as stock analysts.

## Value at Risk

**Value at Risk** is the lower $p$-quantile of the loss ([[Percentile]]). With $F$ the distribution function of $X$, the Value at Risk at level $p$ is

> $$\text{VaR}_p(X) = \inf\{\, x : F(x) \geq p \,\}$$

A [[Probable Maximum Loss|PML]] is a VaR: the 1-in-250 PML is $\text{VaR}_{0.996}$. VaR is well understood, but it is blind to how bad losses beyond the quantile are. Solvency II sets capital at a 99.5% one-year VaR ([[Solvency II]]).

## Tail Value at Risk

**Tail Value at Risk** averages the worst $1-p$ of outcomes. The Tail Value at Risk at level $p$ is

> $$\text{TVaR}_p(X) = \frac{1}{1-p}\int_p^1 \text{VaR}_s(X)\, ds$$

- $\text{VaR}_s(X)$ is the Value at Risk at each level $s$ from $p$ to $1$.

For any distribution, TVaR is VaR plus the average excess over it in the tail:

> $$\text{TVaR}_p = \text{VaR}_p + \frac{E[(X - \text{VaR}_p)^+]}{1-p}$$

- $(X - \text{VaR}_p)^+$ is the amount by which $X$ exceeds $\text{VaR}_p$, or $0$ if it does not.

The **conditional tail expectation** $E[X \mid X \geq \text{VaR}_p]$ equals TVaR for a continuous loss but not when there is a probability mass at $\text{VaR}_p$. *Expected shortfall* usually means TVaR. The **expected policyholder deficit** $E[(X - a)^+]$ is the loss that assets $a$ leave unpaid. With $a = \text{VaR}_p$, the second box gives an EPD of $(1-p)(\text{TVaR}_p - \text{VaR}_p)$. The Swiss Solvency Test uses 99% TVaR.

> [!example]- What are $\text{VaR}_{0.95}$, $\text{TVaR}_{0.95}$ and $\text{CTE}_{0.95}$ of this catastrophe loss? {Example}
> An insurer's annual catastrophe loss $X$ ($\$$ millions) is:
>
> | $x$ | 0 | 10 | 50 | 100 |
> | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
> | $P(X = x)$ | 0.80 | 0.12 | 0.06 | 0.02 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - $E[X] = 0.12(10) + 0.06(50) + 0.02(100) = 6.2$, and $\sigma(X) = \sqrt{362 - 6.2^2} = 17.99$.
> > - $F$ steps through $0.80$, $0.92$, $0.98$ and $1$, so the first value at which $F$ reaches $0.95$ is $50$, and $\text{VaR}_{0.95} = 50$.
> > - The worst $5\%$ of probability is all $2\%$ at $100$ and $3\%$ of the $6\%$ at $50$, so $\text{TVaR}_{0.95} = (2 + 1.5)/0.05 = 70$.
> > - The shortcut agrees: $50 + 0.02(100 - 50)/0.05 = 70$.
> > - CTE conditions on $X \geq 50$, which takes in the whole mass at $50$, so $\text{CTE}_{0.95} = (3 + 2)/0.08 = 62.5$.
> > - A 1-in-20 year costs $\$50$ million, and the worst 1-in-20 years average $\$70$ million.

## Moment-based measures

The standard deviation principle and the variance principle, the classical basis of [[Risk Loads|risk loads]], are

> $$\rho(X) = E[X] + k\,\sigma(X)$$

> $$\rho(X) = E[X] + k\,\text{Var}(X)$$

- $k$ is the selected loading.

Both measure volatility around the mean rather than the tail. For a highly skewed loss such as hurricane risk, the tail measures VaR and TVaR are more appropriate, and TVaR, which charges for the whole tail, is preferred to VaR.

## Coherence

**Coherence** (Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath, 1999) asks for four properties:

- *monotone*: $X \leq Y \Rightarrow \rho(X) \leq \rho(Y)$
- *subadditive*: $\rho(X + Y) \leq \rho(X) + \rho(Y)$
- *positive homogeneous*: $\rho(\lambda X) = \lambda\,\rho(X)$ for $\lambda \geq 0$
- *translation invariant*: $\rho(X + c) = \rho(X) + c$

TVaR has all four. VaR fails subadditivity, so it may not reflect the benefit of diversification. The standard deviation principle fails monotonicity: with $k = 2$, a 50/50 chance of $0$ or $10$ scores $15$, more than a certain loss of $10$. The variance principle fails positive homogeneity and subadditivity, and $(1+\theta)E[X]$ fails translation invariance.

> [!example]- Is VaR subadditive for these two catastrophe treaties? {Example}
> A reinsurer writes two treaties on unrelated perils. Each year exactly one of three states occurs:
>
> | State | Probability |
> | :--- | ---: |
> | No loss | 0.92 |
> | Treaty 1 loses 10 | 0.04 |
> | Treaty 2 loses 10 | 0.04 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Each treaty alone is $0$ with probability $0.96 \geq 0.95$, so its $\text{VaR}_{0.95}$ is $0$.
> > - Its worst $5\%$ is $4\%$ at $10$ and $1\%$ at $0$, so its $\text{TVaR}_{0.95}$ is $0.4/0.05 = 8$.
> > - The pair loses $10$ with probability $0.08$, so its $\text{VaR}_{0.95}$ and $\text{TVaR}_{0.95}$ are both $10$.
> > - VaR gives $10 > 0 + 0$, so combining unrelated risks raised measured risk, and VaR is not subadditive.
> > - TVaR gives $10 \leq 8 + 8$ and rewards the diversification.

## Distortion risk measures

**Distortion (spectral) risk measures** weight each layer of loss by $g(S(x))$, where $S = 1 - F$ and $g:[0,1] \to [0,1]$ is increasing with $g(0) = 0$, $g(1) = 1$. The distortion risk measure is

> $$\rho_g(X) = \int_0^\infty g\big(S(x)\big)\, dx$$

Those conditions make $\rho_g$ monotone and translation invariant. A **concave** $g$ also makes it subadditive, hence coherent and comonotonic additive. The common distortions are:

- proportional hazard, $s^{\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha \leq 1$,
- Wang, $\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(s) + \lambda)$,
- dual, $1 - (1-s)^m$ with $m \geq 1$,
- TVaR, $\min(1, s/(1-p))$,
- constant cost of capital, $\nu s + \delta$ for $s > 0$.

VaR is the distortion equal to $1$ for $s > 1-p$ and $0$ otherwise, a step that is not concave. With $g(s) = s$, $\rho_g$ is the expected value.

> [!example]- For the catastrophe loss above, what are $\rho_g(X)$ with $g(s) = \min(1, s/0.05)$ and with $g(s) = \sqrt{s}$? {Example}
> - $S(x)$ is $0.20$ on $[0, 10)$, $0.08$ on $[10, 50)$, $0.02$ on $[50, 100)$ and $0$ above.
> - The integral is the sum of each layer's width times $g(S)$.
> - With the TVaR distortion, $\rho_g = 10(1) + 40(1) + 50(0.4) = 70$, which is $\text{TVaR}_{0.95}$.
> - With the proportional hazard distortion, $\rho_g = 4.47 + 11.31 + 7.07 = 22.86$.
> - The proportional hazard premium carries a margin of $16.66$ over the expected loss of $6.2$, most of it on the remote layers.

## Weighted TVaR

TVaR is linear in the tail. Kreps writes a risk measure as a *riskiness leverage* applied to each outcome. TVaR's leverage is zero up to the quantile and constant above it, so a loss twice as large counts exactly twice as bad. Venter notes that the tail measures evaluate the risk above their threshold linearly, "which many consider to be an underweighting". A risk-averse management wants the weight to grow as losses start to impair capital.

**Weighted TVaR (WTVaR)** is TVaR calculated on transformed probabilities, for example from the Wang transform, that shift weight towards the larger losses. A loss twice as large then counts as more than twice as bad, which suits a portfolio with heavy-tailed, skewed losses. The mean of a transformed loss distribution is a risk measure in its own right, and Venter suggests it can get around the problems of the tail methods.

From equally likely simulations, WTVaR is computed in four steps:

1. Sort the scenarios from the worst down.
2. Read each one's transformed probability $f^*$ as the difference between successive values of the transformed CDF $F^*$.
3. Take scenarios from the worst until their $f^*$ add up to the tail probability $1 - p$, cutting the last one's weight so the total is exactly $1 - p$.
4. Divide the weighted sum of losses by $1 - p$.

Plain TVaR is the same calculation with each scenario weighted $1/n$.

> [!example]- What are the 1-in-50 TVaR and WTVaR from these simulations? {Example}
> A model produced $200$ equally likely scenarios. The five worst losses and the transformed CDF at each are:
>
> | Rank | Loss | $F^*$ |
> | ---: | ---: | ---: |
> | 1 | 30 | 1.000 |
> | 2 | 20 | 0.990 |
> | 3 | 15 | 0.982 |
> | 4 | 12 | 0.976 |
> | 5 | 9 | 0.971 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The tail probability is $1/50 = 0.02$.
> > - Each scenario has probability $1/200 = 0.005$, so the worst $4$ fill the tail, and $\text{TVaR} = (30 + 20 + 15 + 12)/4 = 19.25$.
> > - The transformed probabilities are $0.010$, $0.008$ and $0.006$, of which only $0.002$ fits before the total reaches $0.02$.
> > - $\text{WTVaR} = (0.30 + 0.16 + 0.03)/0.02 = 24.5$.
> > - The transform gives the worst scenario twice its plain weight, so WTVaR exceeds TVaR.

## Measures for allocating capital

Venter's survey lists the measures used to allocate capital. They are VaR, the EPD (which can be taken beyond a chosen target level rather than beyond default), TVaR, standard deviation, variance and semi-variance. Excess TVaR, $E[X - \mu \mid X > b]$, charges nothing for the mean loss already funded by premium. Limited liability lets an insurer put its default costs to policyholders, and the value of this **default put option** measures that risk. VaR leaves out how far capital is exhausted, while the EPD, the default option, TVaR and excess TVaR are sensitive to the degree of default.

The measure adopted for capital, limits and reinsurance shapes behaviour. A target at one return period can be met by piling risk just beyond it, while TVaR charges for the whole tail. See [[Risk Taxonomies]], [[Risk Modeling]] and [[Risk Capital]].

> [!trap] Reading VaR as the worst case
> A 99% VaR is the loss exceeded 1% of the time, and it says nothing about how large the losses beyond it are. TVaR averages those tail losses.
