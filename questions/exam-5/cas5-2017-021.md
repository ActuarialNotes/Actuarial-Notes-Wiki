Given the following information:

**Ultimate Claim Estimates (\$000)**

| Accident Year | As of 12/31/2015 — Paid Claim Development Technique | As of 12/31/2015 — Reported Claim Development Technique | As of 12/31/2016 — Paid Claim Development Technique | As of 12/31/2016 — Reported Claim Development Technique |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 109 | 107 | 108 | 110 |
| 2014 | 107 | 108 | 105 | 117 |
| 2015 | 107 | 108 | 102 | 122 |
| 2016 | --- | --- | 100 | 150 |

- The actuary selects age-to-age factors for each development technique using a five-year volume-weighted average.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Describe one scenario that could explain the change in estimates from the December 31, 2015 evaluation to the December 31, 2016 evaluation for accident years 2015 and prior.

### Explanation
Read the two evaluations side by side for accident years 2013–2015. The **paid** estimates are essentially flat, drifting slightly down (109 to 108, 107 to 105, 107 to 102). The **reported** estimates move sharply up (107 to 110, 108 to 117, 108 to 122), and the movement grows as the accident year gets more immature.

That signature — reported development rising across every year while paid development stays put — points to a change in the *reported* data that has not touched the payment stream:

**An increase in case reserve adequacy during calendar year 2016.** If the claims department strengthened case reserves on open claims, the latest diagonal of the reported triangle jumps, the volume-weighted reported age-to-age factors rise, and reported ultimates increase for every accident year still open — most for the least mature years, which have the most reserve left to be affected. Payments are unchanged, so the paid technique produces the same answers as before.

An equally acceptable scenario is a **speed-up in claim reporting** with no change in the rate of settlement: more claims are reported earlier, inflating the reported diagonal and the reported development factors, while the pace at which claims are paid is untouched.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand both the paid and reported claim development techniques, their inherent weaknesses, and appropriate alternatives for those weaknesses.

Candidates were expected to understand the differences between the paid and reported claim development techniques in the context of multiple calendar, accident, and evaluation years.

A common error was only discussing the changes in paid claim development technique and missing the more material change in estimates under the reported claim development technique.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Describe one scenario impacting only accident year 2016 that could explain the difference between the two development techniques.

### Explanation
Accident year 2016 shows a gap far larger than any prior year: 100 by the paid technique against 150 by the reported technique, a 50% spread where prior years differ by at most 20.

**A single large claim occurred and was reported in accident year 2016 but has not yet been paid.** Because the claim sits in case outstanding, the 12-month reported figure for 2016 is unusually high, and multiplying it by an age-to-age factor built from years without such a claim leverages it up into a badly overstated reported ultimate. The paid triangle has not seen a dollar of it, so the paid estimate is unaffected and stays in line with prior years.

The scenario must be confined to a single accident year. A general case reserve strengthening or a slowdown in payments would move several accident years at once and so does not explain a distortion isolated to 2016.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the differences between the paid and reported claim development techniques for a single accident year.

A common error was describing scenarios that impact more accident years than just 2016, such as "case reserve strengthening" or "slow down in payments".

## Part c (0.25 points)

Briefly describe an adjustment or an alternate technique for estimating ultimate claims that is appropriate for the scenario identified in part a. above.

### Explanation
Use the **Berquist-Sherman reported claim adjustment**. Restate the historical case outstanding to the current level of adequacy — trend the average case outstanding along the latest diagonal back through the triangle — and rebuild reported claims as paid claims plus adjusted case outstanding. Development factors calculated on that restated triangle are consistent across diagonals, so the reported development technique no longer reads the reserve strengthening as genuine claim development.

Alternatively, use the **expected claims method**, which relies on an a priori loss ratio or exposure-based estimate rather than the company's own development history, and is therefore not distorted by the change in case reserve adequacy at all.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the weaknesses of the reported claim development technique and provide a brief description of an appropriate alternative technique.

A common error was simply identifying an alternative technique without describing why the technique would be appropriate in this scenario.

## Part d (0.25 points)

Briefly describe an adjustment or an alternate technique for estimating ultimate claims that is appropriate for the scenario identified in part b. above.

### Explanation
Use the **reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson method** for accident year 2016. BF adds actual reported claims to an IBNR figure derived from expected claims and the unreported percentage, so the large claim is recognized at its reported value but is not multiplied up by a development factor. The estimate keeps the known claim without letting it leverage the entire ultimate.

Alternatively, handle the claim separately: **remove the large claim from the triangle**, apply the reported development technique to the remaining claims, and add back the claim department's own estimate of ultimate on the large claim.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand the weaknesses of the reported claim development technique and provide a brief description of an appropriate alternative technique.

Common errors included:

- Simply identifying an alternative technique without describing why the technique would be appropriate in this scenario.
- Treating the issue of a large claim as if in a pricing context instead of estimating ultimate claim liabilities. For example, replacing case incurred with an average load representing future expected large claims is not appropriate.
