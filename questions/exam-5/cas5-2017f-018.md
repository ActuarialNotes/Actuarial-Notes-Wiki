## Part a (2.25 points)

Calculate ultimate claims for accident year 2016 using a frequency-severity technique.

### Answer
Approximately \$34,830,000

### Explanation
Ultimate claims are **ultimate counts $\times$ ultimate severity**, computed *separately for each accident half-year* and then summed. Severity is an average, so the two halves can never be added together — only their claim dollars can.

The data is on an accident half-year basis, so 2016-1 is at age 12 and 2016-2 is only at age 6.

**Step 1 — claim count development.** Age-to-age factors on the count triangle:

| AHY | 6-12 | 12-18 | 18-24 | 24-30 | 30-36 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014-1 | 0.950 | 0.998 | 0.999 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| 2014-2 | 0.950 | 0.998 | 0.999 | 1.000 | |
| 2015-1 | 0.950 | 0.998 | 0.999 | | |
| 2015-2 | 0.950 | 0.998 | | | |
| 2016-1 | 0.950 | | | | |
| **Selected** | **0.950** | **0.998** | **0.999** | **1.000** | **1.000** |

The count factors are identical for first- and second-half years, so a single pattern is appropriate. With no development past 36 months the tail is 1.000:

$$CDF_{12} = 0.998 \times 0.999 = 0.9970 \qquad CDF_{6} = 0.95 \times 0.9970 = 0.9472$$

$$\text{Ult counts}_{2016\text{-}1} = 3{,}705 \times 0.9970 = 3{,}694 \qquad \text{Ult counts}_{2016\text{-}2} = 4{,}100 \times 0.9472 = 3{,}883$$

**Step 2 — severity development, recognizing seasonality.** The severity age-to-age factors are *not* the same across half-years:

| AHY | 6-12 | 12-18 | 18-24 | 24-30 | 30-36 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014-1 | 1.008 | 0.995 | 0.999 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| 2014-2 | 1.025 | 0.995 | 0.999 | 1.000 | |
| 2015-1 | 1.008 | 0.995 | 0.999 | | |
| 2015-2 | 1.025 | 0.995 | | | |
| 2016-1 | 1.008 | | | | |

The 6-12 factor is **1.008 for first halves and 1.025 for second halves** — a clean seasonal split that a straight average across all half-years would destroy. Beyond 12 months the two halves behave identically. Selecting separate patterns:

| Pattern | 6-12 | 12-18 | 18-24 | 24-30 | 30-36 | Tail |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First half | 1.008 | 0.995 | 0.999 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| Second half | 1.025 | 0.995 | 0.999 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 |

$$CDF^{\text{sev}}_{12} = 0.995 \times 0.999 = 0.9940 \qquad CDF^{\text{sev},\,H2}_{6} = 1.025 \times 0.9940 = 1.0189$$

$$\text{Ult sev}_{2016\text{-}1} = 4{,}637 \times 0.9940 = \$4{,}609 \qquad \text{Ult sev}_{2016\text{-}2} = 4{,}500 \times 1.0189 = \$4{,}585$$

**Step 3 — ultimate claims.** Multiply within each half-year, then add:

| AHY | Ult counts | Ult severity | Ult claims |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2016-1 | 3,694 | \$4,609 | \$17,025,600 |
| 2016-2 | 3,883 | \$4,585 | \$17,803,600 |
| **AY 2016** | | | **\$34,829,200** |

Carrying full precision instead of the rounded intermediates gives \$34,830,338; the accepted answers cluster around \$34.83 million.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate ultimate claims for accident year 2016 as ultimate claim counts times ultimate severity, summed across the two 2016 accident half-years, with ultimate counts and ultimate severity each obtained by applying the development technique to the corresponding triangle. Because the data is on an accident half-year basis, candidates were expected to recognize the seasonal difference in severity development between first and second halves and to select separate severity patterns; a single straight average across all half-years does not reflect that seasonality.

Common errors included selecting one severity pattern for all half-years; summing the two half-year counts and summing the two half-year severities and multiplying the totals, which double-counts an average and drastically overstates the result; computing only one half-year and reporting it as accident year 2016; and pairing the age-6 figures for 2016-1 with 6-to-ultimate factors when 2016-1 is at age 12 and the 12-to-ultimate factors apply.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Explain why the downward development observed in the claim count triangle in part a. above may occur.

### Explanation
The triangle counts **reported claims excluding claims closed with no payment**. A claim that is reported and set up as an open claim is included at the early maturities, but if it is subsequently closed without any payment it is removed from the count. As those claims are adjudicated and closed CNP, they drop out of the reported count.

Downward development results whenever the number of claims dropping out through closure with no payment exceeds the number of newly reported claims added in the same period — which is exactly what happens at the later maturities of a triangle, where late reporting has largely stopped but claims are still being closed without payment. Hence the 0.95 factor from 6 to 12 months and the mild further decreases thereafter.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize that the reported count triangle excludes claims closed without payment, and to explain that such claims are present in the count at earlier maturities but drop out as they close, producing downward development.

A common mistake was discussing causes of downward development in claim *amounts* — case reserve reductions, salvage and subrogation recoveries — rather than in claim *counts*.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Discuss a diagnostic that can be used to test for seasonality.

### Explanation
Seasonality cannot be seen in annual data, so any useful diagnostic has to be built at a **finer aggregation than a year** — monthly, quarterly or semi-annual — and then compared across the corresponding sub-periods of several years.

One workable diagnostic is a triangle of the **ratio of closed to reported claim counts by accident quarter (or half-year) at each maturity**. Read down a column, the ratio should be stable if there is no seasonality. If claims arising in one part of the year are settled more slowly than those arising in another — winter weather claims, say, versus summer claims — that sub-period will show a persistently lower closed-to-reported ratio at the same age, and the same sub-period will show it every year. A pattern that repeats by season rather than drifting over time is evidence of seasonality rather than of an operational change, and it tells the actuary that development patterns must be selected separately by sub-period.

Equally acceptable: a triangle of **implied frequency (reported counts divided by earned exposures) by quarter**, which will spike in the same quarter each year if the exposure is weather- or usage-driven; or a triangle of **each quarter's reported counts as a percentage of the accident year's total reported counts**, where a consistently higher share in the same quarter across years indicates seasonality. In each case the diagnostic must be stated *and* its interpretation explained.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to discuss a diagnostic that could be used to test for seasonality, including the use of a level of data aggregation finer than annual (monthly, quarterly or semi-annual), and to explain how the diagnostic would be applied and interpreted.

Common errors included offering diagnostics that would not actually identify seasonality, failing to note that the diagnostic must be on a more granular basis than annual, and simply naming a diagnostic with no discussion of how it would be used.
