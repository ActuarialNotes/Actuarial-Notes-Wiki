## Part a (2 points)

Estimate the accident year 2016 IBNR using the Berquist Sherman case outstanding adjustment.

### Answer
IBNR $\approx$ \$521,000 (ultimate $\approx$ \$2,432,000)

### Explanation
The diagnostics say the problem is **case reserve adequacy**, not settlement rates: the closed-to-reported count ratios are flat down every column (77% at 12 months, 90.9% at 24, ~98% at 36), so claims are being settled at the same pace. But the average case outstanding drops sharply along the latest diagonal — \$4,033 to \$3,158 at 36 months, \$4,184 to \$3,305 at 24 months, \$2,950 to \$2,309 at 12 months, roughly a 21% fall each time — while average *paid* severity is rising steadily. Case reserves have been weakened, so the historical reported triangle is on a stronger case basis than the current diagonal and would develop too high.

The Berquist-Sherman case outstanding adjustment restates the whole triangle onto the **latest diagonal's** level of case adequacy.

**Step 1 - select a severity trend.** Use the average paid claim severity, which is unaffected by case reserving practice:

| Column | 2013 $\to$ 2014 | 2014 $\to$ 2015 | 2015 $\to$ 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 months | 4.65% | 5.45% | 4.89% |
| 24 months | 4.95% | 5.19% | |
| 36 months | 4.85% | | |

Select **5%**.

**Step 2 - restate average case outstanding.** Hold the latest diagonal fixed and de-trend it *backwards* up each column at 5% per year:

$$\text{Adjusted avg case}_{AY,\,m} = \text{Avg case}_{\text{latest }AY \text{ in column } m} \div (1.05)^{\text{latest }AY - AY}$$

| AY | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 1,995 | 2,998 | 3,008 | 0 |
| 2014 | 2,094 | 3,148 | 3,158 | |
| 2015 | 2,199 | 3,305 | | |
| 2016 | 2,309 | | | |

The 48-month column stays at 0 — accident year 2013 has no open claims at 48 months, so there is nothing to restate.

**Step 3 - rebuild the reported triangle.** Multiply by the *actual* open claim counts (those are not adjusted) and add the *actual* paid claims, which are also untouched by the case reserve change:

$$\text{Adjusted reported}_{AY,\,m} = \text{Paid}_{AY,\,m} + \frac{\text{Adjusted avg case}_{AY,\,m} \times \text{Open counts}_{AY,\,m}}{1{,}000}$$

For example, accident year 2013 at 12 months: $1{,}100 + \dfrac{1{,}995 \times 165}{1{,}000} = 1{,}429$.

Adjusted reported claims (\$000):

| AY | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 1,429 | 1,899 | 1,869 | 1,815 |
| 2014 | 1,572 | 2,094 | 2,065 | |
| 2015 | 1,733 | 2,306 | | |
| 2016 | 1,911 | | | |

Note the latest diagonal reproduces the given reported claims exactly (accident year 2016 at 12 months is \$1,911K either way) — that is the point of anchoring on the current diagonal.

**Step 4 - develop.** Volume-weighted age-to-age factors on the adjusted triangle:

| Interval | Individual factors | Selected |
|---|---|---|
| 12-24 | 1.3287, 1.3317, 1.3305 | 1.3303 |
| 24-36 | 0.9844, 0.9862 | 0.9853 |
| 36-48 | 0.9710 | 0.9710 |
| 48-ult | — | 1.0000 |

The factors below 1.00 are the case-weakening working through the adjusted triangle in reverse; they are now consistent across accident years, which is the diagnostic that the adjustment worked.

$$\text{CDF}_{12\text{-ult}} = 1.3303 \times 0.9853 \times 0.9710 \times 1.0000 = 1.2727$$

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2016} = 1{,}911 \times 1.2727 = 2{,}432 \text{ (\$000)}$$

$$\text{IBNR}_{2016} = 2{,}432 - 1{,}911 = 521 \text{ (\$000)} = \$521{,}000$$

IBNR is measured against the *reported* claims of \$1,911K, not the paid claims — the case reserves already carried are not IBNR.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to evaluate severity and/or average case outstanding trends, use the selected trend to restate the average case outstanding triangle onto the latest diagonal, rebuild the adjusted reported triangle, and apply the reported development technique to obtain accident year 2016 IBNR. Sample answers ranged from roughly \$517,000 to \$535,000 depending on rounding of the selected factors.

Common errors included reviewing trends in total claims rather than in average severity or average case outstanding; ignoring trend or trending in the wrong direction; applying the trend to every actual average case outstanding value rather than de-trending a single diagonal; treating the adjusted *average* case outstanding as if it were the total case outstanding; attempting to develop the adjusted case or average case triangle to ultimate; treating the average case outstanding values as if they were in thousands; arithmetic errors within the triangle; and stopping at ultimate losses without subtracting reported claims to get IBNR.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Propose and briefly justify another appropriate technique for developing the accident year 2016 IBNR.

### Explanation
The defining feature of this data is a **case reserve adequacy change with no settlement rate change**. An appropriate alternative is therefore any technique that does not use case reserves at all — the paid data is clean.

**Paid claims development technique.** Case outstanding levels never enter a paid triangle, so the weakening leaves it undisturbed. The diagnostics confirm the paid data is well behaved: the closed-to-reported count ratios are flat down every column, so the settlement rate is unchanged, and the paid age-to-age factors are consistent across accident years. (The jump in the paid-to-reported ratio on the latest diagonal is the *denominator* shrinking as case reserves weaken, not claims being paid faster.) Develop accident year 2016 paid claims to ultimate and subtract reported claims for IBNR.

Two other defensible answers:

- **Frequency-severity (disposal rate) technique on paid data.** Average paid severity is growing at a steady ~5% per year at every maturity and the closed-to-reported count ratios are unchanged, so both the disposal rate pattern and the severity trend are stable and estimable from the given triangles - and neither input touches case reserves.
- **Expected claims (expected loss ratio) technique.** The estimate is independent of the reported and paid triangles altogether, so a case reserve change cannot distort it, provided the underlying expected loss ratio is still appropriate.

What does *not* work is any method that relies on the unadjusted reported triangle or on historical reported development factors — reported development, reported Bornhuetter-Ferguson, or reported Cape Cod - since all of them apply factors built on a stronger case basis to a weaker one.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to propose an appropriate method and briefly justify why it works in the presence of changing case reserves, distinguishing a case reserve change from a settlement rate change and knowing how each shows up in the diagnostic triangles.

Common errors included giving a method with no justification; diagnosing a change in settlement rates that the data does not show; justifications that do not address appropriateness under a case reserve change, for example choosing the paid Bornhuetter-Ferguson method because of highly leveraged development factors; reading the 2016 paid-to-reported ratio as evidence of a settlement rate change and then proposing a method suited to that; and confusing a frequency or severity trend, a change in claim experience, and a change in operational practice, leading to methods that handle changing trends rather than changing case reserves.
