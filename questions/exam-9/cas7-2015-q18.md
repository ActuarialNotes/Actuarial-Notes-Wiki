The International Actuarial Association's *A Global Framework for Insurer Solvency Assessment* outlines the following procedure for simulating underwriting losses for a multi-line insurer:

1. For each line of insurance $i$:
   - a. Select a random number $\chi_i$ from a gamma distribution with mean 1 and variance $c_i$.
   - b. Select a random claim count $K_i$ from a Poisson distribution with mean $\chi_i \lambda_i$ where $\lambda_i$ is the expected claim count for line of insurance $i$.
   - c. For each $i$ and for $k = 1, \ldots, K_i$, select a random claim size, $Z_{ik}$, from a lognormal distribution with mean $\mu_i$ and standard deviation $\sigma_i$.
2. Set $X_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K_i} Z_{ik}$ = Loss for line of insurance $i$.
3. Select a random number $p$, from a uniform (0, 1) distribution. For each line $i$, select $\beta_i$ to be the $p^{th}$ percentile of a distribution with $E[\beta_i] = 1$ and $Var[\beta_i] = b_i$.
4. Set $X = \sum_i \beta_i \cdot X_i$ = Loss for the insurer.

This results in the following formula for the variance of a line's loss ratio (losses divided by expected losses):

$$Var\left[\frac{\beta_i \cdot X_i}{E[\beta_i \cdot X_i]}\right] = (1 + b_i)\left(\frac{\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2}{\lambda_i} + c_i\right) + b_i$$

## Part a (0.5 points)

Derive a formula for the minimum variance of a line's loss ratio for the model described above.

### Explanation
The variance is smallest as the expected claim count grows without bound, $\lambda_i \to \infty$, which removes the process variance term:

$$\lim_{\lambda_i \to \infty} Var\left[\frac{\beta_i X_i}{E[\beta_i X_i]}\right] = \lim_{\lambda_i \to \infty}\left[(1 + b_i)\left(\frac{\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2}{\lambda_i} + c_i\right) + b_i\right] = (1 + b_i)c_i + b_i = c_i + b_i c_i + b_i$$

### Examiner Report
Only a few candidates seemed to know which parameter could be varied to derive the minimum variance.

## Part b (0.25 points)

Briefly explain the significance of the result from part a. above for the estimation of an insurer's underwriting risk within any single line of business.

### Explanation
However large the line (even as $\lambda \to \infty$ and the law of large numbers has its full effect), there is a minimum level of variance in a single line's loss ratio that cannot be diversified away.

### Examiner Report
Only a few candidates seemed to understand that the implication of the answer in part a was that the variance couldn't go below zero.
Parts c & d Very few candidates could correctly interpret the role of bi and ci in the variance formula.

## Part c (0.25 points)

Identify the correlation affected by the parameter $c_i$ above.

### Explanation
The parameter $c_i$ affects the correlation between claims *within* line $i$ (the correlation of losses within a line of business); it determines the spread of claims within the line.

## Part d (0.25 points)

Identify the correlation affected by the parameter $b_i$ above.

### Explanation
The parameter $b_i$ affects the correlation *between* lines of business (the correlation of losses across lines).

## Part e (0.75 points)

For a particular line of business, the standard deviation of the loss ratio can be no smaller than 30%. In addition, the standard deviation of inflationary effects is estimated at 4%. Determine estimates of $b$ and $c$ for this line of business.

### Explanation
The parameter $b$ reflects inflationary effects, so

$$\sqrt{b} = 0.04 \;\Rightarrow\; b = 0.0016$$

Setting the minimum variance to $0.3^2$:

$$0.09 = c + b + bc = c(1.0016) + 0.0016 \;\Rightarrow\; c = 0.0883$$

### Examiner Report
Even among those candidates who could derive the minimum variance formula in part a, very few could use it to plug values in where provided to solve for the remaining quantities.
