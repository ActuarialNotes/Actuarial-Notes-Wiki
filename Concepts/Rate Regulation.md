**Rate Regulation** is provincial oversight of the premiums insurers may charge. It applies most intensively to compulsory private passenger [[Automobile Insurance Reform|automobile insurance]].

The statutory standard across Canada, the same three-part test used in the United States, is that rates be:

1. Not excessive, which protects consumers.
2. Not inadequate, which protects solvency.
3. Not unfairly discriminatory, which protects equity.

## Regimes of rate regulation

The three regimes, in decreasing order of regulatory grip, are:

| Regime | When the insurer may use a new rate |
| :--- | :--- |
| Prior approval (file-and-approve) | Only once the regulator approves it. |
| File-and-use | As soon as it files, subject to later disapproval. |
| Use-and-file (open competition) | At once. The market sets the price, and the filing is a record. Most commercial lines and most non-auto personal lines in Canada operate here. |

Ontario private passenger auto is under prior approval, through [[Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario|FSRA]].

Prior approval protects affordability in a compulsory line but introduces lag, so approved rates chase a cost level that has already moved. In a rising-cost environment lag alone produces inadequacy, which is a [[Solvency Regulation|solvency]] problem the rate regulator does not own.

Suppressed rates do not remove cost. The cost moves into reduced availability, growth of the [[Residual Market|residual market]] and the [[Facility Association]], and ultimately insurer withdrawal from the province.

## What a rate filing is judged on

A filing is judged on prospective cost: historical experience developed to ultimate, trended forward to the average date of loss of the policy period, adjusted for enacted [[Automobile Insurance Reform|reforms]], plus expenses and a permitted profit provision.

Regulators also approve the [[Risk Classification Restrictions|risk classification system]], not just the overall level. A filing can be approved on level and rejected on structure, and prohibited variables must be excluded regardless of their predictive power.

> [!example]- What does this rate decision cost the insurer, and what are its options? {Example}
> An insurer's Ontario auto indication is $+11.4\%$. FSRA approves $+4.0\%$. The insurer writes $\$80$ million of Ontario auto premium at a $101\%$ combined ratio before the change.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The shortfall is roughly $11.4\% - 4.0\% = 7.4\%$ of premium, about $0.074 \times 80 = \$5.9$ million of annual underwriting result on the current book.
> > - The insurer can refile with better support. Sometimes the gap is evidential rather than political, such as a thinly supported trend or an unadjusted reform.
> > - It can re-underwrite, tightening eligibility so the mix shifts to segments adequately priced at the approved level. This is the mechanism that produces an availability problem and grows the [[Facility Association]].
> > - It can reduce its writings in the province, releasing capital to a jurisdiction where the rate is adequate.
> > - It can absorb the shortfall while the rate lag unwinds, if it believes approvals will catch up.
> > - The actuary may not restate the indication downward to match the approval. The indication is a technical estimate and the approved rate is a regulatory decision. The actuary documents the difference and its effect on the projected [[MCT]] and the [[FCT]] adverse scenarios.

> [!trap] Restating the indication to match the approved rate
> An approved rate below the indication does not make the indication wrong. The indication stays as estimated, and the gap between the two is what the actuary reports.
