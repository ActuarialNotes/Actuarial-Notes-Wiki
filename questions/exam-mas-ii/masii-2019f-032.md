The modeling team is building a new predictive model. While examining their input data, they produce the following scatterplot for analyzing available predictor variables.

![Four-by-four scatterplot matrix of var1, var2, var3 and var4. The var2 against var3 panels show a tight, strongly increasing relationship, while the panels pairing var1 or var4 with the others show diffuse clouds with no clear pattern](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ActuarialNotes/Actuarial-Notes-Wiki/main/Media/Attachments/masii-2019f-q32-scatter-matrix.png)

The team originally fit a model with var2 and var4. The team decides to also include var3 in a refit model.

Identify which of following is the most likely to occur in the refit model.

- A) Model predictions will be inaccurate
- B) Standard errors for var2 will increase
- C) Coefficient estimate for var3 will be negative
- D) Posterior estimates will improve
- E) Interpretation of the coefficients will be straightforward

## Explanation

The scatterplot matrix is being used to spot **multicollinearity**, and the var2-var3 panels show it: the two variables track each other closely, unlike any other pair in the matrix. Adding var3 to a model that already contains var2 therefore introduces a predictor that carries almost the same information.

The characteristic consequence is that the model cannot tell the two apart. It can fit the *combined* effect precisely but not split it between them, so the individual coefficient estimates become unstable and their **standard errors inflate** — which is (B).

Checking the others:

- **(A)** is wrong for the reason that makes multicollinearity so easy to miss: predictions stay accurate. The fitted values barely move; only the attribution of effect between the correlated predictors is damaged.
- **(C)** cannot be read off the plot. The var2-var3 relationship is positive, but the *sign* of a coefficient in the presence of collinearity is unstable and unpredictable — that is part of the problem, not something the plot determines.
- **(D)** is backwards; adding a redundant, collinear predictor degrades the estimates rather than improving them.
- **(E)** is exactly what multicollinearity destroys — "holding the others constant" is meaningless when two predictors move together.

The answer is **B**.
