An **Intentional Tort** is a civil wrong in which the defendant meant to do the act that harmed the plaintiff, and either meant to bring about its consequence or knew it was substantially certain to follow. It differs from [[Negligence]], where the harm is careless rather than intended, and from [[Strict Liability|strict liability]], where no fault is needed at all.

Intent means purpose or substantial certainty about the result, not a wish to harm. A practical joke that causes an offensive contact is still a battery even though the joker meant no harm.

The intentional torts fall into three groups:

1. Torts against persons:
   - *Battery* is an intentional harmful or offensive contact with another person.
   - *Assault* is an act that intentionally puts someone in reasonable apprehension of an imminent battery, with no contact needed.
   - *False imprisonment* is intentionally confining someone within fixed boundaries without consent or legal authority.
   - *Intentional infliction of emotional distress* (IIED) is extreme and outrageous conduct that intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional distress.
2. Torts against property:
   - *Trespass to land* is an intentional entry onto another's land, liable even without damage.
   - *Trespass to chattels* is an interference with someone's personal property, and *conversion* is an interference so serious, such as destroying or selling the property, that the defendant must pay its full value.
3. Economic and dignitary torts:
   - *Defamation* is a false statement of fact, published to a third party, that harms the plaintiff's reputation. It is *libel* when written and *slander* when spoken, and a public figure must also prove actual malice.
   - *Invasion of privacy* takes four forms: intrusion upon seclusion, appropriation of a name or likeness, public disclosure of private facts, and placing someone in a false light.
   - *Fraud* is a knowingly false statement of material fact made to induce reliance, which the plaintiff justifiably relied on to its loss.
   - *Malicious prosecution* is starting criminal or civil proceedings without probable cause and with malice, where the proceedings end in the plaintiff's favour.

*Public disclosure of private facts* is giving publicity to a true matter concerning another's private life when the matter would be highly offensive to a reasonable person and is not of legitimate concern to the public. *False light* is giving publicity to a matter that places another before the public in a false light that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, where the defendant knew the matter was false or acted in reckless disregard of its falsity.

> [!trap] Assault needs no contact
> Assault is the apprehension of an imminent contact, and battery is the contact itself. A punch that lands without the victim seeing it coming is a battery without an assault.

## Defences

*Consent*, express or implied (a boxer, a surgical patient), defeats most intentional torts within its scope. *Self-defence*, *defence of others* and *defence of property* privilege reasonable force, though deadly force is never justified to protect property alone. A shopkeeper may detain a suspected shoplifter for a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner. *Necessity* can excuse a trespass, *legal authority* covers a lawful arrest, and truth and absolute or qualified *privilege* defeat defamation.

## Damages

Battery and trespass to land are actionable without proof of loss, so nominal damages are available. Because the conduct is deliberate, punitive damages are far more common than in negligence cases, and many states don't allow them to be insured.

## Liability insurance for intentional torts

Liability policies, including the [[Commercial General Liability|CGL]]'s Coverage A, the homeowners policy and the personal auto policy, exclude injury the insured expected or intended. Public policy is behind it, since insurance shouldn't let a person buy protection from the cost of hurting someone on purpose. The CGL exclusion doesn't apply to bodily injury from the use of reasonable force to protect persons or property, so a guard who restrains an attacker can still be covered.

CGL Coverage B covers listed offenses rather than accidents, and some of them are intentional by nature. They include false arrest, detention or imprisonment, malicious prosecution, wrongful eviction or entry, libel and slander, and publication that violates a person's right of privacy. Its own exclusions remove offenses committed with knowledge that they would violate another's rights and publications made with knowledge of their falsity, so a defamation the insured believed to be true stays covered. Assault, battery, IIED, conversion and fraud are not on the list. The expected or intended exclusion belongs to Coverage A only.

One potentially covered count usually obliges the insurer to defend the whole suit, even when most of the settlement is excluded ([[Duty to Defend|duty to defend]]).

> [!example]- Which amounts of this nightclub's settlement does its CGL policy pay? {Example}
> A patron sues a nightclub and its bouncer. The club has a standard CGL policy with no assault and battery endorsement, and settles for $\$120{,}000$, allocated as follows:
>
> | Count | Facts | Amount ($) |
> | :--- | :--- | ---: |
> | Battery | The bouncer punched the patron after he had stopped resisting | 50,000 |
> | False imprisonment | He was held in a back room for an hour before police came | 30,000 |
> | Defamation | The club posted online that he was a thief, which the manager believed but was untrue | 25,000 |
> | IIED | Based on the whole episode | 15,000 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The punch came after the threat had ended, so it was not reasonable force to protect persons or property, and the injury was expected or intended. Coverage A excludes the battery.
> > - Detention is a listed Coverage B offense, so the false imprisonment is covered.
> > - Libel is a Coverage B offense, and the knowledge-of-falsity exclusion doesn't apply because the manager believed the statement, so the defamation is covered.
> > - IIED is not a listed offense and the distress was intended, so neither coverage part pays it.
> > - The policy pays $\$30{,}000 + \$25{,}000 = \$55{,}000$, and the club retains $\$120{,}000 - \$55{,}000 = \$65{,}000$.
> > - Because two counts were potentially covered, the insurer had a duty to defend the whole suit, which in most states includes the uncovered counts.
