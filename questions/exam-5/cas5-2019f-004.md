Given the following information for an insurance company that sells claims-made policies:

- Exposure levels are constant.
- Loss costs increase by 3% each report year.
- An equal number of claims are reported each year.
- All claims are reported within four years of occurrence.

| Report Year | Lag 0 | Lag 1 | Lag 2 | Lag 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| 2015 | 103 | 103 | 103 | 103 |
| 2016 | 106 | 106 | 106 | 106 |
| 2017 | 109 | 109 | 109 | 109 |
| 2018 | 113 | 113 | 113 | 113 |

*(Loss Cost by Report Year Lag.)*

## Part a (0.75 points)

Demonstrate and briefly explain why a claims-made policy will cost less than an occurrence policy.

### Answer
Claims-made (2014) = 400 versus occurrence (2014) = 418

### Explanation
**Read the table the two ways the two coverage triggers read it.** The lag is the report lag, so the cell in row $RY$, column $\ell$ is the loss cost of claims *occurring* in year $RY - \ell$ and *reported* in year $RY$.

- A **claims-made** policy is triggered by the report date, so a 2014 claims-made policy picks up everything reported in 2014 — the whole **2014 row**, all four lags.
- An **occurrence** policy is triggered by the occurrence date, so a 2014 occurrence policy picks up everything that *happened* in 2014 no matter when it is reported — the **diagonal** running down and to the right from the 2014 lag-0 cell.

$$\text{Claims-made }2014 = 100 + 100 + 100 + 100 = 400$$

$$\text{Occurrence }2014 = \underbrace{100}_{RY14,\,\ell=0} + \underbrace{103}_{RY15,\,\ell=1} + \underbrace{106}_{RY16,\,\ell=2} + \underbrace{109}_{RY17,\,\ell=3} = 418$$

$$418 > 400$$

**Why.** Every cell in the claims-made row is at the *same* cost level, because a claims-made policy has no report lag beyond the policy term — all four lags are reported in 2014 and therefore priced at 2014 cost levels. The occurrence diagonal, by contrast, reaches forward one report year at a time, so the later-reported pieces are trended one, two and three years further at 3%. With loss costs increasing, the occurrence policy has more time for trend to act on it and costs more.

The same comparison holds in any year — e.g. for 2015, claims-made is $103 \times 4 = 412$ against occurrence $103 + 106 + 109 + 113 = 431$.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate why claims-made policies cost less than occurrence policies; both written and numerical demonstrations of the loss cost difference were accepted. In either form, the response had to recognize that occurrence policies are more affected by increasing loss costs — that is, by positive trend.

Common mistakes were not mentioning the impact of trend at all, and calculating the loss costs incorrectly (typically summing the wrong row or the wrong diagonal).

## Part b (1 points)

Demonstrate and briefly explain whether a claims-made policy or an occurrence policy would be more underpriced if the actual loss cost trend by report year is 10%.

### Answer
The occurrence policy — 464.1 actual versus 418 charged, versus no shortfall on the claims-made policy

### Explanation
The rates were set assuming 3% report-year trend, so the charges are the part (a) figures: **400** for claims-made 2014 and **418** for occurrence 2014. Now re-cost both under an actual 10% report-year trend, holding the 2014 report-year level at 100.

**Claims-made 2014.** All four lags are reported in 2014, so all four sit at the 2014 report-year level regardless of the trend rate:

$$100 \times 4 = 400$$

The charge of 400 is exactly right — the claims-made policy is **not underpriced at all**.

**Occurrence 2014.** The diagonal steps forward one report year per lag, so each step now compounds at 10% instead of 3%:

$$100 + 100(1.10) + 100(1.10)^2 + 100(1.10)^3 = 100 + 110 + 121 + 133.1 = 464.1$$

against a charge of 418:

$$464.1 - 418 = 46.1 \text{ underpriced} \qquad \left(\frac{464.1}{418} - 1 = 11.0\%\right)$$

**The occurrence policy is more underpriced.** A claims-made policy's cost is set entirely by the report year it covers, so a change in the *report-year* trend rate moves the price and the true cost by the same amount. An occurrence policy reaches across four report years, so a trend misestimate compounds over three additional periods and opens a gap.

Alternatively, run the same comparison on 2015, where the claims-made policy is misestimated too but far less so:

| | Charged (3% trend) | Actual (10% trend) | Shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claims-made 2015 | $103 \times 4 = 412$ | $110 \times 4 = 440$ | $-28$ (6.8%) |
| Occurrence 2015 | $103 + 106 + 109 + 113 = 431$ | $110 + 121 + 133.1 + 146.41 = 510.51$ | $-79.51$ (18.4%) |

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to understand how a change in loss trend affects the pricing of claims-made and occurrence policies, and to demonstrate why the occurrence policy ends up more underpriced. Both written and numerical responses comparing the change in loss costs for the two policy types were accepted; the full re-trended table was never required, only the cells needed for the two loss costs.

Common mistakes included applying the 10% trend incorrectly to the report-year/lag table, computing the trended loss costs incorrectly, and failing to compare the two policy types at all — explaining why the occurrence policy is underpriced *or* why the claims-made policy is barely affected, but not both. Candidates who supplied correct 10%-trend loss costs for both did not need both explanations.

## Part c (0.75 points)

Briefly describe one difference between occurrence policies and claims-made policies regarding each of the following:

i. Coverage trigger
ii. Loss development
iii. Investment income

### Explanation
**i. Coverage trigger.** An occurrence policy is triggered by the **date of loss** — coverage attaches if the event occurs during the policy term, whenever it is later reported. A claims-made policy is triggered by the **report date** — coverage attaches if the claim is reported during the policy term (subject to any retroactive date), whatever year the event occurred in.

**ii. Loss development.** An occurrence policy develops more, because it carries both **pure IBNR** (claims that have occurred but not yet been reported) and **IBNER** (development on known claims). A claims-made policy has essentially **no pure IBNR** — every claim it covers is by definition already reported by the end of the policy term — so its only development is IBNER on known claims. Equivalently: the occurrence policy is exposed to report lag *and* settlement lag, the claims-made policy only to settlement lag.

**iii. Investment income.** The occurrence policy earns **more** investment income. The relevant interval is between collecting the premium and paying the claim, and for an occurrence policy that interval includes the report lag on top of the settlement lag, so the funds backing the reserves are held and invested longer.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to describe the difference between occurrence and claims-made policies with respect to coverage trigger, loss development and investment income. The most common error was failing to identify the *underlying cause* of the difference — asserting more or less development, or more or less investment income, without saying why.

Other common mistakes: for the coverage trigger, answering "report year / accident year", or claiming that a claims-made policy requires the loss to both occur and be reported within the policy term. For loss development, stating that claims-made policies have no IBNR without distinguishing pure IBNR from IBNER, claiming claims-made policies have no development beyond the policy term, or simply calling them shorter-tailed without explaining why. For investment income, asserting more time or more reserves without support, or describing the interval as running from claim occurrence to claim payment rather than from premium collection to claim settlement.
