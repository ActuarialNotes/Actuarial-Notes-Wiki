## [F-001] Explanation attributes the inverted exponent to choice (A); it is choice (C)
- entry_type: finding
- author: agent:validate-v1
- run_id: 2026-09-27T19:59Z/bfa2
- date: 2026-09-28
- severity: minor
- status: open
- locus: Explanation, final paragraph (distractor note)
- claim: Choice (A) inverts the exponent, giving $1.5^{1/n}$ instead.
- evidence: SOA Exam P Sample Questions (Aug 2026 rev.) Q383, questions PDF p.154 (page image read): option (A) is the n-th root of 1.5 = 1.5^(1/n); option (C) is the n-th root of (1.5)^(n+1) = 1.5^((n+1)/n). The correct exponent is n/(n+1) (SOA Sample Solutions Q383, solutions PDF p.106: ratio = 1.5^(n/(n+1)), Solution: D). Inverting that exponent gives (n+1)/n, which is option (C), not option (A); 1/n is not the reciprocal of n/(n+1) for any positive integer n (n=1: reciprocal 2, 1/n = 1). The SOA solution does not discuss distractors, so the vault sentence is unsourced and wrong about which option the inverted exponent produces. The keyed answer D is unaffected.
- source_rank: 1
- proposed_action: Human edit of explanation prose: attribute the inverted exponent (n+1)/n to choice (C); if choice (A) is to be explained, give a mechanism that actually yields 1.5^(1/n) (e.g. taking F(x) = x^n).

## [F-002] Explanation claims the normalizing constant is needed for the exponent; it is not
- entry_type: finding
- author: agent:validate-v1
- run_id: 2026-09-27T19:59Z/bfa2
- date: 2026-09-28
- severity: minor
- status: open
- locus: Explanation, final paragraph (note on c)
- claim: The normalizing constant $c$ cancels out of the ratio entirely, but it is still needed to get the exponent $(n+1)/n$ on $x_p$ right.
- evidence: For any constant c, the integral of c x^(1/n) from 0 to x_p is (c n/(n+1)) x_p^((n+1)/n): the exponent (n+1)/n comes from the power rule alone. Writing p = k x_p^((n+1)/n) for an arbitrary k > 0 gives x_p = (p/k)^(n/(n+1)) and x_0.3/x_0.2 = (0.3/0.2)^(n/(n+1)) = 1.5^(n/(n+1)) whatever k is, which is option (D) and the SOA key (SOA Sample Solutions Q383, solutions PDF p.106, Solution: D). The normalizing constant affects neither the exponent nor the ratio, so the sentence teaches a dependency that does not exist. Answer unaffected.
- source_rank: 5
- proposed_action: Human edit of explanation prose: delete the clause "but it is still needed to get the exponent (n+1)/n on x_p right", or say instead that c is not needed for the ratio at all.
