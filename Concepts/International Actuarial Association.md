The **International Actuarial Association** (IAA) is the worldwide association of national actuarial associations. Its members are associations such as the [[Canadian Institute of Actuaries (CIA)|CIA]], the SOA and the CAS, not individual actuaries, so an actuary is disciplined by their own association and never by the IAA. The IAA sets the **professionalism principles** a full member association must meet, publishes model standards of practice (ISAPs), and publishes educational material such as the IAA Risk Book.

A full member association (FMA) must have:

1. A code of professional conduct.
2. A formal discipline process.
3. A formal due process for standards of practice, if it adopts standards.
4. Education that complies with the IAA Education Guidelines and Syllabus.

It must also meet minimum size and age requirements. These requirements are what make an actuary's credential from one FMA mean something in another country.

## Codes of conduct and professionalism

FMAs' codes must contain a common core of general principles and ethical standards. One such principle is that members comply with the applicable qualification and practice standards. Actuaries are bound by their own association's code and may also be subject to the code and standards of the country where they practise.

The IAA Professionalism Committee's guideline PG1 groups the high-level principles under knowledge and expertise, values and behaviour, and professional accountability. Qualification standards cover initial education, continuing professional development and experience.

## Discipline

The IAA accredits a discipline process that meets five criteria:

1. A complaint process open to anyone affected by an actuary's work and to the actuary's peers.
2. Due process of defence for the actuary complained against.
3. An independent formal appeal.
4. Sanctions that fit the seriousness of the violation, up to termination of membership.
5. Public notice of the result when a penalty is imposed.

> [!example]- How many of the IAA's five discipline criteria does this applicant association's scheme meet, and what must change? {Example}
> A national actuarial association applies for full membership. Its discipline scheme lets only members file complaints. A panel of the governing council hears each case and can issue a private reprimand at most, and appeals go back to the same council. The actuary complained against may present a defence, and the outcome is published when a penalty is imposed.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Only members can complain, so complaints are not open to anyone affected: not met.
> > - The actuary may present a defence: due process is met.
> > - Appeals go back to the council that ruled, so the appeal is not independent: not met.
> > - A private reprimand is the most it can impose, so sanctions do not reach termination of membership: not met.
> > - Outcomes are published when a penalty is imposed: public notice is met.
> > - The scheme meets **2 of the 5** criteria. It must let clients and other affected parties complain, have appeals heard by a body independent of the one that ruled, and allow sanctions up to expulsion before it has the formal discipline process full membership requires.

## Standards, law and educational material

Standards are usually principle-based. They say what the actuary should consider, do and disclose, and they allow a departure that is justified, identified and explained; an unexplained departure is a breach. Regulations are more prescriptive, and where the two conflict the law governs. In order of authority:

1. Law.
2. Applicable standards of practice.
3. Educational material.

International Actuarial Notes, the Risk Book chapters and monographs are educational. An actuary may use them or not, and they do not bind anyone.

## ISAPs as model standards

ISAP 1, General Actuarial Practice, applies to actuarial work generally, and ISAP 4 covers [[IFRS 17]] insurance contracts. An ISAP binds an actuary only when either:

1. A local standard-setter adopts it, bases a standard on it, or declares its standards substantially consistent with it, or
2. The actuary states compliance with it in a report.

> [!example]- Are the ISAPs binding on this actuary, and which issues are material? {Example}
> An actuary works in a country whose standard-setter has not adopted any ISAP. A client asks the actuary to state compliance with ISAP 1 and ISAP 4 in a report on IFRS 17 liabilities of $\$480$ million. The actuary sets materiality at 1% of the liabilities and finds two issues, both increasing the liabilities: a data omission worth $\$1.5$ million and a discount-curve error worth $\$6.0$ million.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The actuary states compliance with the ISAPs, so they bind even though no local standard-setter adopted them.
> > - The threshold is $0.01 \times 480 = \$4.8$ million.
> > - The $\$6.0$ million error is material on its own and must be corrected or disclosed.
> > - The $\$1.5$ million omission is below the threshold alone, but the two together are $\$7.5$ million, so the actuary considers their combined effect rather than dismissing the smaller one.
> > - [[Materiality]] is judged from the intended user's point of view, so a 1% threshold is a starting point, not a rule.

> [!trap] Treating an ISAP as binding because it exists
> An ISAP is a model. It binds through local adoption or through the actuary's own statement of compliance, and not otherwise.
