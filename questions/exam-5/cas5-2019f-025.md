Given the following information for a company:

| Value | Item |
|---|---|
| 5.00 | 12-Ultimate Gross Paid Claims Development Factor |
| 3.30 | 24-Ultimate Gross Paid Claims Development Factor |
| 12,000,000 | Accident Year 2018 Paid Gross Claims @ 12 Months |

| Incremental Gross Industry Payment Pattern for 12 to 24 Months, by Quarter | 12-15 | 15-18 | 18-21 | 21-24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Percent Paid | 50% | 35% | 10% | 5% |

| Value | Item |
|---|---|
| 1,450,000 | Accident Year 2018 Actual Net Paid Claims for 15-18 Months |

- The company maintains a quota share reinsurance agreement where they cede 30% of the business.
- Ultimate losses are estimated using the paid development technique.

## Part a (1.25 points)

Calculate the accident year 2018 expected net paid claims for the period 15 to 18 months, based on the following:

i) claims emerge uniformly between evaluation points.

ii) the industry payment pattern.

### Answer
i. $1,081,818; ii. $1,514,545

### Explanation
**Step 1 — expected gross payments over the full 12-to-24 month interval.** Develop the 12-month paid claims to ultimate, then work back to the expected paid at 24 months:

$$\text{Ultimate gross} = 12{,}000{,}000 \times 5.00 = \$60{,}000{,}000$$

$$\text{Expected paid @ 24 months} = \frac{60{,}000{,}000}{3.30} = \$18{,}181{,}818$$

$$\text{Expected gross paid 12--24} = 18{,}181{,}818 - 12{,}000{,}000 = \$6{,}181{,}818$$

Equivalently, in one step:

$$12{,}000{,}000 \times \left(\frac{5.00}{3.30} - 1\right) = \$6{,}181{,}818$$

**Step 2 — convert to a net basis.** The quota share cedes 30%, so the company retains 70%. Because a quota share is proportional, the same 70% applies to losses as to premium:

$$\text{Expected net paid 12--24} = 6{,}181{,}818 \times 0.70 = \$4{,}327{,}273$$

**Step 3 — allocate to the 15-to-18 month quarter.**

**i) Uniform emergence.** The 12-to-24 month interval contains four quarters, so each receives an equal $25\%$:

$$6{,}181{,}818 \times 0.70 \times 0.25 = \$1{,}081{,}818$$

**ii) Industry payment pattern.** The 15-to-18 month quarter carries $35\%$ of the interval's payments:

$$6{,}181{,}818 \times 0.70 \times 0.35 = \$1{,}514{,}545$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the projected net payments for the 15-to-18 month period under both assumptions — uniform emergence and the industry emergence pattern within the 12-to-24 month interval. Common mistakes included not applying the 70% quota share retention, calculating the emergence for the wrong period (for example 12-to-15 months), and giving a response under only one of the two assumptions.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Recommend whether to change the net estimated unpaid based on the actual results for accident year 2018 in the 15-to-18 month period.

### Explanation
**Recommendation: do not change the net estimated unpaid.**

Comparing actual to expected against each assumption:

| Assumption | Expected | Actual | Actual / Expected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform emergence | 1,081,818 | 1,450,000 | $1.340$ |
| Industry pattern | 1,514,545 | 1,450,000 | $0.957$ |

The industry pattern is the better of the two assumptions on its own merits: payments within a year are front-loaded rather than spread evenly (50% of the interval's payments fall in the first quarter alone), so uniform quarterly emergence is not a realistic description of how claims are paid. Judged against that more appropriate benchmark, actual emergence of \$1.45M against \$1.51M expected is a shortfall of only about **4%** — well within the variation to be expected from a single quarter of data.

Two further considerations point the same way. First, at 12 months the paid development factor is 5.00, so the estimate is **highly leveraged**: small differences in payment timing at this immaturity translate into large swings in the projected ultimate, and reacting to them would make the estimate unstable. Second, the emergence pattern is an **industry** pattern, not the company's own; differences in policy types, claim settlement practices and mix mean it will never match the company exactly, so a small deviation is not evidence that the underlying estimate is wrong.

A change would be warranted only if the discrepancy could be traced to something identifiable that has genuinely changed since the projection was made — a large loss or a significant recovery, say — rather than to ordinary variation around an imperfect benchmark.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recommend and justify whether to change the unpaid projection, supporting the recommendation by comparing actual to expected and evaluating the appropriateness of the uniform and industry assumptions. Common mistakes included giving a recommendation with no justification, and citing changes in case reserve adequacy — irrelevant here, since the question is built entirely on paid data.
