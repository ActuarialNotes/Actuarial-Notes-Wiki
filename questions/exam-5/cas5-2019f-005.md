Given the following to be used in developing a rate indication effective January 1, 2021:

- All policies are annual.
- Rates are expected to be in effect for one year.
- The selected annual loss trend is 2%.

## Part a (0.5 points)

Calculate the loss trend factor applied to losses from accident year 2018.

### Answer
1.072

### Explanation
A loss trend factor runs from the **average date of loss in the experience period** to the **average date of loss in the period being priced** — not from average written or average earned dates.

**Trend-from date.** Accident year 2018 runs 1/1/2018 - 12/31/2018 and, with losses occurring uniformly, its average loss date is

$$7/1/2018$$

**Trend-to date.** Rates take effect 1/1/2021 and stay in effect one year, so policies are written from 1/1/2021 through 12/31/2021 — average written date $7/1/2021$. Each policy is annual, so a loss on a given policy occurs on average six months after it is written:

$$7/1/2021 + 6 \text{ months} = 1/1/2022$$

**Trend period and factor.**

$$7/1/2018 \longrightarrow 1/1/2022 = 3.5 \text{ years}$$

$$\text{Loss trend factor} = 1.02^{3.5} = 1.0718 \approx 1.072$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge of loss trends, in particular the dates used for trending, and the relationship between loss trend and loss development.

Candidates were expected to indicate the trend dates leading to a trend period, which should have been incorporated with the loss trend given (2%) to come to the loss trend factor.

Common mistakes included:

- Trending to the average written date of the prospective period (7/1/2021) instead of the average loss date.
- Stating that the trend is calculated from average earned dates instead of average loss dates.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Calculate the loss trend factor applied to losses from policy year 2018.

### Answer
1.061

### Explanation
Only the trend-*from* date changes; the trend-to date is the same prospective average loss date, $1/1/2022$, as in part (a).

**Trend-from date.** Policy year 2018 is the annual policies written during calendar 2018, so the last one expires 12/31/2019 and the exposure period spans two full years, 1/1/2018 - 12/31/2019. Written evenly, the average written date is $7/1/2018$; a loss on an annual policy occurs on average six months after writing:

$$7/1/2018 + 6 \text{ months} = 1/1/2019$$

Note this is six months later than the accident year 2018 average loss date — a policy year's losses are, on average, half a policy term more recent than the same-numbered accident year's.

**Trend period and factor.**

$$1/1/2019 \longrightarrow 1/1/2022 = 3 \text{ years}$$

$$\text{Loss trend factor} = 1.02^{3} = 1.0612 \approx 1.061$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to indicate the trend dates leading to a trend period, which should have been incorporated with the loss trend given (2%) to come to the loss trend factor. Indicating that the trend end date was the same as part (a) was acceptable.

Common mistakes included:

- Trending to the average written date of the prospective period (7/1/2021) instead of the average loss date.
- Stating that the trend is calculated from average earned dates instead of average loss dates
- Incorrectly calculating the policy year 2018 average loss date or indicating that the average policy year loss date is the same as the average accident year loss date in part (a).

## Part c (0.5 points)

Explain why trending and developing losses do not result in overlapping adjustments.

### Explanation
The two adjustments move losses along **different axes**, so neither one does any part of the other's work.

- **Development** moves losses *forward in maturity within a fixed experience period*. It takes the reported or paid losses for a given accident (or policy) year at the current valuation and projects them to their **ultimate** value — the amount that will finally be paid once every claim from that year is reported and closed. It answers "how much more of this year's losses is still to come?"

- **Trending** moves losses *forward in time from one cost level to another*. It takes the ultimate losses of a past period and restates them at the **cost level of the future policy period being priced**, reflecting inflation and other changes in frequency and severity between the two periods. It answers "what would this year's losses cost if they happened during the period I am rating?"

Because development changes the *valuation* of a fixed year's losses and trending changes the *cost level* those (already ultimate) losses are expressed in, the two act on different dimensions and cannot double-count.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain loss trend and loss development. Stating that trend and development do not overlap was not necessary, as this was given in the question.

Common mistakes included:

- Stating that trending and developing ensures rates are at the correct level.
- Stating that development is done on already trended losses or trending is done on already developed losses.
- Stating that development involves IBNR and trend involves inflation without further detail on why there is not overlap
- Stating that trend evaluates an on-level ultimate.
- Describing trend as moving vertically through the loss triangle and development as moving horizontally through the loss triangle without sufficient additional explanation.
