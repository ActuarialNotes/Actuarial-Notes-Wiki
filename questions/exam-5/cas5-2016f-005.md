An insurance company purchases per risk excess-of-loss reinsurance each year that covers individual claims that exceed the retention.

Given the following information as of December 31, 2015:

| Accident Year | Earned Exposures | Direct Ultimate Losses (\$000) | Claim Counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 1,850 | 185,000 | 185 |
| 2014 | 1,750 | 190,000 | 175 |
| 2015 | 1,650 | 199,500 | 165 |

Ultimate Value of Direct Claims Excess of \$500,000:

| Accident Year | Claim | Direct Ultimate Loss of Individual Claims (\$000) |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | A | 18,400 |
| 2013 | B | 3,200 |
| 2014 | C | 5,700 |
| 2014 | D | 5,200 |
| 2015 | E | 9,500 |
| 2015 | F | 6,200 |

| Accident Year | Retention (\$000) |
|---|---|
| 2013 | 2,000 |
| 2014 | 5,000 |
| 2015 | 10,000 |

- Policies are annual.
- Policies are written uniformly throughout the year.
- Rates are expected to be in effect for one year.
- Planned rate revision to be effective January 1, 2017.

Calculate the average trended pure premium net of reinsurance at the current \$10,000,000 retention.

### Answer
148.99 (\$000) per exposure, i.e. \$148,990 per earned exposure

### Explanation
The key structural point: the reinsurance program to price against is the **current** \$10,000,000 retention, not the historical retentions of \$2,000,000 and \$5,000,000 that actually applied in 2013 and 2014. And the retention must be applied to **trended** claim values, since a claim that sits below \$10,000,000 today may exceed it once inflated to the cost level of the policy period. That ordering — trend first, then cap — is what makes the calculation work.

**Step 1 — severity trend from the direct data.** Frequency is flat, so all of the pure premium trend is severity:

| AY | Frequency | Severity (\$000) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | $185/1{,}850 = 0.100$ | $185{,}000/185 = 1{,}000.00$ | — |
| 2014 | $175/1{,}750 = 0.100$ | $190{,}000/175 = 1{,}085.71$ | $+8.6\%$ |
| 2015 | $165/1{,}650 = 0.100$ | $199{,}500/165 = 1{,}209.09$ | $+11.4\%$ |

Frequency is identically 0.100 in all three years, so the frequency trend is 0%. The two severity changes average about 10%, so select a **10% severity trend** (and therefore a 10% pure premium trend).

Note that the trend is measured on **direct** (gross) severity. Netting the excess out first and then trending the "net" severity would be wrong — it would fit a trend to a series that has already been distorted by three different retentions.

**Step 2 — the trend period.** Rates are effective January 1, 2017 and in effect one year, so the average written date is July 1, 2017; policies are annual, so the average accident date of the forecast period is

$$7/1/2017 + 0.5\text{ yr} = 1/1/2018$$

From each historical year's average accident date of July 1: 4.5 years for 2013, 3.5 for 2014, 2.5 for 2015.

**Step 3 — trend the large claims and cap each one individually at \$10,000.**

| AY | Claim | Direct (\$000) | Trend factor | Trended (\$000) | Excess of 10,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | A | 18,400 | $1.10^{4.5} = 1.5356$ | 28,254 | 18,254 |
| 2013 | B | 3,200 | $1.10^{4.5} = 1.5356$ | 4,914 | 0 |
| 2014 | C | 5,700 | $1.10^{3.5} = 1.3960$ | 7,957 | 0 |
| 2014 | D | 5,200 | $1.10^{3.5} = 1.3960$ | 7,259 | 0 |
| 2015 | E | 9,500 | $1.10^{2.5} = 1.2691$ | 12,056 | 2,056 |
| 2015 | F | 6,200 | $1.10^{2.5} = 1.2691$ | 7,868 | 0 |

Claim E is the reason the trend-then-cap ordering matters: at \$9,500 thousand it is *below* the \$10,000 thousand retention as reported, but at the cost level of the policy period it pierces it by \$2,056 thousand.

**Step 4 — trend direct losses, remove the trended excess, divide by exposures.**

| AY | Direct (\$000) | Trend factor | Trended direct | Less excess | Trended net | Exposures | Net pure premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 185,000 | 1.5356 | 284,079 | 18,254 | 265,825 | 1,850 | 143.69 |
| 2014 | 190,000 | 1.3960 | 265,233 | 0 | 265,233 | 1,750 | 151.56 |
| 2015 | 199,500 | 1.2691 | 253,177 | 2,056 | 251,121 | 1,650 | 152.19 |
| **Total** | | | | | **782,179** | **5,250** | **148.99** |

**Step 5 — the average.** Taking the total trended net losses over the total exposures:

$$\text{Average trended net pure premium} = \frac{782{,}179}{5{,}250} = \mathbf{148.99 \text{ (\$000)}}$$

That is \$148,990 per earned exposure. *Alternatively*, a straight (unweighted) average of the three yearly pure premiums gives $(143.69 + 151.56 + 152.19)/3 = 149.15$; the exposure-weighted figure above is the one the published sample carries.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to select and apply a severity trend, identify the trending period from the effective date and annual policy term, trend the large claims and take the excess over the *current* retention claim by claim, subtract that excess from trended direct losses, and divide by exposures to reach a net pure premium.

Common errors included removing the untrended excess from direct losses before computing average severity and then trending the resulting "net" losses — which misses that a claim close to but under the retention can pierce it after trend; using gross average severity to fit the trend but netting out the excess before applying that trend; and aggregating the large claims within an accident year and applying the \$10,000,000 retention to the aggregate instead of to each individual claim, which is not what a per risk excess-of-loss treaty does.
