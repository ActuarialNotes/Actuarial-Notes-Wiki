## The mechanics

- **Base scenario** — the insurer's business plan, on assumptions consistent with the plan and with recent experience. The base scenario is not conservative; it is the expected path.
- **Adverse scenarios** — plausible but severe. Each is assigned a **percentile ranking**, and the actuary must be able to justify it. Plausibility is the constraint that distinguishes FCT from a pure stress test: a scenario nobody believes is not informative.
- **[[Ripple Effect|Ripple effects]]** — the consequences that follow *automatically* from the scenario. An inflation shock does not only raise claim severity; it changes the discount rate, the reinsurance cost, the required capital, and policyholder behaviour. Failing to model ripple effects is the standard way an FCT understates a scenario.
- **Corrective management actions** — what management would *do* in the scenario (raise rates, cut dividends, buy reinsurance, raise capital). They are permitted, but must be **classified** and disclosed separately from **routine** actions already in the base plan, and they must be actions management would realistically take and could take in the time available.
- **The opinion.** The actuary reports whether the insurer's financial condition is **satisfactory** — the future financial condition is satisfactory if, over the forecast period, the insurer meets its regulatory capital requirement under the base scenario and remains solvent under all plausible adverse scenarios.

## FCT versus ORSA

FCT is the **actuary's** exercise under a professional standard, reported to the board; **[[ORSA]]** is **management's** exercise under [[OSFI ORSA|OSFI Guideline E-19]], covering all risks and setting the internal capital target. They overlap heavily and §4 addresses producing an integrated report — but the accountability and the standard behind each are different, which is exactly what an exam question tests.
