The **History of Solvency Regulation** in the U.S. is how state oversight of insurers' financial strength moved from fixed rules to a uniform, risk-focused system built after the insolvencies of the 1980s, and then to the own-risk tools of the NAIC's Solvency Modernization Initiative after 2008. Throughout, the U.S. system has aimed to minimise the number and impact of insolvencies, not to eliminate them.

The history falls into three eras:

1. **To the 1980s:** fixed capital minima, uniform annual statements and periodic examinations.
2. **1989–2001:** accreditation, [[Risk-Based Capital|RBC]] and codified [[Statutory Accounting Principles|SAP]].
3. **From 2008:** ORSA, governance disclosure and group supervision.

## The fixed-rule era

State statutes set minimum capital and surplus for incorporation and licensing, higher for casualty and multi-line writers. They required annual statements on the NAIC's uniform blank and periodic examinations. The minima did not vary with an insurer's size or risk, and they still exist beneath everything that followed.

The NAIC's [[IRIS Ratios|IRIS]] ratios have screened insurers since the mid-1970s. In the same period post-assessment guaranty associations, built on a 1969 NAIC model, spread across the states. They made surviving insurers the backstop for a failed insurer's covered claims.

## The 1980s crisis and *Failed Promises*

Large P&C failures in the mid-to-late 1980s (Transit, Mission, Integrity) led to hearings chaired by Rep. John Dingell and the 1990 report *Failed Promises*. It found that states were not reviewing the filings they received, were understaffed, had no requirement to examine insurers and did not coordinate. It also floated federal regulation.

## The reforms of 1989–2001

The NAIC adopted **Financial Regulation Standards** in 1989 and the **Accreditation Program** in 1990. A state insurance department is accredited only if its laws, resources and procedures meet baseline standards, and an independent team re-reviews it every five years. Every state is now accredited.

[[Risk-Based Capital]] followed in the early 1990s (the health formula in 1998), with a model act giving regulators automatic authority to intervene. The FAST analysis tools and peer review of nationally significant insurers came in the same decade. **Codification** of [[Statutory Accounting Principles]] took effect January 1, 2001.

> [!example]- Which reform answered each of these solvency problems? {Example}
> *Failed Promises* made four findings, and a fifth problem was widely recognised:
>
> 1. Filings were not being read.
> 2. Departments were understaffed.
> 3. There was no examination requirement.
> 4. States did not coordinate.
> 5. Capital minima were blind to risk.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Unread filings were answered by systematic off-site analysis: IRIS and the FAST scoring tools to prioritise, a *Financial Analysis Handbook* for quarterly review, and NAIC peer review of nationally significant insurers.
> > - Understaffing was answered by accreditation standards requiring adequate resources and qualified staff, not just adequate laws.
> > - The missing examination requirement was answered by an accreditation standard requiring a full-scope financial examination of every domestic insurer at least once every five years.
> > - The lack of coordination was answered by letting other states rely on an *accredited* domiciliary regulator, by the lead-state concept and by shared NAIC databases.
> > - Flat capital minima were answered by RBC, sized to each insurer's asset, credit and underwriting risk, with a legislated intervention ladder.
> > - Together these kept solvency regulation with the states, leaving Congress no gap to fill.

## Risk-focused supervision after Enron

The Model Audit Rule was revised on the [[Sarbanes-Oxley]] pattern, effective 2010. Examinations and analysis became **risk-focused**: they assess what could go wrong and how management controls it, rather than re-verifying balances ([[Solvency Monitoring]]).

## The Solvency Modernization Initiative

From 2008 the Solvency Modernization Initiative reviewed capital, governance and risk management, group supervision, statutory accounting and reinsurance in the light of the crisis and international standards. Its outputs include:

- the ORSA model, adopted 2012 and effective 2015;
- the Form F enterprise risk report, from the 2010 holding-company amendments;
- the Corporate Governance Annual Disclosure (2014);
- a group capital calculation;
- reinsurance collateral reform ([[Insurance Governance]]).
