A public policy white paper (July 2019) from a work group of the American Academy of Actuaries' Financial Reporting Committee on the FASB requirement, ASU 2016-13, for the uncollectible reinsurance reserve (URR) of property/casualty companies. It describes the new rules, how they may differ from current practice, the methods that might be used to estimate the URR, the issues in applying them, and the actuarial standards of practice that may apply.

## 1 What is the issue?
- A new FASB requirement, ASU 2016-13, takes effect in 2020 for certain filers of U.S. GAAP statements and the following year for others; it sets new rules for determining the uncollectible reinsurance reserve and creates new disclosure requirements.
- The collectability of ceded balances can be a material part of a company's solvency and risk evaluations, as reflected in the required comment on reinsurance collectability in P&C actuarial opinions and in the ceded reinsurance credit risk charge in the NAIC risk-based capital formula.
- P&C companies carry a URR as an offset to their ceded reserve balances, for both GAAP and U.S. statutory reporting, but accounting guidance has led to different approaches and measurement objectives.

## 2 What are the new rules?
- The new FASB rules require that uncollectible reinsurance reserves related to credit risk (inability to pay) be set on expected ultimate uncollectible amounts, akin to IFRS 17, Solvency II and various market-consistent approaches.
- The URR addresses uncollectible amounts due to both credit risk and dispute risk, but the new rules apply only to the credit risk portion, so some companies may use an expected ultimate approach for credit risk and a known-impairment approach for dispute risk.

## 3 What is current practice?
- Individual companies generally use the same approach for the URR in their U.S. GAAP and U.S. statutory filings, but the approach varies across the industry.

## 5 Estimation methods
- Definition of 'reinsurance write-off'
    - Write-off histories for one insurer may not be directly comparable to those of another solely because of definitional issues.
- Two methods
    - Experience-based methods use historical reinsurance write-offs and can consider write-offs related to both credit and dispute; rating-based methods use the financial strength ratings of reinsurers and address credit only.
- Rating-based method
    - How is the cumulative probability of default by rating estimated?
    - Transition matrices
    - How is the URR provision for disputes treated in a rating-based method?
- Experience-based method
    - Overall concept
    - Line of business
    - Ongoing versus runoff business
    - Maturity of the reinsurance recoverables
