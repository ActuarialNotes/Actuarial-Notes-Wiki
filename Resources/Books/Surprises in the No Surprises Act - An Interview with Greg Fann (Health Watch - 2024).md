The first part of an interview in the September 2024 issue of the Society of Actuaries Health Section's Health Watch, in which Kristi Bohn asks Greg Fann about the No Surprises Act (NSA). It covers what the Act is and its objectives, how it protects consumers from surprise medical bills, how an in-network price is set, its "baseball" arbitration, and early evidence of its effect.

## What the Act Is and When It Took Effect
- The No Surprises Act was passed as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021 to protect consumers from surprise medical bills, particularly those from emergency services or out-of-network providers at in-network facilities.
- Its primary objectives include a fair payment process between insurers and medical providers, patients responsible only for in-network cost-sharing amounts, and prevention of balance billing in certain situations.
- The law became effective on January 1, 2022, and its independent dispute resolution (IDR) process in April 2022.

## How the Act Protects Consumers
- It prohibits balance billing for emergency services and certain nonemergency services from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities.
- Three categories are protected: most emergency services, including post-stabilization, from an out-of-network facility; nonemergency services from an out-of-network provider at an in-network facility; and out-of-network air ambulance services.
- Ground ambulance services are not protected.
- In most cases a qualifying payment amount (QPA) applies, the median contracted rate that a health plan pays to providers in the same geographic area, adjusted for some qualitative factors.

## Why Surprise Bills Arise
- Surprises arise when enrollees receive services, sometimes unknowingly, from providers not in the network and without a prearranged payment amount.
- Out-of-network bills were high because providers can charge more without a contract, insurers often require greater cost sharing, and insurers may limit their allowed amount so the consumer receives a balance bill for the difference.
- Earlier state-level regulations and insurer negotiations were limited in scope and effectiveness.

## Evidence on the Act's Effect
- Evidence is still emerging; early evidence suggests the Act has reduced surprise medical bills and patient financial burdens, though comprehensive data are not yet conclusive.

## Baseball Arbitration
- Two parties submit their best "fair" amount and the arbitrator must choose one offer without modification.
- The NSA's arbitration involves randomized arbitrators and limits the consideration of evidence to data-based benchmarks, unlike the varied state-regulated arbitration.

## Compliance, Ambiguity and Regulatory Change
- Providers and insurers should familiarize themselves with the Act's requirements, implement clear billing procedures, comply with billing and disclosure rules, and negotiate disputes in good faith.
- Potential loopholes include network adequacy, balance billing in nonemergency scenarios and compliance challenges in arbitration.

## The Surprises in the Act
- The number of disputed payments is about 14 times what was projected.
- The IDR process holds the most remaining uncertainty and is left to the next segment of the interview.
