Given the following information:

| Size of Loss | \$100,000 Limit Claims | \$100,000 Limit Losses | \$250,000 Limit Claims | \$250,000 Limit Losses | \$500,000 Limit Claims | \$500,000 Limit Losses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $X \leq \$100{,}000$ | 100 | \$8,000,000 | 35 | \$1,800,000 | 35 | \$1,800,000 |
| $\$100{,}000 < X \leq \$250{,}000$ | | | 40 | \$7,400,000 | 25 | \$3,900,000 |
| $\$250{,}000 < X \leq \$500{,}000$ | | | | | 15 | \$5,200,000 |

| Limit | Indicated Factor (pure premium generalized linear model analysis) |
|---|---|
| \$100,000 | 1.00 |
| \$250,000 | 0.95 |
| \$500,000 | 1.15 |

For the \$250,000 policy limit:

## Part a (1.25 points)

Calculate the indicated increased limits factor, assuming a basic limit of \$100,000.

### Answer

**Indicated ILF(\$250,000) = 1.599**

The ILF is the ratio of the limited average severity at \$250,000 to the limited average severity at the basic limit, \$100,000, building the higher limit up layer by layer.

**Limited average severity at \$100,000** — every claim, from policies of every limit, contributes to the basic layer (100 + 35 + 40 + 35 + 25 + 15 = 250 claims):

$$\text{LAS}(\$100{,}000) = \frac{\$8{,}000{,}000 + \$1{,}800{,}000 + \$1{,}800{,}000 + (40 + 25 + 15) \times \$100{,}000}{250} = \frac{\$19{,}600{,}000}{250} = \$78{,}400$$

**Average loss in the layer \$100,000 – \$250,000** — only policies with limits of at least \$250,000 can show losses in this layer, so the layer is measured on their claims (35 + 40 + 35 + 25 + 15 = 150):

$$\text{Layer losses} = (\$7{,}400{,}000 - 40 \times \$100{,}000) + (\$3{,}900{,}000 - 25 \times \$100{,}000) + 15 \times \$150{,}000 = \$7{,}050{,}000$$

$$\text{Average layer loss per claim} = \frac{\$7{,}050{,}000}{150} = \$47{,}000$$

$$\text{ILF}(\$250{,}000) = \frac{\$78{,}400 + \$47{,}000}{\$78{,}400} = \frac{\$125{,}400}{\$78{,}400} = 1.599$$

### Examiner Report

The majority of candidates scored well on this part. This question involved a straightforward calculation.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Explain the difference between the indicated increased limits factor calculated in part a. above and the generalized linear model results.

### Answer

The traditional empirical ILF calculated in part a assumes that claim frequency is the same regardless of policy limit — it treats the loss development from each limit group as coming from a homogeneous population. The GLM, by contrast, does not assume uniform frequency across limits. It models behavioral differences among insureds who choose different policy limits (e.g., higher-limit policyholders may be more risk-averse, may have different claim-filing propensities, or may be a systematically different risk profile). These frequency/behavioral differences can cause the GLM-indicated factors to diverge from the empirically-calculated factors — and can even produce counterintuitive results such as a factor below 1.00 at an intermediate limit (here, 0.95 at \$250k).

### Examiner Report

Almost half of all candidates received full credit on this part for indicating that, unlike the traditional ILF approach used in part a., the GLM approach does not assume frequency is the same for all limits, or that the GLM approach recognizes behavioral differences among insureds at different limits.

A small group of candidates received partial credit for indicating that the GLM approach considers correlations between rating variables or for making a less-than-fully-formulated attempt to explain that the GLM method is influenced by frequency or behavioral differences.

A large group of candidates received no credit for stating that the GLM-indicated ILF considers all variables simultaneously , considers other variables, considers correlation between limits, is distorted due to low volume, is distorted due to low credibility, considers variability in higher layers, etc. While these statements may be true, they are not the correct explanation for the difference between the two indicated ILFs in this problem. Certain arguments, such as sparse data and low credibility were pointed out to critique one of the methods without addressing that this issue would in fact impact both methods and addressing the degree to which each of the methods would be impacted. Some candidates assumed the GLM used a curve-fitting procedure and thus better dealt with sparse data, which does not appear consistent with the indicated ILFs from the GLM.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Select an increased limits factor and briefly explain the rationale for the selection.

### Answer

**Selection: 1.599 (the empirically calculated ILF from part a)**

The GLM-indicated factor of 0.95 for the \$250,000 limit is counterintuitive and unreasonable: it implies the insured should be charged less for a higher policy limit than the basic \$100,000 limit. The GLM produces a reversal/discontinuity at \$250,000. The empirical factor of 1.599 is consistent with the expected pattern that ILFs increase (at a decreasing rate) as limits rise. Therefore, the traditionally-calculated ILF of 1.599 is the more defensible selection.

### Examiner Report

The majority of candidates received full credit on this part, providing both an acceptable selection and rationale for that selection. Candidates received full credit for selecting the traditionally-calculated ILF and citing that the GLM-indicated ILF results in a counterintuitive/unreasonable/nonsensical factor relative to the factors for the \$100K and \$500K limit, or in discontinuities/reversals. However, candidates who selected the GLM-indicated factor, due to its recognition of frequency or behavioral differences, its sophistication/comprehensiveness, or its recognition of correlations between rating variables also received full credit. Similarly, weightings of the two, if appropriately determined, were awarded full credit.

Another large group of candidates received partial credit, typically for providing a reasonable selection as discussed above, though without any supporting explanation, or with inapplicable support.
