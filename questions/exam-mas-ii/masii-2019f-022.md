Your coworker built a linear model predicting a random variable $Y$:

$$
\begin{align*}
Y_i &\sim \text{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma) \\
\mu_i &= \alpha + \beta_{x_1}\log(x_{1i}) + \beta_{x_{2i}}x_2 \\
\alpha &\sim \text{Normal}(100, 100) \\
\beta_{x_1} &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 5) \\
\beta_{x_{2i}} &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 5) \\
\sigma &\sim \text{Uniform}(0, 100)
\end{align*}
$$

You are given additional detail about pre-processing performed on the predictor variables:

- All variables are continuous numeric
- $x_{1i}$ is strictly positive
- Variable $x_{2i}$ has been standardized to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1

Your coworker gives you the following three statements:

I. $\alpha$ = average outcome
II. $\beta_{x_1}$ = change in outcome for each 1 log change of $x_{1i}$
III. $\beta_{x_2}$ = change in outcome for each 1 standard deviation change of $x_{2i}$

Determine which of the above statements are false.

- A) I only
- B) II only
- C) III only
- D) I, II and III
- E) The answer is not given by (A), (B), (C), or (D)

## Explanation

Note that the question asks which statements are **false**.

The intercept is the expected outcome when *every* predictor term is zero, so read off what each predictor being zero means:

**I is false.** Setting $x_2 = 0$ does put that variable at its average, because it was standardized. But the first term is zero only when $\log(x_{1i}) = 0$, i.e. when $x_{1i} = 1$ — and 1 is not the average of $x_1$; it was never centred. So $\alpha$ is the expected outcome at $x_1 = 1$ and average $x_2$, not the average outcome. Centring $\log(x_1)$ as well would be needed to make the statement true.

**II is true.** The coefficient multiplies $\log(x_{1i})$ directly, so a one-unit increase in $\log(x_{1i})$ moves the expected outcome by $\beta_{x_1}$.

**III is true.** $x_{2i}$ is on a standardized scale, so a one-unit change *is* a one-standard-deviation change, and the expected outcome moves by $\beta_{x_2}$.

Only statement I is false, so the answer is **A**.
