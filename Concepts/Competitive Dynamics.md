**Competitive Dynamics** is the total set of competitive actions and responses taken by all the firms competing in a market. **Competitive rivalry** is the ongoing exchange of actions and responses between a firm and a particular competitor.

Hitt, Ireland and Hoskisson explain competitive dynamics through:

1. How much two firms overlap: market commonality and resource similarity.
2. What drives a firm to act: awareness, motivation and ability.
3. How likely a firm is to attack.
4. How likely a rival is to respond.
5. How fast advantages are imitated, which separates slow-cycle, fast-cycle and standard-cycle markets.

## Market commonality and resource similarity

Market commonality is the number of markets two firms both compete in and how important each is to them. Resource similarity is how far their tangible and intangible resources match in type and amount.

Firms high on both are direct competitors and expect to be drawn into rivalry. High commonality also encourages mutual forbearance, since an attack in one market invites retaliation in another.

## Drivers of competitive behaviour

Rivalry needs awareness of the competitor, motivation to act (what the firm gains or loses) and the ability to act (the resources and flexibility to do so). Competitor analysis of these three predicts who will move.

## Strategic and tactical actions

A strategic action commits significant resources and is hard to reverse, such as entering a new province or building a distribution network. A tactical action is quick and cheap to reverse, such as a price change or a commission promotion. Tactical actions draw more responses, and faster ones, because they are easy to match. Strategic actions are costly and slow to imitate, so rivals respond to them less often.

## Likelihood of attack

First movers take the first action to build an advantage. They can earn above-average returns until rivals respond, at the cost of risk and the slack resources innovation needs. Second movers imitate after learning from the first mover's mistakes, at lower cost, and can do better than the first mover. Late movers respond long after and usually earn below-average returns. Firm size and product quality also shape whether a firm attacks.

## Likelihood of response

A firm is more likely to respond when the action is tactical, when the attacker has a reputation as a strong competitor whose moves succeed, and when the firm depends heavily on the attacked market for its revenue and profit. That last condition is market dependence.

## Market cycles

In slow-cycle markets, advantages are shielded from imitation for long periods by patents, scarce resources or unique history. In fast-cycle markets, capabilities are copied quickly and cheaply. Firms there keep innovating and cannibalize their own products rather than protect an advantage that will be copied anyway. Standard-cycle markets sit between. Advantages are moderately shielded, and economies of scale and brand loyalty matter most, as in most personal lines and individual [[Life Insurance|life insurance]].

> [!example]- How should this insurer classify each product's market, and how will its auto rivals respond to a 5% rate cut? {Example}
> A multi-line insurer offers three products. Its group disability book relies on 40 years of claims data and a specialist return-to-work team. Its mobile app lets customers buy travel insurance in two minutes, a feature launched six months ago. Its personal auto book is the third largest in its province, and its profit depends on scale in claims handling and on brand.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Group disability is the closest to slow-cycle, because its advantage rests on data and expertise that rivals cannot buy or copy quickly.
> > - The travel app is fast-cycle, because a rival can build the same feature within months. The insurer must keep innovating rather than defend it.
> > - Personal auto is standard-cycle, where scale and brand protect share but the product itself is easy to copy.
> > - A 5% rate cut is a tactical action, quick to match and to reverse.
> > - Auto rivals with high market dependence, such as monoline auto writers, are the most likely to respond, and to respond quickly.
> > - A diversified rival with a small auto share may hold its price, accept some loss of volume, or reply in a different market where it is stronger.

## Responding to a price cut

Suppose a rival cuts its price by $\Delta$ per unit. A defender that sells $q$ units at price $P$ and cost $C$, and expects to lose a fraction $\ell$ of its volume if it holds its price, earns, by holding its price, a profit of

> $$\Pi_{\text{hold}} = q(1 - \ell)(P - C)$$

Matching keeps the volume at a lower margin, and the profit by matching is

> $$\Pi_{\text{match}} = q(P - \Delta - C)$$

The break-even loss, above which matching earns more, is

> $$\ell^{*} = \frac{\Delta}{P - C}$$

This arithmetic is what the behavioural factors above then tilt.

> [!example]- Should this auto insurer match its rival's price cut? {Example}
> The insurer writes 50,000 auto policies at an average premium of $\$1{,}000$ and a full cost of $\$920$. A rival with high market commonality cuts its rates by 5%, or $\$50$ a policy. The insurer expects to lose 20% of its policies if it holds its price.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Holding earns $50{,}000 \times 0.80 \times (1{,}000 - 920) = \$3{,}200{,}000$.
> > - Matching earns $50{,}000 \times (1{,}000 - 50 - 920) = \$1{,}500{,}000$.
> > - The break-even loss is $\ell^{*} = 50/80 = 62.5\%$.
> > - Holding the price earns more unless the insurer would lose more than 62.5% of its policies, which a 5% price gap is unlikely to cause.
> > - The arithmetic ignores two things a strategist weighs. Policies lost now may be hard to win back, and the business that leaves first may be the best risks, since price-sensitive good drivers shop most.
> > - The [[Underwriting Cycle|underwriting cycle]] is in part this game played across a whole market.

> [!trap] Market commonality is not resource similarity
> Market commonality is about overlapping markets, and resource similarity is about overlapping capabilities. An answer that treats a rival in every one of the firm's markets as a direct competitor, without comparing their resources, misjudges how direct the rivalry is.
