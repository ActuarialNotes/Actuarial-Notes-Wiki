**Corporate-Level Strategy** specifies the actions a firm takes to gain a competitive advantage by selecting and managing a group of different businesses competing in different product markets. Its two questions are which businesses the firm should be in and how the corporate office should manage them.

How a single business competes is [[Business-Level Strategy|business-level strategy]]. Corporate-level strategy is about the portfolio of businesses and the role of the head office. Its main tool is diversification, which creates value only when the businesses are worth more together than apart, that is, when

> $$V_{AB} > V_A + V_B$$

- $V_A$ and $V_B$ are the standalone values of businesses $A$ and $B$.
- $V_{AB}$ is their value under one owner.

## Levels of diversification

Hitt, Ireland and Hoskisson classify a firm by the share of its revenue earned by its dominant business, which is

> $$s_{\max} = \max_j \frac{R_j}{\sum_k R_k}$$

- $R_j$ is the revenue of business $j$.

| Level | Share of the dominant business | Links between the businesses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Single business | 95% or more | Not used |
| Dominant business | 70% to 95% | Not used |
| Related constrained | Below 70% | All share product, technology and distribution links |
| Related linked (mixed related and unrelated) | Below 70% | Only a few links |
| Unrelated | Below 70% | None |

Below 70%, the revenue shares alone do not decide the level. The links do, so a group with three equal businesses can be related constrained or unrelated.

> [!example]- What is each of these three insurance groups' level of diversification? {Example}
> Each group has revenue of $\$5{,}000$ million. Group B's three lines are all sold through the same agents on one policy system.
>
> | Group | Businesses and revenue ($ millions) |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | A | Individual life 3,600, group benefits 900, asset management 500 |
> | B | Personal auto 2,000, homeowners 1,500, small commercial 1,500 |
> | C | Insurance 2,250, hotels 1,500, timberland 1,250 |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Group A's dominant share is $3{,}600/5{,}000 = 72\%$, between 70% and 95%, so it is a dominant-business firm.
> > - Group B's is $2{,}000/5{,}000 = 40\%$ and Group C's is $2{,}250/5{,}000 = 45\%$, both below 70%, so their links decide.
> > - Group B's businesses share distribution, systems and underwriting skills, so it is related constrained.
> > - Group C's insurance, hotels and timber share nothing operational, so it is unrelated, and its only route to value is financial economies.

## Sources of value from diversification

Diversification creates value in three ways:

1. **Operational relatedness.** Businesses share activities in the value chain, such as a claims system, a call centre or a sales force, and gain economies of scope. A personal lines group that sells auto and home through the same agents is the standard insurance example.
2. **Corporate relatedness.** Businesses share core competencies, such as underwriting skill, pricing analytics or a brand, transferred from the corporate level rather than through shared operations. The transfer is hard for rivals to imitate, which is why it can create lasting value.
3. **Financial economies.** Unrelated diversification creates value through efficient internal capital allocation, moving cash to the businesses with the best prospects, and through restructuring businesses bought cheaply. For insurers, combining uncorrelated risks also lowers the capital the group needs, the [[Diversification Credit|diversification credit]].

Some diversification is value-neutral, a response to circumstances rather than a source of value. Hitt lists antitrust regulation and tax law, low performance in the core business, uncertain future cash flows, the wish to reduce the firm's overall risk, and spare tangible or intangible resources.

Some diversification reduces value. Managers may diversify to reduce the risk to their own jobs or to raise their pay, since both tend to rise with firm size. [[Corporate Governance|Governance]] mechanisms, such as the board, incentive pay and the market for corporate control, exist partly to check these motives.

> [!trap] Counting risk reduction as value created
> Shareholders can diversify their own portfolios, so a firm lowering its earnings volatility does not by itself create value for them. For an insurer, the exception is diversification that lowers required capital or the cost of financial distress, and an answer should say so.

## Means of diversification

Firms diversify by internal development, by [[Mergers and Acquisitions|merger and acquisition]], or by cooperative strategies such as joint ventures. For an acquisition, the value test becomes

> $$\text{Value to acquirer} = \text{PV(synergies)} - \text{Integration cost} - \text{Premium paid}$$

- $\text{PV(synergies)}$ is the present value of the gains from combining the businesses.
- $\text{Premium paid}$ is the price paid less the target's standalone value.

A deal with real synergies can still destroy value for the acquirer if it pays them all away in the premium.

> [!example]- How much value does this acquisition create for the acquirer's shareholders? {Example}
> An insurer worth $\$800$ million buys a regional insurer whose standalone value is $\$300$ million, paying $\$360$ million. Merging the two claims operations is expected to save $\$8$ million a year after tax in perpetuity, discounted at 10%. Integration will cost $\$15$ million.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The present value of the synergies is $8/0.10 = \$80$ million.
> > - The premium paid is $360 - 300 = \$60$ million.
> > - The value to the acquirer is $80 - 15 - 60 = \$5$ million.
> > - Of the $\$65$ million of net synergy, $\$60$ million goes to the target's shareholders, so paying $\$6$ million more would destroy value for the acquirer.
> > - The synergy is operational relatedness, a shared claims activity, and it is realised only if the integration succeeds.
