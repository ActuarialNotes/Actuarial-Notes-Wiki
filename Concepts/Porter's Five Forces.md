**Porter's Five Forces** is Michael Porter's model of industry structure: the rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers and the threat of substitutes together determine how much of the value an industry creates its firms can keep as profit. It is a tool for building a [[Business Strategy|business strategy]].

The five forces, as they apply to insurers, are:

1. **Rivalry among existing competitors.** Rivalry is most damaging when it is fought on price, because price cuts pass value straight to buyers. It is intense when there are many competitors of similar size, products are hard to tell apart, and exit is costly. Personal lines insurance, sold as a standardized product to customers who can compare prices, is a common example.
2. **Threat of new entrants.** The threat limits how high prices can go, since high profits would attract newcomers. For insurers, the barriers include the capital that solvency regulation requires, licensing in each jurisdiction, access to distribution, and the claims data needed to price accurately.
3. **Bargaining power of buyers.** Buyers are powerful when they are large, buy in volume, face low switching costs or can easily compare prices. Large commercial accounts and the brokers who place them have more power than an individual auto buyer.
4. **Bargaining power of suppliers.** An insurer's suppliers include [[Reinsurance|reinsurers]], capital providers and the [[Insurance Producer|producers]] who control access to customers, and each gains power when there are few of them or the insurer can't easily switch.
5. **Threat of substitutes.** A substitute meets the same need in a different way and caps the price an industry can charge. Substitutes for commercial insurance include [[Self-Insured Retention|self-insurance]], captives, [[Risk Retention Groups|risk retention groups]] and [[Insurance-Linked Securities|insurance-linked securities]], and buyers turn to them when insurance becomes expensive, as it does in a hard [[Underwriting Cycle|underwriting cycle]].

A strong force takes value away from the industry, so the strongest forces set the limit on its profitability.

## Industry profit and a firm's advantage

The five forces set the industry's average profit, because powerful buyers push prices down and powerful suppliers push costs up. A firm's competitive advantage over that average is

> $$\pi - \bar{\pi} = (P - \bar{P}) + (\bar{C} - C)$$

- $P$ and $C$ are the firm's price and cost per unit sold, and $\pi = P - C$ is its profit per unit.
- $\bar{P}$, $\bar{C}$ and $\bar{\pi}$ are the industry averages.

The advantage comes from a higher relative price, a lower relative cost, or both. It comes from the firm's positioning and its value chain, not from the forces.

> [!example]- How much more does this direct writer earn per policy than the average insurer, and where does the advantage come from? {Example}
> With illustrative figures, the average personal auto insurer charges \$1,500 per policy, with losses and LAE of \$1,095, commissions of \$225 and other expenses of \$135. A direct writer sells without commissioned producers. It charges \$1,450, its losses and LAE are \$1,080, advertising and its sales center cost \$110 and other expenses are \$130.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The average cost is $1{,}095 + 225 + 135 = \$1{,}455$, so $\bar{\pi} = 1{,}500 - 1{,}455 = \$45$.
> > - The direct writer's cost is $1{,}080 + 110 + 130 = \$1{,}320$, so $\pi = 1{,}450 - 1{,}320 = \$130$.
> > - The price part is $1{,}450 - 1{,}500 = -\$50$, and the cost part is $1{,}455 - 1{,}320 = \$135$.
> > - The direct writer earns $-50 + 135 = \$85$ more per policy, a margin of about 9.0% against 3.0%. It is a cost advantage built in distribution, one activity of its value chain.

## Value chain and SWOT analysis

A firm's value chain charts the path by which its product is created and sold, and the product becomes more valuable to the buyer at each step. Primary activities create and deliver the product. For an insurer these include marketing and distribution, [[Underwriting|underwriting]], policy issuance and claims. Support activities, such as firm infrastructure, human resources and technology, underpin them. VRIO asks whether a strength is valuable, rare, hard to imitate and supported by the organization, and so a source of competitive advantage.

A SWOT analysis sets the two pictures side by side. Strengths and weaknesses are internal to the firm, and opportunities and threats are external. The five forces are a structured way to find the opportunities and threats, the value chain is a structured way to find the strengths and weaknesses, and the strategy should use the strengths to pursue the opportunities while defending against the threats.

> [!example]- Where does each fact about this cyber insurance entry fall in the five forces and in a SWOT analysis? {Example}
> A mid-sized commercial insurer is deciding whether to start writing cyber insurance. Several large insurers already write the line. Reinsurance capacity for cyber is limited and expensive. Large buyers increasingly use captives for the first layers of cyber risk. Loss data is short and changing fast. The insurer has a strong broker network and a good claims reputation, but no in-house security expertise.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Several large incumbents mean rivalry is significant.
> > - The short, changing loss data is a barrier to entry that protects incumbents with more experience.
> > - Scarce, costly reinsurance is high supplier power, which raises the entrant's cost.
> > - Captives are a substitute that caps the price of the lower layers, and large buyers who can self-insure have bargaining power.
> > - In the SWOT, the broker network and claims reputation are strengths, and the missing security expertise is a weakness. The growing demand is the opportunity, and the reinsurance cost and substitutes are threats.

> [!trap] Calling another insurer a substitute
> A substitute meets the buyer's need by a different means, such as a captive instead of an insurance policy. Another insurer selling the same coverage is rivalry.
