**Risk Management Objectives** are the goals an organization sets for its [[Risk Management|risk management]] program. They are usually split into **pre-loss objectives**, which the program pursues before any loss happens (economy, reduction of anxiety and meeting externally imposed obligations), and **post-loss objectives**, which it must be able to meet after a loss (survival, continued operation, stable earnings, continued growth and social responsibility).

The objectives tie the program to the organization's own goals, and they are what the benefits of risk management are measured against. The [[Risk Management Process|risk management process]] starts from them.

## Pre-loss objectives

1. **Economy.** Before a loss, the organization should prepare for potential losses in the most economical way. That means comparing the cost of safety programs, insurance premiums and the other techniques for handling losses, rather than buying every protection available.
2. **Reduction of anxiety.** Some exposures, such as the chance of a major lawsuit, cause more worry for the risk manager, executives and owners than others. A pre-loss objective is to reduce that anxiety to a tolerable level, which can justify protection that economy alone would not.
3. **Externally imposed obligations.** Laws and regulations impose obligations that must be met whatever they cost, such as installing safety devices to protect workers, disposing of hazardous materials properly and labeling consumer products.

A legal obligation is a pre-loss objective because it applies whether or not a loss ever happens.

## Post-loss objectives

1. **Survival.** The first post-loss objective is survival: after a loss, the organization can resume at least partial operation within a reasonable time.
2. **Continued operation.** For some organizations, the ability to keep operating after a severe loss is itself an extremely important objective, a stricter goal than survival.
3. **Stability of earnings and continued growth.** After a loss, the organization wants to maintain its earnings, such as earnings per share, and to continue growing, so the risk manager considers how a loss would interrupt both.
4. **Social responsibility.** The organization seeks to limit the effect a loss has on other people and on society, such as employees who lose work, customers who lose a supplier and a community that loses a major employer.

> [!example]- Is each of this manufacturer's goals a pre-loss or a post-loss objective, and which one? {Example}
> A furniture manufacturer's board approves a risk management policy that lists these goals:
>
> 1. Choose among sprinklers, a larger deductible and higher insurance limits by comparing their costs over five years.
> 2. Be able to restart at least one production line within 30 days of a fire.
> 3. Comply with the state's workplace safety rules on machine guarding.
> 4. Keep earnings per share within 10% of plan in a year with a major loss.
> 5. Keep supplying the two hospitals that depend on its beds, even if a plant is damaged.
> 6. Limit the effect of a plant closure on the town where the plant is the largest employer.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Goal 1 is pre-loss economy, because the firm prepares for losses in the most economical way by comparing the costs of the techniques.
> > - Goal 2 is post-loss survival, because it resumes at least partial operation within a reasonable time.
> > - Goal 3 is a pre-loss externally imposed obligation, because the rule applies whether or not a loss ever happens.
> > - Goal 4 is post-loss stability of earnings, because it concerns earnings in the period after a loss.
> > - Goal 5 is post-loss continued operation, because keeping on operating through the loss is more demanding than partial resumption.
> > - Goal 6 is post-loss social responsibility, because it limits the loss's effect on people outside the firm.

## Conflicting objectives

Economy pulls toward retaining losses and spending little, while reduction of anxiety and the post-loss objectives pull toward more protection. A choice that minimizes cost but leaves the firm unable to survive a large loss fails the post-loss objectives. Stating the objectives in a risk management policy statement lets the organization resolve these conflicts in advance and communicate them consistently.

## Risk management and firm value

In a simple valuation, the value $V$ of the organization is its expected annual profit $\bar{\pi}$ times a multiple $m$ the owners apply to it, so the value is

> $$V = m \times \bar{\pi}$$

A constant profit valued as a perpetuity at a 10% return gives $m = 10$. The expected annual profit is

> $$\bar{\pi} = R - C_{\text{rm}} - W - I$$

- $R$ is revenue.
- $C_{\text{rm}}$ is the cost of the risk management program, such as safety spending or insurance premiums.
- $W$ is salaries.
- $I$ is interest on debt.

Spending on risk management adds $C_{\text{rm}}$ to the costs, so with everything else fixed it lowers profit and value. It raises value only by changing the other terms: customers who prefer a secure supplier buy more ($R$ rises), employees accept less to work for a safer firm ($W$ falls), lenders charge less ($I$ falls), and owners may accept a higher multiple $m$ for a less risky firm. This is the sense in which risk management benefits stakeholders other than the owners.

> [!example]- What is this firm's value before and after its safety and insurance program? {Example}
> With illustrative figures in thousands a year, a firm has revenue of \$2,000, salaries and other operating costs of \$1,500 in total, and interest of \$200. Its owners value it at 10 times profit. It adopts a safety and insurance program costing \$60 a year. Revenue rises to \$2,040, employees accept \$30 less in total pay, and lenders cut interest by \$10.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Before the program, profit is $2{,}000 - 1{,}500 - 200 = 300$, so the value is $10 \times 300 = 3{,}000$.
> > - After it, the costs are $1{,}500 - 30 = 1{,}470$ and the interest is $190$.
> > - Profit is $2{,}040 - 60 - 1{,}470 - 190 = 320$, so the value is $10 \times 320 = 3{,}200$.
> > - If the owners also accept a multiple of 11 for the more secure firm, the value is $11 \times 320 = 3{,}520$.
> > - The program costs \$60 a year but raises value, because customers, employees and lenders all respond to a more secure firm.
> > - Judged by its cost alone, the program would look like a \$600 loss in value ($10 \times 60$).

## Benefits of enterprise risk management

The [[Enterprise Risk Management|enterprise risk management]] framework of COSO (2017) lists the benefits an organization can realize by integrating risk management throughout the entity: a wider range of opportunities, risks identified and managed entity-wide, more positive outcomes and fewer negative surprises, less variability in performance, better deployment of resources, and greater resilience ([[Organizational Resilience]]). These extend the traditional objectives, which concern pure loss exposures, to risks with an upside as well.

> [!trap] Survival versus continued operation
> Survival means resuming at least partial operation within a reasonable time. Continued operation is the stricter goal of keeping the organization operating after a severe loss, so a goal of uninterrupted supply is continued operation, not survival.
