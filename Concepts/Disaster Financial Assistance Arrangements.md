The **Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements** (DFAA) are the federal cost-sharing program under which Ottawa reimburses a province for a share of its response and recovery costs after a large natural disaster. The DFAA are not insurance. There is no premium and no contract, and a province applies after the event.

The arrangements work as follows:

1. The province designs and delivers the assistance.
2. The federal government reimburses eligible costs on a sliding scale. The federal share rises in steps with the provincial cost per capita, toward $90\%$ for the largest events.
3. Eligible costs include emergency response, restoration of public infrastructure, and assistance to individuals and small businesses for **uninsurable** losses.
4. Losses for which insurance was **reasonably and readily available** are not reimbursed.

## The insurability boundary

As overland [[Flood Insurance|flood insurance]] became available in Canada, the insurability boundary moved. Losses that the DFAA once covered are now expected to be insured, a deliberate shift of cost from taxpayers to policyholders.

> [!example]- How does this flood's cost divide between the DFAA and the insurance market? {Example}
> A province suffers a flood causing $\$1.2$ billion of damage:
>
> | Damage | Amount |
> | :--- | ---: |
> | Private homes where overland flood coverage is available and affordable | $\$400$ million |
> | Homes in a high-risk zone where no insurer will write | $\$300$ million |
> | Public infrastructure | $\$350$ million |
> | Emergency response | $\$150$ million |
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The DFAA do not apply to the $\$400$ million of homes where insurance was reasonably available. Uninsured owners in this group bear their own loss.
> > - The $\$300$ million in the uninsurable zone is eligible, because coverage was not available.
> > - The $\$350$ million of infrastructure and $\$150$ million of response are eligible, as the program's core purpose.
> > - About $\$800$ million falls on the public purse and $\$400$ million on the insurance market.
> > - The $\$300$ million goes to properties in a zone so hazardous that no insurer will write it, and paying it after each event funds their continued occupation of the floodplain.

## Moral hazard and fiscal exposure

Reliable post-disaster assistance reduces the incentive to buy insurance, to mitigate, or to avoid building in hazardous locations, which is a form of [[Moral Hazard|moral hazard]]. Because assistance is decided after the event, it cannot be conditioned on precautions taken before it. Repeated rebuilding of the same flood-prone properties is the standard illustration.

DFAA payments have grown by an order of magnitude over recent decades, driven by [[Climate Risk|climate]]-related events and by exposure growth in hazardous areas. The federal liability is effectively an **uncapped, unfunded catastrophe exposure**.

The direction of reform is to tie assistance to mitigation and relocation, tighten the insurability test, and replace ad hoc payments with a structured [[Flood Insurance|flood insurance]] program in which the subsidy is explicit and priced. A public high-risk flood pool would charge a premium the homeowner sees, carry a subsidy voted in a budget, and condition eligibility on mitigation or a buy-out for the worst locations.

> [!trap] Treating the DFAA as insurance
> The DFAA reimburse a province after the event, with no premium and no contract, and they exclude losses that could have been insured. A homeowner who could have bought overland flood coverage and didn't is not covered by them.
