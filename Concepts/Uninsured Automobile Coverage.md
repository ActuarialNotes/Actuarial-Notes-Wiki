**Uninsured Automobile Coverage** is the part of an auto policy that compensates an insured injured, or whose vehicle is damaged, by an uninsured or unidentified (hit-and-run) driver. It is compulsory in Canadian auto policies, so the cost of uninsured driving is borne by insured drivers rather than left with the victim.

The coverage and its optional neighbour respond to three kinds of at-fault driver:

| At-fault driver | Coverage that responds |
| :--- | :--- |
| Uninsured: identified, with no valid policy | Uninsured coverage, up to its limit |
| Unidentified: a hit-and-run | Uninsured coverage, with corroborating evidence that another vehicle was involved |
| Underinsured: insured, but for a limit below the claimant's damages | The optional underinsured motorist endorsement, which tops up to the insured's own limit |

The unidentified case carries the extra evidence requirement because otherwise a single-vehicle accident could be recharacterised as a hit-and-run.

> [!example]- Which coverage responds in each of these four cases? {Example}
> An insured with $\$1$ million third-party liability and an optional underinsured motorist endorsement of $\$1$ million suffers $\$700{,}000$ of damages.
>
> 1. The at-fault driver has no insurance.
> 2. The at-fault driver carries the $\$200{,}000$ minimum limit.
> 3. The at-fault vehicle left the scene and cannot be identified, and an independent witness saw the collision.
> 4. As in 3, but there is no witness and no physical evidence of another vehicle.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - Case 1: uninsured coverage responds, up to its limit. The insurer pays and subrogates against the driver personally, with little prospect of recovery.
> > - Case 2: the other driver's insurer pays $\$200{,}000$, and the underinsured endorsement tops up the remaining $\$500{,}000$ to the insured's own $\$1$ million limit. In most provinces the endorsement's terms reduce its available limit by amounts recovered elsewhere.
> > - Case 3: unidentified-motorist coverage responds, because the independent witness supplies the corroboration it requires.
> > - Case 4: this coverage likely pays nothing, because the claim lacks the corroborating evidence. The insured's own collision coverage, if purchased, pays for the vehicle damage regardless of fault.

## Fault basis and subrogation

The coverage responds on a fault basis. The insured must show the uninsured driver was legally liable, so the coverage brings [[Tort Law|tort]] principles into a first-party coverage. The insurer stands in the shoes of the absent defendant and may contest liability and the amount of damages.

Where the uninsured driver is identified, the insurer generally has [[Salvage and Subrogation|subrogation]] rights against them personally. Recovery rates are low, so the coverage is priced as though recovery were nil.

## Cost and public backstops

The main cost driver is the province's rate of uninsured driving. Provinces with more uninsured driving see materially higher costs in this coverage, so enforcement (plate-based insurance verification, penalties) is a public policy lever that directly affects its premium.

Some provinces add a motor vehicle accident claims fund, a government fund of last resort for victims with no available coverage at all. It is a public analogue to a [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty fund]].

> [!trap] Treating every hit-and-run as covered
> An unidentified-motorist claim with no witness and no physical evidence of another vehicle fails the corroboration requirement. The loss then falls to the insured's own collision coverage, which they may not have bought.
