Given the following information for a workers compensation book written with a per-occurrence deductible of \$500,000:

Fitted Limited Severity:

| Limit | 36 months | Ultimate |
|---|---|---|
| Unlimited | \$5,000 | \$9,000 |
| \$500,000 | \$4,500 | \$6,600 |

| | 36 months | Ultimate |
|---|---|---|
| Observed Deductible Loss | \$1,000,000 | - |
| Observed Excess Loss | \$275,000 | - |
| Modeled Excess Age-to-Ultimate Factor | 3.00 | - |
| Insurance Charge Ratio | 0.20 | 0.30 |

- Expected claims volume = 1,000 claims.

## Part a (2.25 points)

Assess the reasonability of the modeled excess IBNR compared to the loss ratio approach.

### Explanation
The answer has three pieces: the modeled excess IBNR, the excess IBNR from the loss ratio approach, and an assessment of the two.

**Modeled XS IBNR**

$$\text{XS IBNR} = \text{Reported XS Loss} \times \text{Modeled XS LDF} - \text{Reported XS Loss} = 275\text{K} \times 3 - 275\text{K} = 550\text{K}$$

(The report also accepted an XS LDF derived from the severities instead of the modeled 3.00: $275\text{K} \times 4.806 - 275\text{K} = 1{,}045\text{K}$, with the derivation shown below.)

**IBNR from the loss ratio approach**

The loss ratio approach is $P \cdot E \cdot \chi - \text{Rept XS}$: the expected ultimate loss times the ultimate insurance charge, less the reported excess loss.

$$\text{Ult XS} = \text{Ult Total Sev} \times \text{Counts} \times \text{Ins Charge}_{ult} = 9{,}000 \times 1{,}000 \times 0.30 = 2.7\text{M}$$

$$\text{Rept XS} = \text{Total Reported Loss} \times \text{Ins Charge}_{36} = (1\text{M} + 275\text{K}) \times 0.20 = 255\text{K}$$

$$\text{XS IBNR} = 2.7\text{M} - 255\text{K} = 2{,}445\text{K}$$

The report printed twenty accepted versions of this piece. Several derive development factors from the severity table first:

$$R^{500}_{ult} = 6{,}600 / 9{,}000 = 0.733 \qquad R^{500}_{36} = 4{,}500 / 5{,}000 = 0.900 \qquad LDF^{unlim}_{36-ult} = 9{,}000 / 5{,}000 = 1.80$$

$$XSLDF_{36-ult} = LDF^{unlim}_{36-ult} \times \frac{1 - R^{500}_{ult}}{1 - R^{500}_{36}} = 1.80 \times \frac{1 - 0.733}{1 - 0.900} = 4.806$$

For example, with the excess severity times counts as the expected excess ultimate:

$$\text{XS Ult Loss} = (9{,}000 - 6{,}600) \times 1{,}000 = 2.4\text{M}$$

$$\text{XS IBNR} = 2.4\text{M} \times (1 - 1/4.806) = 1{,}900{,}624$$

Other accepted results included 1.9M, 2,125K, 1.6M, 413.5K and 561K, depending on which severities, counts and charges were combined.

**Assessment of the two methods** (sample responses)

- The modeled XS IBNR is less reasonable compared to the LR approach. There may be imperfect data or uncertainty volatile in the actual loss experience.
- The modeled XS IBNR is higher than the LR approach because XS LDFs tend to be highly leveraged, especially in early maturities. And the LR approach doesn't take into consideration actual experience and uses industry factors, which may not be appropriate. Using a credibility approach of the two methods might be more appropriate.
- Modeled XS IBNR is much higher than calculated using the LR method. This may be due to the highly leveraged XS LDF that is applied. The LR approach is more stable than direct development.
- LR approach IBNR is much higher than modeled. This is because the XS LDF is very volatile and uncertainty in estimation is high. Also we may have little reported XS loss at early maturity for WC, which is long tail and subject to latent claims.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the XS IBNR using the Direct Development approach and the LR approach with the Table M (insurance charge) provided (or an alternative approach that calculates valid XS Ultimate Loss and Reported XS Loss), and then determine the most appropriate method to use in the given situation with logical support.
Common mistakes for LR and Modeled IBNR calculations included:

- Only calculating the ultimate loss without calculating the IBNR.
- Treating the ultimate loss as IBNR.
- Applying XS ultimate loss and reported loss to the insurance charge.
- Applying the wrong formula to get the limited/XS LDF when using the severity relativities (e.g., Limited LDF = Unlimited LDF × (1 – RUlt) / (1 – R36), when it should be XS LDF).
- Applying both insurance charges (36 month and ultimate) to the ultimate loss.
- Not applying the insurance charge to the ultimate unlimited/limited loss.
- Applying Limited LDF to the total loss or applying the Unlimited LDF to the limited loss.

Common mistakes for the assessment of the 2 methods included:

- Only stating that one method is lower/higher than the other method without providing valid reasoning, since numerical support alone was not sufficient (e.g., LR approach is higher than the Modeled approach, and therefore Modeled approach is not reasonable).

## Part b (0.5 points)

Given the following:

Volume Weighted Claim Count Age-to-Age Development Factors as of (months):

| 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 | 48-60 | 60-72 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.50 | 1.25 | 1.15 | 1.05 | 1.00 |

Describe an issue with using limited severity data at 36 months.

### Explanation
Sample responses:

- Since the number of claims is expected to go up (ATA of 1.15 and 1.05), that will affect the severity unlimited and limited in different ways.
- By using the limited severity at 36 month, we are assuming that all claims have been reported by 36 month. Since there is still claim count development after 36 month, this violates the assumption and it is not proper to use the limited severity at 36 month.
- Since not all losses are reported at 36 months, the 36 months severity may be overstated, there are more claims expected which may be small.
- The issue is that the expected claims at age 36 is less than 1,000, only 1,000 / (1.15 × 1.05) = 828 are reported, likely those unreported are the most complex and severe. The unreported counts will have higher severity than the given severities at ultimate. Using the given severities will understate the IBNR.
- A number of claims have yet to be reported based on the development factors given.
Claims that exceeded the deductible are likely to do so after longer periods of time, i.e.
there is more development in the excess layer than in the deductible layer.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know that using counts that are still developing will distort the limited severity calculations or the IBNR calculations.

Common mistakes included:

- Calculating the XS/Limited counts LDF using the severity relativities even though too little information was given, and not tying back to the claim count development's impact on the severity calculation.
- Mentioning other valid issues of the LR approach without tying back to the claim count development, such as adjusting for inflation or different development patterns between excess and limited layer.
