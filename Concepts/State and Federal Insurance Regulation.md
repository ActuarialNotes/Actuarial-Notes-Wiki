**State and Federal Insurance Regulation** is the dual structure of U.S. insurance oversight. The **states** license, examine, review the rates and forms of, and if necessary liquidate insurers, while the **federal government** overrides state insurance law only through statutes that specifically relate to insurance, monitors the sector through the Federal Insurance Office, and runs a few insurance programs of its own.

## Reverse preemption under McCarran-Ferguson

The McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945, [[History of Insurance Regulation]]) sets the rule between the two, known as reverse preemption. A federal statute of general application cannot displace a state law regulating the business of insurance. Only a federal statute that specifically relates to the business of insurance can. Federal antitrust law has its own carve-out, covered in [[Antitrust Law]].

> [!example]- A state's insurance code voids arbitration clauses in insurance policies. Does the Federal Arbitration Act override that rule? {Example}
> - The Federal Arbitration Act is a statute of general application, because it does not specifically relate to the business of insurance.
> - The state rule governs the terms of the insurance contract between insurer and policyholder, so it regulates the business of insurance.
> - McCarran-Ferguson therefore protects the state rule from displacement, and the clause is generally unenforceable in that state.
> - The result can differ where the arbitration clause rests on an international treaty rather than the domestic act, an area where federal courts have split, because reverse preemption is a rule about Acts of Congress.

## The role of the states

Each state licenses insurers and producers, regulates rates, forms and market conduct for business written there, and collects premium tax. The **domiciliary** state carries the solvency load: financial examinations, [[Risk-Based Capital]], holding-company filings and receivership. Other states rely on its work, which is what the NAIC **accreditation** program exists to make safe ([[Solvency Monitoring]]).

## The NAIC

The NAIC is the standard-setting and support organization of the chief insurance regulators of the 50 states, D.C. and five territories. It drafts **model laws**, which bind no one until a legislature enacts them. It maintains the [[NAIC Annual Statement|annual statement]] and the [[Statutory Accounting Principles|statutory accounting]] manual, runs the financial databases and analysis tools, and accredits state departments. It coordinates the states but does not govern them, and it has no enforcement power of its own.

## The federal role

The federal government is present in four ways:

1. **Monitoring and international affairs**: the Federal Insurance Office (FIO) and FSOC, created by [[Dodd-Frank]]. FIO negotiates **covered agreements** (U.S.–EU 2017, U.S.–UK 2018) that can preempt state measures inconsistent with them.
2. **Statutes that specifically relate to insurance**: the Liability Risk Retention Act ([[Risk Retention Groups]]), the Nonadmitted and Reinsurance Reform Act ([[Excess and Surplus Lines]]), and Gramm-Leach-Bliley (bank–insurer affiliation, privacy, functional regulation).
3. **General laws that still apply**: securities law and [[Sarbanes-Oxley]] for public insurers, the Fair Credit Reporting Act for [[Credit-Based Insurance Scoring|insurance scores]], and ERISA, which takes self-funded employer plans out of state reach.
4. **Federal programs**: the National Flood Insurance Program, federal [[Crop Insurance|crop insurance]], and the terrorism backstop under [[TRIA]].

Critics of the state system point to fifty-plus filing regimes and slow product approval, and defenders point to local responsiveness and the sector's record in 2008. Optional federal charter bills have recurred since the 2000s, and none has passed.

> [!example]- Who regulates each part of this group? {Example}
> A NYSE-listed holding company owns:
>
> - an Ohio-domiciled P&C insurer writing personal auto in 25 states, which cedes a quota share to a UK reinsurer;
> - a Delaware-domiciled surplus lines insurer;
> - a federal savings association.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - **Ohio** regulates the auto insurer's solvency: examinations, RBC, dividends and affiliate transactions, holding-company filings.
> > - Ohio also decides the credit for reinsurance on the UK cession. Under the U.S.–UK covered agreement, a qualifying UK reinsurer cannot be required to post collateral.
> > - **Each of the 25 states** regulates the auto insurer's licence, rate and form filings, market conduct, premium tax and guaranty-association membership.
> > - **Delaware** regulates the surplus lines insurer's solvency. Its individual placements are regulated and taxed only by each insured's home state, through licensed surplus lines brokers, with no rate or form approval.
> > - **Federal banking regulators** supervise the savings association, and its presence makes the parent a savings and loan holding company supervised by the **Federal Reserve**.
> > - The **SEC** regulates the parent's public reporting, including SOX certifications and internal-control reporting.
> > - No one regulator sees the whole group, which is the gap that lead-state coordination, supervisory colleges and the holding-company Form F risk report are meant to close.

> [!trap] Treating an NAIC model law as binding
> The NAIC cannot impose a model law on anyone. A model law takes effect in a state only when that state's legislature enacts it.
