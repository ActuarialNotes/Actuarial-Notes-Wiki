Given the following information:

| | |
|---|---|
| \$500 | Current average premium |
| \$600 | Indicated average premium |
| \$510 | All competitors' average premium |

## Part a (0.5 point)

List two likely consequences of the company implementing the indicated rate.

### Explanation

Implementing the indication raises the average premium from \$500 to \$600, roughly 18% above the competitors' \$510 average. Two likely consequences:

- **Retention falls.** Policyholders faced with a large increase will shop and switch to competitors whose average premium is far lower, so the renewal ratio declines.
- **New business volume falls and market share shrinks**, since prospective insureds shopping on price will also choose the competitors — the close (hit) ratio drops.

Other acceptable consequences include an improved loss ratio and higher profit per risk (the rate is now adequate), and possible objections from regulators on the size of the filed increase.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to provide two distinct consequences of the given scenario; both positive and negative consequences were accepted.

Common mistakes included stating that the company would be subject to adverse selection or that the loss ratio would deteriorate (the opposite of the expected effect), and stating that the company should not implement the indicated premium — a recommendation, not a consequence.

## Part b (0.5 point)

Briefly describe two factors that affect an insured's propensity to renew.

### Explanation

- **Size of the rate change.** A large increase at renewal prompts the insured to shop, whereas a renewal offer close to the expiring premium is usually accepted without comparison.
- **Quality of service, including claim handling.** An insured who has had a claim handled well, or who values the service received, will tolerate a higher price and is more likely to renew.

Other acceptable factors include the price relative to competitors, the overall price level of the product (expensive products get shopped harder), tenure and brand loyalty (longer-tenured insureds renew at higher rates), the age of the insured (younger insureds shop more), and whether the coverage is still needed or is mandatory.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to briefly *describe* two factors affecting the insured's propensity to renew.

The common mistake was listing a factor such as "price" with no description of why it affects the renewal decision.

## Part c (0.75 point)

The company has decided to not implement the indicated rate. List three non-pricing solutions the company could implement to ensure profitability does not deteriorate.

### Explanation

- **Reduce expenses** — cut commission levels, marketing spend or general overhead, which lowers the premium needed for the same margin.
- **Tighten underwriting guidelines** to write fewer high-risk exposures and non-renew the worst-performing risks, improving the expected loss ratio without a rate change.
- **Improve claims handling** — hire more experienced adjusters and control claim leakage, reducing the loss dollars paid on the same book.

Other acceptable answers include reducing benefits (raising deductibles or lowering limits), targeted marketing toward more favourable risks, and introducing loss-mitigation programs for insureds.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to list three non-pricing solutions for maintaining profitability.

Common mistakes included re-segmenting risks by changing relativities without changing the overall rate (still a pricing solution), "changing the mix of business" or "changing coverage" without stating the direction of the change, increasing investment income, and purchasing reinsurance.

## Part d (0.5 point)

Identify an issue with comparing one company's premium to another and briefly propose a solution to this issue.

### Explanation

**Issue.** Average premium comparisons confound price with mix of business. Two companies can charge appropriate rates and still show very different average premiums if they write different risk profiles — the competitors' \$510 average may reflect a book of lower-risk insureds, different coverage levels, limits and deductibles, or different underwriting guidelines and target markets, rather than a genuinely lower price for the same risk.

**Solution.** Compare on a like-for-like basis instead of in aggregate: select a set of representative risk profiles and obtain competitor quotes for them, or re-rate the company's own book using competitors' filed rate manuals, so the comparison holds the risk characteristics fixed. Comparing average premiums by segment, alongside the segment relativities, achieves the same thing.

### Examiner Report

Candidates were expected to show an understanding of the conditions required for a fair premium comparison between companies. Some candidates did not answer this part in the context of the information given, or proposed a solution to an issue other than the one they had identified.

Common mistakes on the issue included citing different expense assumptions or different profit provisions as the reason premiums are not comparable. Common mistakes on the solution included "look at pure premium instead" and "adjust for bias and then compare", which is too vague.
