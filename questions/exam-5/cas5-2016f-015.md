Given the following for a workers compensation policyholder:

| Individual Claims Reported During the Experience Period |
|---|
| \$19,000 |
| \$3,000 |
| \$102,500 |
| \$11,000 |

- Standard premium = \$435,000.
- 3-year payroll = \$14,590,000.
- Expected loss rate = 2.40 per \$100 of payroll.
- D-ratio = 0.19.
- Primary loss cap = \$5,000.
- Primary credibility = 0.75.
- Excess credibility = 0.15.

Calculate the policy's premium under an experience rating plan.

### Answer
\$358,826

### Explanation
Experience rating splits both actual and expected losses into a **primary** layer (small, frequent, and highly predictive of the insured's own future experience) and an **excess** layer (large, volatile, and much less credible), then credibility-weights each layer separately against expectation. Primary losses get the high credibility (0.75), excess losses the low one (0.15).

**Step 1 — split the actual losses at the \$5,000 primary cap.**

| Claim | Primary $= \min(\text{claim},\ 5{,}000)$ | Excess $=$ claim $-$ primary |
|---|---|---|
| \$19,000 | \$5,000 | \$14,000 |
| \$3,000 | \$3,000 | \$0 |
| \$102,500 | \$5,000 | \$97,500 |
| \$11,000 | \$5,000 | \$6,000 |
| **Total** | $A_p = \$18{,}000$ | $A_e = \$117{,}500$ |

Note the \$102,500 claim contributes only \$5,000 of primary loss — capping is exactly what stops one severe claim from dominating the modification.

**Step 2 — expected losses from the payroll and the loss rate.** The payroll given is the **3-year** figure and the experience period is three policy years, so it is used as-is — not divided by three:

$$E = 2.40 \times \frac{14{,}590{,}000}{100} = 2.40 \times 145{,}900 = \$350{,}160$$

**Step 3 — split expectation with the D-ratio.** The D-ratio is the expected *primary* proportion:

$$E_p = 0.19 \times 350{,}160 = \$66{,}530.40 \qquad E_e = (1 - 0.19) \times 350{,}160 = \$283{,}629.60$$

**Step 4 — the experience modification.** Each layer's actual is credibility-weighted against its own expectation, and the total is divided by expected losses:

$$M = \frac{A_p Z_p + E_p (1 - Z_p) + A_e Z_e + E_e (1 - Z_e)}{E}$$

$$M = \frac{18{,}000(0.75) + 66{,}530.40(0.25) + 117{,}500(0.15) + 283{,}629.60(0.85)}{350{,}160}$$

$$M = \frac{13{,}500 + 16{,}632.60 + 17{,}625 + 241{,}085.16}{350{,}160} = \frac{288{,}842.76}{350{,}160} = 0.8249$$

**Step 5 — apply the modification to standard premium.**

$$\text{Premium} = 435{,}000 \times 0.8249 = \mathbf{\$358{,}826}$$

Alternatively, the NCCI form of the formula reaches the same number. Its weighting factor is the *ratio* of the two credibilities, $w = Z_e / Z_p = 0.15/0.75 = 0.2$, and its ballast follows from $Z_p = E/(E+B)$:

$$0.75 = \frac{350{,}160}{350{,}160 + B} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \$116{,}720$$

$$M = \frac{A_p + w A_e + (1-w) E_e + B}{E + B} = \frac{18{,}000 + 23{,}500 + 226{,}903.68 + 116{,}720}{466{,}880} = 0.8249$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate primary and excess losses from the individual claims and the loss cap; expected losses from the payroll and loss rate, split into primary and excess with the D-ratio (or, using the NCCI alternative, with the B and w factors); the experience modification from those components and the two credibility factors; and finally the premium.

Candidates were also expected to know that the experience rating modification uses an experience period of **three** policy years. Because standard premium already contemplates the modification, any answer that correctly calculated the modification received full credit; most candidates went on to multiply standard premium by it.

Common mistakes included assuming a one-year experience period and dividing the payroll by three; reversing $E_p$ and $A_e$ in the modification formula; applying the D-ratio to get expected *excess* losses instead of expected primary losses (or the reverse); multiplying the premium by $1 + M$ or $1 - M$ rather than by $M$; and, among candidates using the NCCI alternative, using one of the credibility factors as the weighting factor instead of their ratio.
