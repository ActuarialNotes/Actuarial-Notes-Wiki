**Insurance Regulation** in Canada is the supervision of insurers by government, split between a **federal** solvency regulator ([[OSFI]]) and **provincial** regulators who license insurers and police [[Market Conduct Regulation|market conduct]] and [[Rate Regulation|rates]]. No single body regulates an insurer end to end — the same company answers to OSFI for capital and to each province it writes in for how it sells and prices.

> $$\text{Regulation} = \underbrace{\text{Solvency}}_{\text{mostly federal}} \; + \; \underbrace{\text{Market conduct}}_{\text{provincial}}$$

- The split comes from the **[[Federal-Provincial Jurisdiction|division of powers]]**: the federal government incorporates and supervises federally registered insurers under the [[Insurance Companies Act]], while "property and civil rights in the province" gives each province authority over the contract itself, licensing of insurers and agents, and premium rates.
- The three classic **rationales** for regulating insurance are (1) *solvency* — policyholders buy a promise and cannot assess an insurer's balance sheet; (2) *information asymmetry* — the contract is complex and drafted by the insurer; (3) *availability and affordability* of coverage the public is compelled to buy, which is why [[Automobile Insurance Reform|auto]] is the most heavily regulated line.
- Coordination between the many provincial regulators runs through the **[[Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators]]**, and prudential coordination with OSFI through information-sharing agreements — but coordination is not consolidation, and multi-jurisdiction compliance cost is a standing industry complaint.
- Regulation is increasingly **[[Principles-Based Regulation|principles-based]]**: OSFI sets outcomes ([[ORSA]], [[FCT]], [[Corporate Governance]] expectations) and holds the board and senior management responsible, rather than prescribing every calculation.
- The [[Appointed Actuary]] is a *regulatory* creation: the [[Insurance Companies Act]] requires the appointment and gives the actuary duties owed to the company, but with reporting obligations the regulator relies on.

> [!example]- Who Regulates What {Example}
> A federally incorporated insurer writes personal automobile insurance in Ontario and Alberta and commercial property across Canada. Identify which regulator addresses each of the following.
>
> 1. Whether the company holds enough capital.
> 2. Whether its Ontario auto rates may increase $6\%$.
> 3. Whether a claims-handling practice is unfair to policyholders.
> 4. Whether the company may write business at all.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > 1. **OSFI** — federal solvency supervision, enforced through the [[MCT]] and its capital ratio targets.
> > 2. **FSRA** (Ontario) — provincial [[Rate Regulation|rate regulation]]; Ontario private passenger auto is file-and-approve, so the increase needs approval. Alberta's rate board would decide separately for Alberta.
> > 3. **The provincial regulator** in the province where the conduct occurred — market conduct is provincial.
> > 4. **Both.** OSFI issues the federal order to commence and carry on business; each province issues a separate licence for that province.
> >
> > The point of the exercise: there is no "the regulator." An exam answer that names only OSFI for a market-conduct question, or only the province for a capital question, has missed the structure.

> [!example]- Why Regulate Insurance at All {Example}
> A commentator argues that competition alone protects insurance consumers, so solvency regulation is unnecessary. Give the actuarial response.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Competition disciplines price, but insurance is a **promise to pay later**, and three features break the ordinary market:
> >
> > - **The consumer cannot price the promise.** Assessing whether an insurer will be solvent in ten years requires reserve and capital analysis the buyer cannot do. Competition on price alone therefore rewards the insurer that under-reserves, which is the opposite of the desired outcome.
> > - **Failure externalises.** An insolvency imposes losses on claimants who are not the insurer's customers at all — injured third parties — and on the industry through [[Guaranty Funds|guaranty fund]] assessments ([[PACICC]]).
> > - **Compulsion.** Drivers are legally required to buy auto insurance, so the "just don't buy it" response is unavailable, which is what justifies [[Rate Regulation|rate]] and availability regulation on top of solvency regulation.
