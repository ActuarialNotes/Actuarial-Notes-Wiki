Given the following for a portfolio:

| Stock | Beta | Capitalization (\$) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 1.10 | 20,000 |
| B | 1.25 | 50,000 |
| C | 0.90 | 30,000 |

- The risk-free rate is 5%.
- The market price of risk is 1.5.
- The standard deviation of the market portfolio is 20%.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Using the traditional Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), calculate the expected rate of return on the portfolio.

### Explanation
The market price of risk is the market risk premium divided by the market variance:

$$1.5 = \frac{E(r_M) - 0.05}{0.20^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E(r_M) = 0.11$$

Weight each beta by the stock's share of the capitalization:

$$\beta_{portfolio} = 0.20(1.10) + 0.50(1.25) + 0.3(0.90) = 1.115$$

$$E(r) = 0.05 + 1.115(0.11 - 0.05) = 0.1169$$

Alternatively:

Calculate each stock's CAPM return, $E(r) = r_f + \beta(r_M - r_f)$ with $r_M = 0.11 = 11\%$, and weight the returns by capitalization:

$$A: 0.05 + 1.1(0.06) = 0.116$$

$$B: 0.05 + 1.25(0.06) = 0.125$$

$$C: 0.05 + 0.9(0.06) = 0.104$$

$$\text{Rate on portfolio} = \frac{20(0.116) + 50(0.125) + 30(0.104)}{100} = 11.69\%$$

### Examiner Report
- Candidates were expected to
  - derive the market risk premium or the market expected return
  - calculate the weighted average beta using the provided capitalization for each stock and then use this beta, the given risk-free rate and the calculated market risk premium to calculate the portfolio expected return OR
  - calculate the expected return for each stock using the given risk-free rate and the given beta as well as the calculated market risk premium. Then candidates needed to calculate the portfolio's expected return using the weighted average of the returns based on the given stock capitalizations.
- Common mistakes include:
  - not using the correct market price of risk ratio.
  - providing a final answer in dollars instead of providing the percentage expected return (i.e. 11,169 instead of 11.169%) (as printed).
  - using the formula of sharp ratio instead of the correct formula for market price of risk ratio.

## Part b (0.75 points)

Explain the zero-beta version of the CAPM.

### Explanation
Zero-beta CAPM is used when borrowing is restricted. Construct the zero-beta portfolio on the underside of the efficient frontier & use in place of the risk-free asset.

Alternatively:

This incorporates fact that investors can't borrow at risk-free rate. Zero-beta portfolio is uncorrelated with original portfolio on opposite side of the efficient frontier. Investors will invest in higher beta stocks: zero-beta CAPM adjusts expected return.

### Examiner Report
- Candidates were expected to explain the zero-beta CAPM.
- Common mistakes include:
  - Stating that the zero-beta portfolio has the same risk as the given risky portfolio did not receive credit unless the candidate explicitly stated that the zero-beta portfolio has the same risk as the corresponding risky portfolio in a situation where the minimum variance frontier is symmetric around the global minimum variance portfolio.
  - Stating that zero beta portfolio is a stock on the inefficient frontier. The zero-beta portfolio is a portfolio of stocks constructed to have a beta of zero.
  - Stating that zero-beta portfolio is negatively correlated with the market portfolio instead of stating that it is uncorrelated with the market portfolio.
  - Stating that zero-beta portfolio is uncorrelated to the entire efficient frontier
  - Stating that there are short-selling restrictions without specifying borrowing restrictions at risk-free rate.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Assume the zero-beta version of the CAPM holds. The expected rate of return on the zero-beta portfolio is 8%. Calculate the expected rate of return on the above portfolio.

### Explanation
Replace the risk-free rate with the expected return on the zero-beta portfolio, keeping the portfolio beta from part a:

$$E(r) = 0.08 + 1.115(0.11 - 0.08) = 0.11245 \text{ (as printed)} = 11.345\%$$

Alternatively:

Stock by stock, $r_i = r_z + \beta_i(r_M - r_z)$: A 0.113, B 0.1175, C 0.107, so $R_p = \sum w_i r_i = 11.345\%$.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to replace the risk-free rate with the given expected return on the zero-beta portfolio.

Common mistakes include:

- Recalculating the market risk premium based on incorrect assumption that the given market price of risk would be equal to $(E(r_M) - E(r_z)) / \sigma_M^2$.
- Recalculating $E(r_z)$ based on incorrect assumption that the given market price of risk would be equal to $(E(r_M) - E(r_z)) / \sigma_M^2$.
- Using value of $E(R_M)$ derived in part a in part c calculation for $E(r_M)$.
