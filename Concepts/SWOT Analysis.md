**SWOT Analysis** is a strategic planning method that lists an organization's strengths and weaknesses, which are internal to it, and the opportunities and threats in its environment, which are external, so that its strategy can be built from the match between the two. It is one of the two methods for analyzing an insurer's environment in strategic management, beside [[Porter's Five Forces]], which gives the structured view of the external half.

Every factor is placed by two questions: whether it is inside the organization or outside it, and whether it helps or harms the organization's goals.

| | Helpful | Harmful |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Internal | Strengths (S) | Weaknesses (W) |
| External | Opportunities (O) | Threats (T) |

## Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths and weaknesses are the organization's own resources and capabilities relative to its competitors, such as its capital, brand, distribution, underwriting and claims expertise, data, systems, expense ratio and people. A strength is only a strength if it is better than what competitors have or matters to customers, so a capability every competitor also has is not one. An insurer's concentration of policies in an exposed area, its expense ratio and its loss experience describe the insurer itself, so they are strengths or weaknesses, even when the hazard behind them, such as wildfire, is external.

## Opportunities and threats

Opportunities and threats come from the [[External Environment|external environment]]: customers, competitors, regulation, the economy, technology, society and the physical environment. The organization can't control them, only respond to them. The five forces give a structured way to find them in the industry, and a scan of the wider environment finds the rest.

## Matching the lists

Pairing each internal list with each external one gives four kinds of strategic options, sometimes called a TOWS matrix:

1. **Strength-opportunity (SO)** options use a strength to pursue an opportunity.
2. **Strength-threat (ST)** options use a strength to defend against a threat.
3. **Weakness-opportunity (WO)** options fix a weakness so as to pursue an opportunity.
4. **Weakness-threat (WT)** options limit exposure where a weakness meets a threat.

A strategy is built from the match between the two lists, not from either list alone.

> [!example]- What are this insurer's SWOT and one option from each pairing? {Example}
> A regional mutual insurer writes homeowners insurance in one western state through independent agents. With illustrative facts:
>
> - It has a 70-year reputation for fair claims handling, a strong surplus and loyal agents.
> - Its policy administration system is outdated, it has little catastrophe modelling expertise, and 40% of its policies are in areas exposed to wildfire.
> - National insurers are withdrawing from wildfire-exposed areas, and demand for homeowners coverage there is unmet.
> - Regulators are allowing wildfire mitigation discounts.
> - Reinsurance for wildfire is getting more expensive, and an insurtech is offering instant online quotes in the state.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The strengths are its claims reputation, strong surplus and loyal agents.
> > - The weaknesses are its outdated system, thin catastrophe modelling expertise and the concentration of its book in wildfire areas, which is internal because it describes the insurer's own portfolio.
> > - The opportunities are the unmet demand left by national insurers withdrawing and the approval of mitigation discounts.
> > - The threats are the rising cost of wildfire reinsurance and the insurtech competitor.
> > - An SO option uses its surplus and agents to write selected business the national insurers are leaving, priced with mitigation discounts that reward safer homes.
> > - An ST option puts its claims reputation in its agents' selling, since the insurtech competes on convenience but has no claims record.
> > - A WO option licenses a [[Catastrophe Modelling|catastrophe model]] or hires modelling staff before growing in wildfire areas, so the new business is priced and selected well.
> > - A WT option limits [[Concentration Risk|concentration]] in the most exposed areas, buys reinsurance before costs rise further, and replaces the policy system so agents can quote as fast as the insurtech.
> > - The SWOT does not choose among the options, so the insurer weighs them against its goals and [[Risk Appetite|risk appetite]].

## SWOT in strategic management

The SWOT comes after the insurer sets its mission and goals and before it chooses a strategy, in the [[Strategic Management Process|strategic management process]]. Its results feed the choice of [[Business Strategy|strategy]] at the corporate and business levels ([[Corporate-Level Strategy]], [[Business-Level Strategy]]), and it is repeated as conditions change.

An opportunity must not be confused with the strategic move that would exploit it: an untapped market is the opportunity, and entering it is the move. SWOT is a relatively simple tool, best used to generate ideas rather than as a rigorous method for selecting strategies, so its results are a starting point for crafting strategy.

> [!trap] Listing an external factor as a weakness
> Ask whether the organization controls the factor. A competitor's new product, a regulation or a recession is external, so it is a threat or an opportunity, never a weakness, however much it hurts.
