Given the following information:

| Accident Year | On-Level Earned Premium (\$000) | Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000) | Reported CDF to Ultimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 750 | 500 | 1.100 |
| 2015 | 800 | 475 | 1.250 |
| 2016 | 1,000 | 400 | 1.550 |

- A legislative change effective January 1, 2016 is expected to reduce claims costs by 20% for claims occurring after the effective date.

Estimate ultimate claims for accident year 2016 using the Cape Cod technique.

### Answer
Approximately \$613 thousand

### Explanation
The Cape Cod technique is a Bornhuetter-Ferguson in which the expected claim ratio is not selected but **derived from the data itself**, as total adjusted reported claims over total *used-up* premium.

**Step 1 — put all accident years on the 2016 claim cost level.** The legislative change takes effect January 1, 2016 and reduces claim costs by 20% for accidents after that date. Accident year 2016 is entirely post-reform and needs no adjustment; accident years 2014 and 2015 are entirely pre-reform and must be multiplied by $1 - 0.20 = 0.80$ to state them at the post-reform level:

| AY | Reported claims | Reform factor | Adjusted reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 500 | 0.80 | 400 |
| 2015 | 475 | 0.80 | 380 |
| 2016 | 400 | 1.00 | 400 |
| **Total** | | | **1,180** |

**Step 2 — used-up premium.** Only the portion of each year's premium corresponding to claims already reported belongs in the denominator:

$$\text{Used-up premium}_{AY} = \frac{\text{On-level earned premium}}{CDF_{AY}}$$

| AY | OLEP | CDF | Used-up premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 750 | 1.100 | 681.82 |
| 2015 | 800 | 1.250 | 640.00 |
| 2016 | 1,000 | 1.550 | 645.16 |
| **Total** | | | **1,966.98** |

**Step 3 — the Cape Cod expected claim ratio.** One ratio over all years, aggregated numerator over aggregated denominator:

$$ECR = \frac{1{,}180}{1{,}966.98} = 0.600$$

Note that reported claims are used *undeveloped* in the numerator precisely because the denominator has already been reduced to the used-up (i.e. reported) portion of the premium. Developing the claims to ultimate *and* using used-up premium would double-count the development.

**Step 4 — apply Bornhuetter-Ferguson to accident year 2016.**

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2016} = \text{Reported} + ECR \times \text{OLEP} \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{CDF}\right)$$
$$= 400 + 0.600 \times 1{,}000 \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{1.550}\right) = 400 + 600 \times 0.3548 = \$612.9 \text{ thousand}$$

Since accident year 2016 is already at the post-reform level, no reform adjustment is undone at the end. Had a pre-reform year been estimated, the expected-claims term would have to be restated back to that year's cost level by dividing by 0.80.

**Alternatively**, an equivalent formulation develops the adjusted reported claims to ultimate and divides by *total* on-level earned premium:

$$ECR = \frac{400(1.100) + 380(1.250) + 400(1.550)}{750 + 800 + 1{,}000} = \frac{1{,}535}{2{,}550} = 0.602$$

giving $400 + 0.602 \times 1{,}000 \times (1 - 1/1.550) = \$613.6$ thousand. What is not acceptable is mixing the two — developed claims over used-up premium.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to know the mechanics and assumptions of the Cape Cod estimation technique and to adjust reported claims for the legislative change before deriving the expected claim ratio.

Common errors included not adjusting reported claims for the legislative change, or using the wrong reform factor by accident year — the correct factors are 0.80 for accident years 2014 and 2015 and 1.00 for accident year 2016, not 1.20, and not 0.80 applied to 2016. Others derived the expected claim ratio improperly by developing reported claims to ultimate while also dividing by used-up premium, or by failing to compute used-up premium at all; some simply selected an expected claim ratio rather than deriving it from aggregated claims and used-up premium. A final common error was applying the ratio directly to earned premium to obtain ultimate claims instead of using the Bornhuetter-Ferguson form with the percent unreported.
