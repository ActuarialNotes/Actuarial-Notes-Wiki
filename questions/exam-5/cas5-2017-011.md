Given the following information:

| Size of Loss (\$000) | Loss Distribution | Average Reported Loss (\$000) |
|---|---|---|
| X <= 200 | 20% | 100 |
| 200 < X <= 400 | 20% | 300 |
| 400 < X <= 600 | 20% | 500 |
| 600 < X <= 800 | 20% | 700 |
| 800 < X <= 1,000 | 20% | 900 |
| Total | 100% | 500 |

- Expected claim frequency = 1%.
- Expected losses are uniformly distributed.
- A home is valued at \$1,000,000.

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the rate per \$1,000 of coverage for the home at the following amounts of insurance:

i. \$1,000,000
ii. \$600,000

### Answer
i. \$5.00 per \$1,000 of coverage; ii. \$7.00 per \$1,000 of coverage

### Explanation
The rate per \$1,000 of coverage is the pure premium divided by the amount of insurance in thousands:

$$\text{Rate per \$1{,}000} = \frac{\text{frequency} \times \text{severity limited to the AOI}}{\text{AOI}/1{,}000}$$

Two things must move when the amount of insurance changes: the severity is **capped** at the amount of insurance, and the **denominator** is the amount of insurance actually purchased.

**i. AOI = \$1,000,000 (insured to value).** No loss can exceed the home's \$1,000,000 value, so nothing is capped and the expected severity is the given total of \$500,000:

$$\text{PP} = 0.01 \times 500{,}000 = \$5{,}000 \qquad \text{Rate} = \frac{5{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000/1{,}000} = \mathbf{\$5.00}$$

**ii. AOI = \$600,000.** Losses above \$600,000 are paid only up to the limit, so the top two bands — 40% of losses in total — contribute \$600,000 each rather than their unlimited averages:

$$E[\min(X, 600{,}000)] = 0.20(100{,}000) + 0.20(300{,}000) + 0.20(500{,}000) + 0.40(600{,}000)$$

$$= 20{,}000 + 60{,}000 + 100{,}000 + 240{,}000 = \$420{,}000$$

$$\text{PP} = 0.01 \times 420{,}000 = \$4{,}200 \qquad \text{Rate} = \frac{4{,}200}{600{,}000/1{,}000} = \mathbf{\$7.00}$$

The rate per unit of coverage is *higher* at the lower amount of insurance, because the first \$600,000 of every loss is the layer most likely to be hit. This decreasing rate per \$1,000 as the amount of insurance rises is exactly why an insurer that assumes insurance to value undercharges the underinsured.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to illustrate knowledge of coinsurance calculations and their implications.

Candidates were expected to calculate rates both with full insurance to value and with underinsurance. That process included capping severities in the upper layers of the expected loss distribution as well as calculating a pure premium and subsequent rate.

Common errors included:

- Not recognizing the need for capping in the loss distribution.
- Ignoring the frequency component of the calculation.
- Using the wrong limit in the denominator of the rate calculation (usually using \$1 million in the denominator for both limits).
- Performing calculations for just one of the limits provided.
- Using incorrect values in calculated (as printed) the expected loss including using percentage weights that did not sum to 1.0 (ex. 20% x 100 + 20% x 300 + 20% x 500 = 180).

## Part b (0.5 points)

Briefly discuss a problem associated with underinsurance from the following perspectives:

i. Insured
ii. Insurer

### Explanation
**i. Insured.** The insured is not made whole. A total or near-total loss exceeds the amount of insurance, so the shortfall is retained personally. Even for a partial loss the insured is exposed to a coinsurance penalty: carrying less than the coinsurance requirement means the loss is reimbursed only in the proportion the amount of insurance bears to the required amount, so the insured absorbs part of a loss that is well within the policy limit.

**ii. Insurer.** Rates are inadequate for the underinsured business. Rate per \$1,000 of coverage decreases as the amount of insurance rises, so if the insurer prices as though every home were insured to value it charges the underinsured home a rate built for a lower rate per unit than that home actually requires. Note the failure is specific to the underinsured group — the policyholder insured to value is being charged an adequate rate, not an excessive one that subsidizes anybody.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify a shortcoming of underinsurance from both the perspective of the insured and the insurer.

Common errors included:

- Assuming those who are fully insured are paying a rate that subsidizes the underinsured when, in reality, the rate charged to the fully insured policyholder is just adequate and not excessive.
- Identifying regulator actions after a catastrophe as a possible disadvantage to the insurer. As this is an issue that would likely cause payments higher than purchased limits on both those underinsured and those insured to value (due to increased cost of construction, for example), the candidates did not identify a problem specific to underinsurance.
- Using "rates are inequitable or inappropriate" as an issue for the insurer. There is a broad array of issues that cause rates to be inequitable – candidates need to demonstrate that they understand the mechanics of what is happening specific to underinsurance. This simple statement does not demonstrate which rate is equitable and which is not (between the fully insured and underinsured). This answer could reasonably be interpreted as stating that both groups of insureds are being charged the wrong rate but actually those insured to value are being charged the correct rate.

## Part c (1 points)

The home is insured for \$700,000 with no deductible and a coinsurance requirement of 80%. Calculate the indemnity payments and coinsurance penalties for the following losses:

i. \$600,000
ii. \$850,000

### Answer
i. Indemnity \$525,000, penalty \$75,000; ii. Indemnity \$700,000, penalty \$0

### Explanation
The coinsurance requirement is 80% of the home's \$1,000,000 value, or \$800,000. The insured carries \$700,000, which is short of that, so the apportionment ratio is

$$a = \min\!\left(1, \; \frac{\text{amount of insurance}}{\text{coinsurance \%} \times \text{value}}\right) = \min\!\left(1, \frac{700{,}000}{0.80 \times 1{,}000{,}000}\right) = 0.875$$

The indemnity payment is the apportioned loss, capped at the policy limit; the penalty is what would have been paid without the coinsurance provision less what is actually paid:

$$\text{Indemnity} = \min\!\left(\text{AOI}, \; a \times L\right) \qquad \text{Penalty} = \min(\text{AOI}, L) - \text{Indemnity}$$

**i. Loss = \$600,000.**

$$\text{Indemnity} = \min(700{,}000, \; 0.875 \times 600{,}000) = \min(700{,}000, \; 525{,}000) = \$525{,}000$$

$$\text{Penalty} = 600{,}000 - 525{,}000 = \$75{,}000$$

**ii. Loss = \$850,000.**

$$0.875 \times 850{,}000 = 743{,}750 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \text{Indemnity} = \min(700{,}000, \; 743{,}750) = \$700{,}000$$

Without the coinsurance clause the payment would also have been capped at the \$700,000 limit, so

$$\text{Penalty} = 700{,}000 - 700{,}000 = \$0$$

Once the apportioned loss reaches the amount of insurance the policy limit binds instead of the coinsurance clause, and the penalty disappears.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate indemnity payments and coinsurance penalties for an underinsured policy given two loss scenarios.

Common errors included:

- Errors in calculating the coinsurance requirement or apportionment ratio.
- Performing calculations for just one of the losses provided.
- Not recognizing the need to cap the indemnity payment at the policy limit of \$700,000 for the second loss amount.
- Calculating only indemnity payments or only penalties but not both.
- Using incorrect values (especially the loss amount or amount of insurance) in the calculation of indemnity payments or penalties.
