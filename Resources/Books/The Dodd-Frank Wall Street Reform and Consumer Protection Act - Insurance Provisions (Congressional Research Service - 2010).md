A Congressional Research Service report, dated August 17, 2010, that explains how insurance markets were affected by the financial crisis and summarizes the provisions of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (P.L. 111-203) that pertain to insurance. It notes that the act did not create a federal regulatory system for insurance, and states that the report will not be updated.

## Summary

## Insurance and the Financial Crisis
- Under the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, insurance regulation is generally left to the individual states.
- The involvement of AIG and the smaller bond insurers changed the tenor of the debate around insurance regulation, with increased emphasis on the systemic importance of some insurance companies.
- The insurance industry saw two significant failures: the financial guarantee or "monoline" bond insurers, and AIG, which essentially failed in mid-September 2008.
- AIG was primarily state-chartered insurance subsidiaries, but at the holding company level it was a federally regulated thrift holding company overseen by the Office of Thrift Supervision.

## The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (P.L. 111-203)
- The act passed the House on June 30, 2010 and the Senate on July 15, 2010, and was signed on July 21, 2010.

### Federal Insurance Office
- Title V, Subtitle A creates a Federal Insurance Office (FIO) inside the Department of the Treasury, to monitor all aspects of the insurance industry and coordinate and develop policy relating to international agreements.
- FIO can preempt state laws and regulations that conflict with international agreements, only where the state measure results in less favorable treatment of a non-U.S. insurer than a U.S. insurer and is inconsistent with a written international agreement on prudential measures.
- FIO's preemption authority does not extend to state measures governing rates, premiums, underwriting or sales practices, state coverage requirements or state antitrust laws.

### Consumer Financial Protection
- Title X creates a Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection within the Federal Reserve; consumer protection issues relating to the business of insurance do not fall under the bureau's oversight and remain with the states.

### Investor Protection and Securities Provisions
- Section 989J is broadly aimed at returning indexed annuities solely to state oversight, with the exemption from SEC oversight depending in part on the states or the companies meeting certain consumer protection standards.
- Section 913 authorizes the SEC to establish a fiduciary duty for broker-dealers who give personalized investment advice, which may affect insurance agents who sell securities products.

### Systemic Risk Provisions
- Financial companies, including insurers, judged systemically significant by the Financial Stability Oversight Council are subject to Federal Reserve oversight and higher prudential standards.
- The Volcker Rule (Section 619) restricts proprietary trading by banking entities, with an exemption for trading by a regulated insurance company for its general account.
- Insurance subsidiaries of a financial company in the act's special resolution regime are not subject to it; their resolution continues under the state insurance resolution system.

### Surplus Lines and Reinsurance
- Title V, Subtitle B, the Nonadmitted and Reinsurance Reform Act of 2010, vests the home state of the insured with sole authority to regulate and collect taxes on a surplus lines transaction.
- For reinsurance, it vests the home state of the insurer purchasing the reinsurance with authority over the transaction, and the home state of the reinsurer with sole authority to regulate the reinsurer's solvency.
