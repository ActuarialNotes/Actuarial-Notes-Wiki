Given the following loss data for a property insurer:

**Loss Peril**

| Accident Year | Sizes of loss | Fire Claim Counts | Fire Ground-Up Losses (\$) | Flood Claim Counts | Flood Ground-Up Losses (\$) | Other Claim Counts | Other Ground-Up Losses (\$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 0-\$500,000 | 800 | 80,000,000 | 50 | 2,500,000 | 1,500 | 15,000,000 |
|  | > \$500,000 | 20 | 12,000,000 | 30 | 16,500,000 |  |  |
| 2015 | 0-\$500,000 | 780 | 81,900,000 |  |  | 1,450 | 15,225,000 |
|  | > \$500,000 | 10 | 6,300,000 |  |  | 5 | 4,020,000 |
| 2016 | 0-\$500,000 | 750 | 82,687,500 |  |  | 1,550 | 17,088,750 |
|  | > \$500,000 | 25 | 13,891,500 |  |  |  |  |

|  |  |
|---|---|
| \$500,000 | Excess loss threshold used by the insurer for individual reported losses |

The insurer's claims information relating to all major events that occurred in the past 3 years are:

- The definition of catastrophe losses is \$25 million in losses across the industry.
- All flood claims were caused by a single flood event that occurred in 2014, causing \$500 million direct insured losses in the industry.
- In 2016, one fire claim occurred for the insurer as the result of a forest fire, causing \$25 million direct insured losses in the industry.
- Ground-up loss of this fire claim is \$1,000,000.
- There is no further development on losses.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Calculate the excess loss factor.

### Explanation
Losses are capped at the \$500,000 threshold: every loss below it is non-excess, and each loss above it contributes \$500,000 to non-excess losses and the remainder to excess losses. The excess loss factor is

$$\text{ELF} = 1 + \frac{\text{excess losses}}{\text{non-excess losses}}$$

This answer computes a **non-catastrophe** factor, removing both catastrophes from the data (the report also accepted including all losses, or combining a cat load with a non-cat excess factor):

- The 2014 flood is a catastrophe (\$500 million of industry losses, above the \$25 million definition), so all flood losses are removed.
- The 2016 forest fire (\$25 million of industry losses) meets the definition, so its \$1,000,000 claim is removed from 2016 fire losses above \$500,000, leaving 24 claims and \$12,891,500.

| | Non-excess losses | Excess losses |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 fire | 80,000,000 + 20 × 500,000 = 90,000,000 | 12,000,000 - 10,000,000 = 2,000,000 |
| 2015 fire | 81,900,000 + 10 × 500,000 = 86,900,000 | 6,300,000 - 5,000,000 = 1,300,000 |
| 2016 fire | 82,687,500 + 24 × 500,000 = 94,687,500 | 12,891,500 - 12,000,000 = 891,500 |
| 2014 other | 15,000,000 | 0 |
| 2015 other | 15,225,000 + 5 × 500,000 = 17,725,000 | 4,020,000 - 2,500,000 = 1,520,000 |
| 2016 other | 17,088,750 | 0 |
| **Total** | **321,401,250** | **5,711,500** |

$$\text{ELF} = 1 + \frac{5{,}711{,}500}{321{,}401{,}250} = 1.0178$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate an excess loss factor for property insurance utilizing ground up loss data including catastrophes. Candidates were also expected to describe the purpose for incorporating an excess loss factor into the ratemaking process in lieu of unadjusted loss data.

Candidates were expected to calculate an excess loss factor by calculating losses limited to \$500k (non-excess losses) and losses excess of \$500k (excess losses). Losses were provided ground up split into two categories, losses < \$500k and losses >\$500k. Candidates needed to split the losses > \$500k into non-excess losses and excess losses, while all losses < \$500k fell into the non-excess losses. The excess loss factor was calculated as the ratio of excess losses to non-excess losses + 1.
Three cat treatments were accepted:

1. Calculating a non-cat excess loss factor, i.e. excluding all catastrophes from the loss data
2. Calculating an excess loss factor for both non-cat and cat losses, i.e. including all data
3. Calculating a combined cat load and excess loss factor, i.e. ground up cat losses plus excess non-cat losses

Common errors included:

- Calculating an excess loss ratio, as the question explicitly asked for an excess loss factor.
- Calculating the excess loss factor as 1 – Loss Elimination Ratio.
- Calculating the excess loss factor as Excess Losses / Total Losses.
- The data was presented as ground up losses for two categories – losses less than \$500k and losses greater than \$500k. Some candidates misinterpreted the buckets to be limited and excess losses and made no adjustments to split the losses >\$500k into non-excess and excess losses.
- Treating the data as if it were censored data, as the labels in the table clearly note the data is ground up.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Describe one reason to use an excess loss factor when setting property insurance rates.

### Explanation
Large and catastrophe losses are rare and volatile from year to year. Including them as they happened would make the rate indication swing with whether a large loss occurred in the experience period. Capping each loss at the threshold and adding back an excess loss factor — built from a longer-term average of excess losses — smooths the indication so that each year's premium carries a stable provision for large losses rather than the actual large losses of the experience years.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify the volatility inherent in excess / catastrophe losses from year to year and describe how incorporating the excess loss factor approach in premium / rate indications will smooth out results by capping losses and adding on a longer-term average of excess / catastrophe losses.

Common errors included:

- Not expanding on reason for using excess loss factor.
- Using excess loss factors to accommodate censored data, as ELFs are not used to account for data limitations but rather to smooth out volatility.
- For use in reinsurance pricing or determining layers to reinsure, as ELFs may be used in the process of pricing reinsurance but would not be the reason to incorporate ELFs in the ratemaking process.
