A company that self-insures has the following limited historical information:

**Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 4,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 | 8,250 |
| 2013 | 5,000 | 7,500 | 9,375 | |
| 2014 | 6,000 | 9,000 | | |
| 2015 | 7,500 | | | |

**Cumulative Paid Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1,600 | 4,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 |
| 2013 | 2,000 | 5,000 | 7,500 | |
| 2014 | 2,400 | 6,000 | | |
| 2015 | 3,000 | | | |

**Industry Benchmark Claims Development Factors**

| Age-Age | Reported | Paid |
|---|---|---|
| 60-Ult | 1.015 | 1.100 |
| 48-60 | 1.025 | 1.150 |
| 36-48 | 1.050 | 1.250 |
| 24-36 | 1.150 | 1.500 |
| 12-24 | 1.250 | 2.500 |

- Case outstanding for accident year 2011 as of December 31, 2015 = \$500,000.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Use the industry benchmark claims development factors to estimate the unpaid claims for accident year 2011 as of December 31, 2015.

### Answer
\$597,059

### Explanation
Accident year 2011 is at **60 months** as of December 31, 2015, and case outstanding is the only company figure available for it. The case outstanding development technique converts case reserves into unpaid claims by scaling them with a factor built out of the reported and paid development factors at that age.

**Step 1 — express reported, paid and case in terms of one unknown ultimate.** Let $U$ be the ultimate for accident year 2011, and let $r = 1.015$ and $p = 1.100$ be the 60-to-ultimate reported and paid factors:

$$\text{Reported}_{60} = \frac{U}{r}, \qquad \text{Paid}_{60} = \frac{U}{p}, \qquad \text{Case}_{60} = \frac{U}{r} - \frac{U}{p}$$

**Step 2 — form the ratio of unpaid to case outstanding.** Unpaid claims are the ultimate less what has been paid:

$$\frac{\text{Unpaid}}{\text{Case}} = \frac{U - U/p}{U/r - U/p} = \frac{1 - 1/p}{1/r - 1/p}$$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by $pr$ clears the fractions and gives the compact form:

$$\frac{\text{Unpaid}}{\text{Case}} = \frac{r(p-1)}{p-r} = \frac{1.015 \times 0.100}{1.100 - 1.015} = \frac{0.1015}{0.085} = 1.19412$$

**Step 3 — apply it to the company's case outstanding.**

$$\text{Unpaid}_{2011} = 1.19412 \times \$500{,}000 = \mathbf{\$597{,}059}$$

Note that this factor is greater than one: it already includes the case outstanding itself plus the further development on top of it, so the \$500,000 must not be added again at the end.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to apply the case outstanding development technique, deriving the factor from the 60-to-ultimate reported and paid benchmark development factors and multiplying it by the given case outstanding.

Common errors included using the wrong form of the case outstanding factor; picking the 12-to-ultimate factors rather than the 60-to-ultimate factors appropriate to accident year 2011's maturity; and omitting the case outstanding amount itself from the final unpaid figure.

## Part b (1 points)

Assess the reasonableness of using the industry benchmark reported and paid claims development factors for this company.

### Explanation
The test is to build the company's own age-to-age factors from the two triangles and compare them column by column with the benchmarks.

**Reported claims age-to-age factors:**

| Accident Year | 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 1.500 | 1.250 | 1.100 |
| 2013 | 1.500 | 1.250 | |
| 2014 | 1.500 | | |
| **Company selected** | **1.500** | **1.250** | **1.100** |
| Industry benchmark | 1.250 | 1.150 | 1.050 |

The company's reported claims develop **substantially faster** than the industry at every age — 50% versus 25% over the first year, and materially higher at 24-36 and 36-48 as well. The industry reported factors are far too low for this company and are **not reasonable** to use.

**Paid claims age-to-age factors:**

| Accident Year | 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | 2.500 | 1.500 | 1.250 |
| 2013 | 2.500 | 1.500 | |
| 2014 | 2.500 | | |
| **Company selected** | **2.500** | **1.500** | **1.250** |
| Industry benchmark | 2.500 | 1.500 | 1.250 |

The company's paid factors match the industry benchmarks exactly at every age. The payment and settlement pattern is in line with the industry, so the industry paid factors **are reasonable** for this company.

Taken together the comparison points at one cause: the paid patterns agree, so the difference must sit in the case reserves. This company carries a different case reserve adequacy or philosophy from the industry — its reported claims are lower relative to ultimate at each age, which is why they must develop faster to reach the same place.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute company age-to-age factors from both triangles, compare each set against the corresponding industry benchmark, and then state a conclusion on the reasonableness of using the benchmarks — separately for reported and for paid.

The common error was performing the comparison but never stating whether the industry factors were reasonable for this company.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Given the response to part b. above, discuss the reasonableness of the estimate in part a. above.

### Answer
It is unreasonable — the estimate is understated.

### Explanation
The part a. calculation uses one company-specific input, the \$500,000 of case outstanding, and two benchmark inputs. Part b. established that only one of those benchmarks is defensible: the paid factor is in line with the company, but the reported factor is not — the industry's 60-to-ultimate reported factor of 1.015 is far too low for a company whose reported claims develop at roughly twice the industry rate.

Trace the error through the factor $r(p-1)/(p-r)$. Raising $r$ toward its true company level increases the numerator and shrinks the denominator, so the true unpaid-to-case ratio is larger than 1.194. The estimate in part a. is therefore **understated**, and the response is not reasonable as it stands.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to link the finding that the industry reported factors are inappropriate for this company to a specific direction of error in part a. — that the estimate is understated and therefore unreasonable.

Common errors included calling the part a. answer inaccurate without identifying the direction, and concluding that it was overstated.
