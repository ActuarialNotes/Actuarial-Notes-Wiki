## Part a (0.5 points)

Briefly explain whether this variable should be included in the rating plan.

### Explanation
This is a **consistency-over-time test**: the same rating variable is fitted separately by accident year and the year-by-year patterns are compared. What matters is not the level of any one line but whether the *shape* repeats.

Here it does. Every one of the four accident years falls from level 0 to level 1, the lines never cross, and the drop is large in relative terms — roughly 55% to 75% of the level-0 value in each year. A signal that reproduces itself in four independent years is unlikely to be noise, so the variable has genuine predictive power and should be included in the rating plan.

The one thing that argues the other way is volume. The bars show only about 60 exposures at level 0 and about 15 at level 1 across all four years — on the order of 15 and 4 exposures per accident year. A response that identified the consistent downward pattern and then declined to include the variable because the differential rests on so few exposures (and so is not credible) also earned full credit; the required observation is the consistency across accident years, not the include/exclude verdict.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recognize the consistent downward pattern between the two levels of Rating Factor 1 across all four accident years, and to read that consistency as evidence of predictive power. Full credit was available for either conclusion — include or exclude — as long as the consistent pattern was identified, so a candidate who noted the thin exposure volume or another confounding factor and chose not to include the variable was not penalized.

A common mistake was focusing on the absolute values of the predicted values rather than on the relativity between the two levels or the trend across years.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Briefly discuss two reasons why GLM analysis is typically performed on loss cost data instead of loss ratios.

### Explanation
Two reasons, either pair of which earns credit:

**1. Modelling loss ratios requires premium on level, which is hard at the record level.** A loss ratio needs a premium denominator restated to current rate level. On-leveling is straightforward in aggregate (the parallelogram or extension-of-exposures methods) but awkward for the individual records a GLM is fitted to, and any residual rate-level distortion in the denominator flows straight into the fitted relativities. Loss cost — losses per exposure — needs no such adjustment.

**2. Loss cost has well-understood distributional forms; loss ratios do not.** Frequency and severity have standard, defensible model choices (a Poisson or negative binomial frequency, a gamma or lognormal severity, or a Tweedie for pure premium directly), which is exactly what a GLM needs in order to specify an error structure and link function. There is no standard probability distribution for a loss ratio.

Two further reasons that also earned credit: modelling loss cost lets the actuary split the result into frequency and severity, which gives insight into which part of the claims process a variable is driving — a split a loss ratio cannot support; and industry loss cost data is published by bureaus such as ISO and NCCI, so a loss cost model can be benchmarked against the wider market, while an experienced actuary's a priori expectations about frequency and severity trends give a sanity check that loss ratio output does not.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to recall two distinct reasons that actuaries generally model loss costs rather than loss ratios in a GLM.

Common mistakes included listing advantages of GLMs over univariate methods, which answers a different question, and giving the same reason twice in two forms.
