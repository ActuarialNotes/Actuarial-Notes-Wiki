With illustrative figures in thousands of dollars, a firm has annual revenue of \$5,000, salaries of \$3,000, other operating costs of \$1,200 and interest of \$300. Its owners value it at 10 times its annual profit. The firm adopts a safety and insurance program that costs \$100 a year. As a result, customers buy more, raising revenue by \$60; employees accept \$40 less in total pay; and lenders cut its interest by \$20. The owners keep the multiple of 10. What is the firm's value after the program?

- A) \$4,000
- B) \$5,000
- C) \$5,200
- D) \$6,200

## Explanation

Profit before the program, and the value it supports:

$$
\begin{align*}
\pi_{\text{before}} &= 5{,}000 - 3{,}000 - 1{,}200 - 300 \\
&= 500 \\
V_{\text{before}} &= 10 \times 500 \\
&= 5{,}000
\end{align*}
$$

After the program, every term changes:

$$
\begin{align*}
\pi_{\text{after}} &= 5{,}060 - 100 - 2{,}960 - 1{,}200 - 280 \\
&= 520 \\
V_{\text{after}} &= 10 \times 520 \\
&= \mathbf{5{,}200}
\end{align*}
$$

The program costs \$100 a year but raises value, because customers, employees and lenders respond to a more secure firm. The answer is **C**.

**Why not the others**

- **A** — \$4,000 counts the program's \$100 cost and nothing else (ten times \$400). It ignores the benefits to other stakeholders, which are how risk management creates value.
- **B** — \$5,000 is the value before the program, as if the cost and the benefits had no effect.
- **D** — \$6,200 counts the benefits but forgets to deduct the \$100 program cost (ten times \$620).
