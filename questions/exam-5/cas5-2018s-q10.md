A company is considering whether to use vehicle color as a private passenger auto rating variable for bodily injury coverage.

## Part a (1 point)

Evaluate the use of vehicle color as a rating variable using two operational criteria.

### Explanation
Two operational criteria:

- **Objective definition:** a rating variable should have clear, unambiguous categories. Vehicle color mostly does, but not entirely — multi-tone, custom-painted or wrapped vehicles and borderline shades ("dark red" or "maroon") would need definitions, and different people could classify the same vehicle differently.
- **Verifiability / manipulation:** the insurer must be able to verify the variable. Color is reported by the insured and is not reliably verifiable without an inspection or photographs (the color on file with the manufacturer may not be the current one, since vehicles are repainted). An insured who knows a color is surcharged has an incentive to misreport it.

(Inexpensive to administer is also an operational criterion: color is cheap to collect on the application, but the cost of verifying it and of building it into the system would count against it.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to demonstrate knowledge on the evaluation of practical considerations for the insurer and societal impacts of implementing a new variable, vehicle color, into the private passenger auto bodily injury rating plan.

Candidates were expected to make an insightful assessment of adding vehicle color to the private passenger auto bodily injury rating plan, from the operational perspective of the insurer, by providing and supporting two criteria.

Common errors included:

- Simply listing an operational criteria, or failing to provide any reasoning behind the listed criteria. For example, "Objective - yes."
- Not providing support for assertions of whether color satisfied a given operational criteria. For example, "Color is inexpensive to administer."
- Evaluating the same criteria for both responses. For example, "1. Change in rating algorithm might be costly when involving change in the system, especially to the extent of a complicated algorithm. It might cause a large premium swing to insured due to new system cost if implementing a new rating variable. 2. There is no historical data or information on the new implemented rating variable. It would require a new questionnaire set up and survey to the policyholders. It might also incur an additional cost for extra workforce to conduct the survey. Hence, the use of vehicle color is not recommended as it might incur additional expense to the insurer." Both of these responses are evaluating the "cost" criteria.
- Evaluating vehicle color using statistical, social, and/or legal criteria rather than operational criteria. For example, "Vehicle color is not correlated with bodily injury claims, so this would not be a good rating variable to use."

## Part b (1 point)

Evaluate the use of vehicle color as a rating variable using two social criteria.

### Explanation
Two social criteria:

- **Causality:** the public accepts a rating variable more readily when it has an intuitive causal link to losses. There is no obvious reason the color of a vehicle should cause more or more severe bodily injuries to others, so insureds would see a color-based price as arbitrary and unfair, even if the data showed a correlation.
- **Controllability:** a variable the insured can control lets them lower their premium by reducing risk. Color can be chosen when buying a vehicle, but changing it afterwards means a costly repaint, and choosing a different color does not change how the vehicle is driven — so it gives the insured no real lever to reduce risk.

(Affordability — color is unlikely to drive large premium swings — and privacy — color is not intrusive information — were other social criteria that could be evaluated.)

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected make an insightful assessment of adding vehicle color to the private passenger auto bodily injury rating plan, from the perspective of society.

Common errors included:

- Simply listing a social criteria, or for giving no reasoning behind it. For example, "Not causal."
- Not providing support for assertions of whether color satisfied a given social criteria. For example, "Color does not invade insured's privacy."
- Not tying an assertion back to the perspective of the insured or society. For example, only stating "There is no intuitive relationship between vehicle color and expected BI losses".
- Confusing social criteria with legal or regulatory considerations. For example, "Rating based on color of vehicle is discriminatory. It's not an accepted risk characteristic, and would likely not hold up in court if someone whose rates increased decided to sue."
