**Rate Regulation** is provincial oversight of the premiums insurers may charge. The statutory standard across Canada is that rates be **not excessive, not inadequate, and not unfairly discriminatory** — the same three-part test used in the United States — and it applies most intensively to compulsory private passenger [[Automobile Insurance Reform|automobile insurance]].

> $$\text{Rates must be} \begin{cases} \text{not excessive} & \text{(consumer protection)} \\[4pt] \text{not inadequate} & \text{(solvency)} \\[4pt] \text{not unfairly discriminatory} & \text{(equity)} \end{cases}$$

- **The three regimes**, in decreasing order of regulatory grip:
  - **Prior approval / file-and-approve** — rates may not be used until the regulator approves. Ontario private passenger auto works this way, through [[Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario|FSRA]].
  - **File-and-use** — the insurer files and may use the rates immediately, subject to later disapproval.
  - **Use-and-file / open competition** — the market sets the price; the filing is a record. Most commercial lines and most non-auto personal lines in Canada operate here.
- The tension is direct: prior approval protects affordability in a compulsory line but introduces **lag**, so approved rates chase a cost level that has already moved. In a rising-cost environment lag alone produces inadequacy, which is a [[Solvency Regulation|solvency]] problem the rate regulator does not own.
- A filing is judged on **prospective cost**: historical experience developed to ultimate, trended forward to the average date of loss of the policy period, adjusted for enacted [[Automobile Insurance Reform|reforms]], plus expenses and a permitted profit provision.
- Regulators also approve the **[[Risk Classification Restrictions|risk classification system]]**, not just the overall level. A filing can be approved on level and rejected on structure, and prohibited variables must be excluded regardless of predictive power.
- Suppressed rates do not remove cost — they relocate it, into reduced availability, growth of the [[Residual Market|residual market]] and the [[Facility Association]], and ultimately into insurer withdrawal from the province.

> [!example]- Indicated Versus Approved {Example}
> An insurer's Ontario auto indication is $+11.4\%$. FSRA approves $+4.0\%$. The insurer writes $\$80$ million of Ontario auto premium at a $101\%$ combined ratio before the change.
>
> What is the consequence, and what are the insurer's options?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The shortfall is roughly $11.4\% - 4.0\% = 7.4\%$ of premium — about $\$5.9$ million of annual underwriting result on the current book. The combined ratio worsens rather than improving toward the target.
> >
> > Realistic options, and what each costs:
> >
> > - **Refile with better support.** Sometimes the gap is evidential, not political: a thinly supported trend or an unadjusted reform is the actual reason for the cut.
> > - **Re-underwrite.** Tighten eligibility so the mix shifts to segments adequately priced at the approved level. This works, but it is exactly the mechanism that produces an **availability** problem and grows the [[Facility Association]].
> > - **Reduce writings** in the province, releasing capital to a jurisdiction where the rate is adequate.
> > - **Absorb it** while the rate lag unwinds, if the insurer believes approval will catch up.
> >
> > What the actuary may **not** do is restate the indication downward to match the approval. The indication is a technical estimate; the approved rate is a regulatory decision. Documenting the difference — and its effect on projected [[MCT]] and on the [[FCT]] adverse scenarios — is the actuary's job.
