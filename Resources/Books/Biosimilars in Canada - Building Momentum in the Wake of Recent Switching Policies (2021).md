A slide presentation to the 2021 CADTH Symposium (November 2021), presented by Yvonne Zhang and prepared by Yvonne Zhang and Caroline Peterson of the Patented Medicine Prices Review Board's National Prescription Drug Utilization Information System (NPDUIS) team. It compares the emerging Canadian market for biosimilars with international counterparts and assesses the impact of recent and upcoming biosimilar switching initiatives and the potential for cost savings.

> [!info] On the syllabus
> - [[Exam GH-201C (SOA)|Exam GH-201C]] — Biosimilars in Canada: Building Momentum in the Wake of Recent Switching Policies, Nov 2021; Outcomes 4b.

## Background and Objectives
- Canadian sales of biologics reached nearly $10 billion in 2020, placing Canada among the top-ranked OECD countries in per capita spending.
- Biosimilars offer a significant opportunity for cost savings for Canadian payers.
- The presentation compares the emerging Canadian biosimilar market with international counterparts and assesses the impact of recent and upcoming biosimilar switching initiatives.

## Approach and Data Sources
- The study is part of the PMPRB's Biologics in Canada chartbook series.
- Data sources are IQVIA's MIDAS and Canadian Drugstore and Hospital Purchases Audit (CDH) databases and the NPDUIS database at the Canadian Institute for Health Information.
- Drug costs from the NPDUIS database are the amounts accepted toward reimbursement and do not reflect rebates and confidential product listing agreements.

## Overview
- Biologics market trends in Canada and international comparisons
- Biosimilar availability and uptake in Canada versus other OECD countries
- Biosimilar uptake challenges and current policies
- Cost-saving opportunities from biosimilars

## Biologic medicine sales have tripled over the last decade
- Biologic medicine sales in Canada rose from $3.3 billion in 2011 to $10.0 billion in 2020, a 10-year compound annual growth rate of 13.2 percent, with a 14.6 percent increase in the last year.
- Biologics accounted for one third of pharmaceutical spending in Canada in 2020, with sales per capita of $262.

## Canada placed among the top-ranked countries in the OECD for biologics spending
- In 2020 biologics accounted for 33.9 percent of pharmaceutical sales in Canada, the third-highest share in the OECD, against a median of 27.4 percent.
- Canada's $262 per person in 2020 was the fourth-highest per capita sales among OECD countries.

## Availability of biosimilars in Canada has increased recently, without a major impact on overall costs
- Canada and the US continue to lag the European market in the number of biosimilar approvals and their availability.
- Biosimilars' share of overall biologic sales in Canada remained modest, at 5.4 percent in 2020.

## Biosimilar uptake in Canada is moderate compared to other OECD markets
- Infliximab, the highest-selling biologic, had a 20 percent biosimilar share in Canada in 2020, well below the OECD median of 73 percent.
- Adalimumab, the second top-selling biologic, had no recorded biosimilar sales in Canada in 2020.

## Uptake of biosimilars in the public plans has been modest in 2019/20 owing to a number of limiting factors
- Several key factors influence biosimilar uptake in Canada: interchangeability (biosimilars are not interchangeable with the reference biologic), the strategies of the originator manufacturer to maintain market share, and patient switching (not mandatory).
- Biosimilars recently approved in Canada and reimbursed in NPDUIS public drug plans in 2019/20 are tabulated against their originator biologics.

## National and pan-Canadian approaches related to biosimilars
- Regulatory and pricing: Health Canada's regulatory review, and the PMPRB's October 2020 Guidelines under which patented biosimilars are always classified as Category II.
- Reimbursement: as of June 2019 CADTH no longer reviews biosimilars, and the pCPA's 2018 Biologics Policy Directive requires transparent price reductions to the lowest list price.
- Biosimilar switching policies: since May 2019, public drug plans in British Columbia, Alberta, New Brunswick and Quebec have launched non-medical biosimilar switching policies.

## Several Canadian payers have announced or undertaken initiatives to increase biosimilar uptake
- A table lists the initiatives of public payers by province, and of private payers.

## Implemented biosimilar initiatives show positive signs in terms of increased utilization
- Biosimilar policies in British Columbia led to a dramatic increase in utilization of infliximab biosimilars, while national uptake has been slow, with gradual market penetration.

## Canadians are only realizing a fraction of the potential savings from biosimilars
- Of the savings estimated for 2020, 23 percent ($168 million) were realized and 77 percent ($546 million) were unrealized potential savings.
- The estimates cover infliximab, etanercept and insulin glargine, by province.

## Methods and limitations about the estimation of cost implications and potential savings from higher biosimilar uptakes
- Estimated cost implications compare the status quo with a scenario in which biosimilar uptake matched that prompted by British Columbia's non-medical switching policies.
- Effects on the price levels of biosimilars were not explored.

## Conclusions
- Biologics are a high-growth market segment in Canada, with sales tripling over the last decade.
- Biosimilar availability has increased recently without a major impact on overall costs, and uptake is moderate compared to other OECD markets.
- Ongoing initiatives aim to improve uptake and pricing nationwide, and the potential savings offered by biosimilars are significant in Canada.
