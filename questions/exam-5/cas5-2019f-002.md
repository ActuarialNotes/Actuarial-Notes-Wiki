Given the following policies for an insurance company:

| Policy | Effective Date | Expiration Date | Written Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | March 1, 2017 | February 28, 2018 | 1,200 |
| B | June 1, 2017 | November 30, 2017 | 1,500 |
| C | July 1, 2017 | June 30, 2018 | 2,000 |
| D | October 1, 2017 | September 30, 2018 | 750 |
| E | January 1, 2018 | December 31, 2018 | 900 |
| F | April 1, 2018 | September 30, 2018 | 1,650 |
| G | August 1, 2018 | July 31, 2019 | 1,350 |

## Part a (0.25 points)

Calculate the written premium for the fiscal year ending July 31, 2018.

### Answer
3300

### Explanation
A fiscal year ending July 31, 2018 covers the twelve months **August 1, 2017 through July 31, 2018**. Written premium is assigned in full to the period containing the policy's effective date, so the only question is which policies incepted in that window:

| Policy | Effective Date | In FY 8/1/2017–7/31/2018? |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3/1/2017 | No — before the window |
| B | 6/1/2017 | No — before the window |
| C | 7/1/2017 | No — one month before the window opens |
| D | 10/1/2017 | Yes |
| E | 1/1/2018 | Yes |
| F | 4/1/2018 | Yes |
| G | 8/1/2018 | No — one day after the window closes |

$$750 + 900 + 1{,}650 = 3{,}300$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate written premium over the twelve months from August 1, 2017 to July 31, 2018. Common mistakes included selecting a fiscal year that was longer or shorter than twelve months (for example June 1, 2017 to July 31, 2018), and totalling the written premium for every policy rather than restricting it to the policies incepting inside the fiscal year.

## Part b (0.25 points)

Calculate the in-force premium as of December 15, 2018.

### Answer
2250

### Explanation
In-force premium is the written premium on policies that have incepted and not yet expired on the valuation date. The term lengths differ, so each policy's expiration date has to be checked rather than assumed:

| Policy | Policy period | In force 12/15/2018? |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3/1/2017 – 2/28/2018 | No — expired |
| B | 6/1/2017 – 11/30/2017 | No — expired (6-month policy) |
| C | 7/1/2017 – 6/30/2018 | No — expired |
| D | 10/1/2017 – 9/30/2018 | No — expired |
| E | 1/1/2018 – 12/31/2018 | Yes — 900 |
| F | 4/1/2018 – 9/30/2018 | No — expired (6-month policy) |
| G | 8/1/2018 – 7/31/2019 | Yes — 1,350 |

$$900 + 1{,}350 = 2{,}250$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate the in-force premium as of December 15, 2018. The most common mistake was assuming policy F was a twelve-month policy and therefore still in force on that date, when it is a six-month policy that expired September 30, 2018.

## Part c (0.5 points)

Calculate the 2018 calendar year written premium if Policy C is cancelled on March 31, 2018.

### Answer
3400

### Explanation
Calendar year 2018 written premium picks up every policy incepting during 2018, plus any mid-term premium adjustment recorded during 2018 — and a cancellation is exactly such an adjustment.

**Policies written in 2018:**

$$E + F + G = 900 + 1{,}650 + 1{,}350 = 3{,}900$$

**Cancellation of Policy C.** Policy C runs 7/1/2017 – 6/30/2018 for 2,000. Cancelling it on March 31, 2018 returns the unexpired three months (April 1 through June 30, 2018) of a twelve-month term, booked as negative written premium in calendar year 2018:

$$-2{,}000 \times \frac{3}{12} = -500$$

$$\text{CY2018 WP} = 3{,}900 - 500 = 3{,}400$$

Alternatively, reach the same figure through the written-earned-unearned relationship, $WP = EP + \Delta UEP$. Calendar year 2018 earned premium is

$$1{,}200\left(\tfrac{2}{12}\right) + 2{,}000\left(\tfrac{3}{12}\right) + 750\left(\tfrac{9}{12}\right) + 900 + 1{,}650 + 1{,}350\left(\tfrac{5}{12}\right) = 4{,}375$$

with unearned premium of $1{,}200(\tfrac{2}{12}) + 2{,}000(\tfrac{6}{12}) + 750(\tfrac{9}{12}) = 1{,}762.50$ at 12/31/2017 and $1{,}350(\tfrac{7}{12}) = 787.50$ at 12/31/2018. Then

$$\text{CY2018 WP} = 4{,}375 + (787.50 - 1{,}762.50) = 3{,}400$$

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify policies E, F and G as the business written in 2018, and to recognize how cancelling policy C affects 2018 written premium. Common mistakes included assuming that because policy C was originally written in 2017 its 2018 cancellation had no effect on calendar year 2018 written premium, and calculating calendar year 2018 earned premium instead of written premium.

## Part d (0.5 points)

Discuss if it is appropriate for this insurer to estimate earned premium for the current year by averaging the in-force premium at the end of the current year and prior year.

### Explanation
**It is not appropriate for this insurer.** Averaging beginning and ending in-force premium approximates calendar year earned premium only when two conditions hold: all policies carry the same term (normally annual), and policies are written uniformly throughout the year. Neither holds here.

- **The policies are not all annual.** Policies B and F are six-month policies. A six-month policy can incept and expire entirely between two year-end valuations, so it contributes earned premium during the year while appearing in neither in-force figure — the average silently omits it.
- **The policies are not written uniformly.** Inceptions are clustered irregularly (March, June, July, October, then January, April, August) and the premium amounts vary widely, so the in-force premium at the two year-ends is not representative of the average exposure carried through the year.
- **The book is small and changing in size.** With only a handful of policies, a single large policy incepting or expiring near a year end swings the average sharply, and the method cannot distinguish that from genuine growth or shrinkage. The estimate would be volatile.

The more nearly uniform the writing pattern and the more consistent the policy term, the more accurate this approximation becomes; this insurer satisfies neither condition.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to explain why estimating earned premium by averaging in-force premium was inappropriate specifically for the insurer in this question. Saying "timing of policies", "different effective dates" or "cancellation of policies" without further detail did not earn full credit — any insurer writing multiple policies has different effective dates. The expected connection was that the more uniformly policies are written, the more accurate the estimate becomes. Common mistakes included citing "stability of the company" without saying which elements had to be stable, asserting the method was appropriate in general without identifying the restrictions that make it inaccurate, and showing the calculation without discussing why it was inappropriate.

## Part e (0.25 points)

Identify one potential use of in-force premium other than estimating earned premium.

### Explanation
Any one practical application of in-force premium is sufficient, for example:

- **Measuring the impact of a proposed rate change** — re-rating the in-force book at proposed rates shows the premium effect on the business actually on the books.
- **Determining how much reinsurance to purchase**, by measuring the exposure currently carried.
- **Tracking growth or contraction of the book**, by comparing in-force premium at successive points in time.
- **Assessing the current potential for loss**, since in-force premium is a proxy for exposure presently at risk.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to identify a practical application of in-force premium. Common mistakes included defining in-force premium rather than describing a use for it ("see the size of the business"), giving an answer that amounted to estimating earned premium despite the question excluding it, using in-force premium to compute a retention rate without accounting for changing premium per risk and new business written, using it to compute unearned premium without describing the calculation, and using it as the denominator of a loss ratio or a loss frequency.
