A **Nuclear Verdict** is a jury award far out of proportion to the plaintiff's economic loss. By the threshold most commonly used, it is a verdict of **\$10 million or more**, and an award of \$100 million or more is called *thermonuclear*. Nuclear verdicts are the most visible symptom of [[Social Inflation|social inflation]], the growth in liability claim costs beyond what economic inflation explains.

The social-inflation component $s$ is the part of the observed [[Loss Trend|severity trend]] left after removing general price and wage inflation. It satisfies

> $$1 + s = \frac{1 + \text{severity trend}}{1 + \text{economic inflation}}$$

The component $s$ is a residual, so it absorbs every litigation-driven cost change at once.

> [!example]- Bodily injury severity has trended 10% a year while economic inflation ran 3%. What is the social-inflation component? {Example}
> - $1 + s = 1.10/1.03 = 1.0680$.
> - The social-inflation component is $s \approx 6.8\%$ a year.

## Drivers and exposed lines

The commonly cited drivers are:

1. Anchoring, where counsel suggests very large figures for non-economic damages.
2. Trial tactics that appeal to jurors' fear and anger toward corporate defendants.
3. Declining public trust in business.
4. Third-party litigation funding, which finances more cases through to trial.
5. Heavy attorney advertising.
6. Court backlogs.

Non-economic and [[Punitive Damages|punitive]] damages have no market price, which is where anchoring works. The lines most exposed are commercial auto (trucking), product liability, premises liability, medical professional liability, and the umbrella and excess layers above them.

A verdict is not a payment. Appeals, remittitur, statutory caps and post-verdict settlements cut many awards down. The larger cost is indirect: each headline verdict resets settlement values for the thousands of similar claims that never reach a jury.

## Effect on reserves and excess layers

Historical development factors understate emergence, so recent accident years are under-reserved and adverse development appears as a calendar-period effect in the [[Development Triangle|development triangle]].

Trend has a **leveraged** effect on [[Layer of Insurance|excess layers]], so reinsurers and excess insurers feel it first.

> [!example]- How does one year of 10% trend change a $\$1$M primary layer and a $\$4$M xs $\$1$M excess layer on these claims? {Example}
> Three open commercial auto bodily injury claims are valued at $\$800{,}000$, $\$1{,}500{,}000$ and $\$3{,}000{,}000$.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - The trended claims are $\$880{,}000$, $\$1{,}650{,}000$ and $\$3{,}300{,}000$.
> > - The primary layer holds $0.80 + 1.00 + 1.00 = \$2.80$M before trend and $0.88 + 1.00 + 1.00 = \$2.88$M after.
> > - The excess layer holds $0 + 0.50 + 2.00 = \$2.50$M before trend and $0 + 0.65 + 2.30 = \$2.95$M after.
> > - Ground-up losses rise $5.83/5.30 = 1.100$, so $10\%$.
> > - The primary layer rises $2.88/2.80 = 1.029$, so $2.9\%$.
> > - The excess layer rises $2.95/2.50 = 1.180$, so $18\%$.
> > - A trend the primary insurer barely notices nearly doubles in the layer above it, which is why excess and reinsurance pricing reacted to social inflation before primary pricing did.

## Failure to settle

An insurer that rejects a policy-limits settlement demand before a nuclear verdict can face [[Bad Faith Damages|bad-faith]] liability for the amount above its limit. A nuclear verdict can then move an excess loss back onto the primary carrier as an extra-contractual obligation, outside the policy limit and outside the rate.

> [!example]- How is this $\$18$M verdict absorbed, and what changes if the case then settles for $\$12$M? {Example}
> A trucking company carries a $\$1$M primary auto policy, a $\$4$M xs $\$1$M umbrella and a $\$10$M xs $\$5$M excess policy. Before trial the plaintiff offered to settle for the $\$1$M primary limit, and the primary insurer refused. The jury returns $\$18$M.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - At the verdict, the primary pays $\$1$M, the umbrella $\$4$M and the excess $\$10$M.
> > - The remaining $\$18\text{M} - \$15\text{M} = \$3$M is uninsured.
> > - At a $\$12$M settlement, the primary pays $\$1$M, the umbrella $\$4$M and the excess $\$7$M, and nothing is uninsured.
> > - Either way the excess layer, priced as remote, pays most of the loss.
> > - The primary insurer refused a within-limits offer, so it is exposed to a failure-to-settle claim for the amount above its limit.

## Legislative responses

The current wave of [[Tort Reform|tort reform]] includes limits on anchoring and on "phantom" medical damages, and disclosure of litigation funding (Georgia, 2025). [[Tort Law]], [[Litigation Costs]] and [[Class Action]] cover the wider legal setting.
