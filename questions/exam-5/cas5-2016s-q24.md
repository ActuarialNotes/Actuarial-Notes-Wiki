Given the following information:

Cumulative Reported Claims ($) as of (months):

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 450 | 650 | 730 | 750 |
| 2012 | 500 | 700 | 780 | |
| 2013 | 500 | 750 | | |
| 2014 | 700 | | | |

| Accident Year | Selected Ultimate Claims ($) | Reported Claims ($) as of December 31, 2015 |
|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 750 | 750 |
| 2012 | 1,000 | 950 |
| 2013 | 1,050 | 900 |
| 2014 | 1,200 | 950 |

- There is no development after 48 months.

## Part a (1.5 points)

Compare actual reported claim emergence to expected reported claim emergence in calendar year 2015 for accident years 2011 through 2014.

### Explanation
**Step 1 — reported claim development**, from the triangle:

| Accident Year | 12-24 | 24-36 | 36-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 1.44 | 1.12 | 1.03 |
| 2012 | 1.40 | 1.11 | |
| 2013 | 1.50 | | |
| All-year average | 1.45 | 1.12 | 1.03 |

Cumulating (with 48-ultimate $= 1.00$) gives age-to-ultimate factors of $1.66$ at 12 months, $1.15$ at 24, $1.03$ at 36 and $1.00$ at 48.

**Step 2 — expected emergence in calendar year 2015.** Each accident year's IBNR at December 31, 2014 is its selected ultimate less the claims reported by then, and the reporting pattern says what share of that IBNR should emerge as the year ages one step:

| Accident Year | IBNR at 12/31/2014 | Age-to-ult at 12/31/2014 | Age-to-ult at 12/31/2015 | Expected 2015 emergence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 0 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 2012 | $1{,}000 - 780 = 220$ | 1.03 | 1.00 | 220.00 |
| 2013 | $1{,}050 - 750 = 300$ | 1.15 | 1.03 | 238.43 |
| 2014 | $1{,}200 - 700 = 500$ | 1.66 | 1.15 | 337.26 |
| Total | | | | 795.69 |

**Step 3 — compare with what actually emerged** (reported at 12/31/2015 less reported at 12/31/2014):

| Accident Year | Actual emergence | Expected emergence | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 0 | 0.00 | 0 |
| 2012 | $950 - 780 = 170$ | 220.00 | -50 |
| 2013 | $900 - 750 = 150$ | 238.43 | -88 |
| 2014 | $950 - 700 = 250$ | 337.26 | -87 |
| Total | | | **-226** |

Actual emergence came in below expected in every year, $226$ short in total for accident years 2011-2014.

of this question provided a reported development pattern and part b. asked for a
recommendation to adjust unpaid claims. However, the intention of part a. was identify whether emergence was more or less than expected. Many candidates implicitly assumed that the lower- than-expected reported emergence translated to lower-than-expected paid emergence.
Candidates working under a different assumption who answered appropriately given their stated assumption were also awarded credit.
Some candidates attempted to name a technique that would result in all three scenarios, rather than one technique for each scenario.

## Part b (1.5 points)

When considering actual emergence compared to expected emergence, the actuary can react in one of three ways:

i. Reduce the recommended unpaid claims
ii. Leave the recommended unpaid claims at the same expected level
iii. Increase the recommended unpaid claims

Identify and briefly justify a reserving technique that would generate each of the three potential reactions using the results of part a. above.

### Explanation
i.
The reported development technique would reduce unpaid claims because it will apply the development factors to the lower-than-expected actual reported claims amount as of 12/31/2015.
ii.
The Bornhuetter-Ferguson technique will leave unpaid claims unchanged since it continues to project future claims according to the a priori expected claims ratio.
iii.
The expected claims technique will increase the unpaid claims because it assumes that the ultimate claims will not change. If actual emergence is low, then it just means actual emergence will be higher in future periods.

### Examiner Report
specifically asks for a reserving technique. Reserving techniques are items such as
Paid/Reported Development, Expected Claims Method, Bornhuetter-Ferguson, Cape Cod, Berquist-Sherman, etc. Many candidates responded with proposed adjustments to data rather than reserving techniques, with responses such as, "adjust the expected ultimate downward" or "assume that a large loss remains unpaid." These responses are incorrect as they do not propose and support the proposal of a reserving technique.
