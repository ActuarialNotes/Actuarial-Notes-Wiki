Given the following:

| Effective Date | Rate Change |
|---|---|
| September 1, 2012 | -10% |
| September 1, 2013 | -5% |
| September 1, 2014 | -3% |

- A law change mandated a rate decrease of 15% effective February 1, 2015 applicable to all in-force policies.
- All policies are annual.

## Part a (1 points)

Calculate the on-level factor to current rate level for calendar year 2014 earned premium.

### Answer
0.816313

### Explanation
**Step 1 — set up the rate level history.** With annual policies, a rate change effective September 1 cuts a diagonal across the calendar-year square. Three rate levels touch calendar year 2014; index the level in force just after the 9/1/2012 change to 1.000:

| Period | In force for writings | Cumulative rate level |
|---|---|---|
| B | 9/1/2012 to 8/31/2013 | 1.00000 |
| C | 9/1/2013 to 8/31/2014 | $1.00 \times 0.95 = 0.95000$ |
| D | 9/1/2014 onward | $0.95 \times 0.97 = 0.92150$ |

The law change is *not* a fourth region of the 2014 parallelogram: it takes effect February 1, 2015, after the calendar year being on-leveled, so it changes only the current rate level.

**Step 2 — area weights within calendar year 2014.** The 9/1 change dates sit $8/12$ of the way through the year, so the corner triangles are:

$$\text{Weight}_B = 0.5 \times \tfrac{8}{12} \times \tfrac{8}{12} = 0.2222$$

$$\text{Weight}_D = 0.5 \times \tfrac{4}{12} \times \tfrac{4}{12} = 0.0556$$

$$\text{Weight}_C = 1 - 0.2222 - 0.0556 = 0.7222$$

Weight B is the triangle of premium still earning in 2014 from policies written before the 9/1/2013 change; weight D is the triangle earned in 2014 from policies written after the 9/1/2014 change.

**Step 3 — average rate level for 2014.**

$$0.2222(1.00) + 0.7222(0.95) + 0.0556(0.92150) = 0.959528$$

**Step 4 — current rate level.** Every change to date, including the 15% law-mandated decrease, is cumulated:

$$1.00 \times 0.95 \times 0.97 \times 0.85 = 0.783275$$

**Step 5 — on-level factor.**

$$\text{OLF}_{2014} = \frac{0.783275}{0.959528} = \mathbf{0.816313}$$

The base chosen for the index is arbitrary. Indexing the level *before* the 9/1/2012 change to 1.000 gives levels of 0.90, 0.855 and 0.82935, an average of 0.863575 and a current level of 0.704948 — and $0.704948 / 0.863575 = 0.816313$, the identical factor, since only the ratio matters.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to build an earned premium on-level factor with the parallelogram method: compute the area weights for calendar year 2014, cumulate the rate changes into a rate level for each area, form the 2014 average rate level, and divide the current cumulative rate level by it.

Common errors included misapplying the law change — treating the 15% as an increase, or applying it at February 1, 2014 instead of February 1, 2015; transposing rate changes and effective dates, or leaving a rate or law change out of the current rate level or the 2014 average; and mis-weighting the areas, most often by computing the triangles as though the changes fell on October 1 rather than September 1.

## Part b (0.5 points)

Identify a weakness with the parallelogram method and briefly describe a solution.

### Explanation
**Weakness — it assumes policies are written uniformly throughout the year.** The area weights are computed purely from geometry, so they are only correct if writings are spread evenly over the calendar year. For a seasonal line, or a book whose volume is growing or shrinking, the true exposure-weighted average rate level differs from the geometric one and the on-level factor is wrong.

**Solution — extension of exposures.** Rerate every historical policy individually using the current rate manual. This produces premium at current rate level directly, with no assumption at all about the distribution of writings, and is standard practice where the policy-level detail and systems are available. A cheaper partial fix is to apply the parallelogram method to **quarterly or monthly** data, which shrinks the interval over which uniform writing has to be assumed.

A second acceptable weakness: the parallelogram method adjusts only for the *average* rate change, so if a revision was targeted at particular classes or territories and the mix of business has shifted, the on-level premium will be distorted. Extension of exposures resolves this too, because each policy is rerated with its own classification.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to state a genuine weakness of the parallelogram method and pair it with a solution that actually addresses that weakness.

Common errors included identifying only one half of the pair — a weakness with no solution, or a solution with no weakness; asserting "uniform distribution" without saying uniform in what (writing of policies, over the year); and confusing uniform *writing* of policies with uniform *earning* of premium, which is a different assumption.
