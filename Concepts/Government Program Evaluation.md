**Government Program Evaluation** is the structured assessment of whether a government or insurance-industry program — a workers' compensation board, a public auto insurer, a residual market, a guaranty fund, an agricultural or disaster program — achieves its purpose at an acceptable cost and can go on doing so. Exam 6C names the criteria: solvency, efficiency, stability, viability and long-term prospects, and the effects of external factors.

> $$\text{Funded ratio} = \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Liabilities for benefits incurred}}$$

> $$\text{Expense ratio} = \frac{\text{Administrative expense}}{\text{Premium or assessment revenue}}$$

- **[[Solvency]]** — can the program pay what it already owes? The measures differ by program: the funded ratio for a workers' compensation board, capital against a legislated target for a [[Public Auto Insurance|public auto insurer]], the fund balance against the [[Probable Maximum Loss|PML]] for a catastrophe fund, post-event assessment capacity for [[PACICC]]. A program that is solvent only because it can tax or assess later should be described that way.
- **Efficiency** — how much of each premium dollar reaches claimants, and at what transaction cost (administration, litigation, disputes). It includes *incentive* efficiency: flat levies, subsidised premiums or benefits with no link to behaviour create [[Moral Hazard]] and [[Adverse Selection]] that raise the program's cost.
- **Stability** — are rates and benefits predictable? Large year-to-year swings in assessment rates, politically timed rate freezes followed by catch-up increases, or benefit schedules rewritten every few years ([[Automobile Insurance Reform]]) are all instability, whatever the average level.
- **Viability and long-term prospects** — is the funding model sustainable as exposure, demographics and costs change? The warning signs are a growing unfunded liability, falling voluntary participation (good risks leaving), a growing [[Residual Market]], and a funding model that works only while the program is young.
- **External factors** — what outside the program moves its results: interest rates (the discount rate on long-tail liabilities), wage and medical [[Inflation]], the economy (payroll or premium base, claim duration), [[Climate Risk|climate]] (flood, crop and catastrophe frequency), court decisions widening coverage, and federal cost-sharing formulas.
- Evaluation also asks whether the program meets its **objective** and how it interacts with the [[Voluntary Private Insurance Market]] — the remaining parts of the [[Social Insurance]] framework. A good answer takes each criterion in turn, gives evidence for each, and ends with a recommendation.

> [!example]- Evaluating a Workers' Compensation Board {Example}
> A provincial [[Workers Compensation Insurance|workers' compensation]] board reports:
>
> - assets $\$12.6$ billion; benefit liabilities $\$14.0$ billion, with a duration of $8$ years;
> - assessable payroll $\$80$ billion; average assessment rate $\$1.85$ per $\$100$ of payroll;
> - administrative expense $\$190$ million;
> - average rate changes over five years of $+2\%$, $+14\%$, $-9\%$, $+11\%$, $-6\%$;
> - a funding policy of retiring any unfunded liability over $10$ years at $5\%$.
>
> Evaluate the board against each criterion.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > **Solvency.** The board is $10\%$ underfunded:
> >
> > $$
> > \begin{align*}
> > \text{Funded ratio} &= \frac{12.6}{14.0} \\
> > &= 90\%
> > \end{align*}
> > $$
> >
> > **Cost of the funding policy.** Retiring the $\$1.4$ billion shortfall over $10$ years at $5\%$:
> >
> > $$
> > \begin{align*}
> > \text{Annual payment} &= \frac{\$1.4\text{B}}{a_{\overline{10}|0.05}} \\
> > &= \frac{\$1.4\text{B}}{7.7217} \\
> > &= \$181.3\text{M}
> > \end{align*}
> > $$
> >
> > That is $\$0.23$ per $\$100$ of payroll — a $12\%$ surcharge on the $\$1.85$ average rate.
> >
> > **Efficiency.** Revenue is $0.0185 \times \$80\text{B} = \$1.48\text{B}$, so the expense ratio is $190 / 1{,}480 = 12.8\%$. The figure means little alone: ask what it buys (return-to-work programs lower claim duration) and compare cost per claim with similar boards.
> >
> > **Stability.** Rate changes swinging between $-9\%$ and $+14\%$ are poor stability for employers who budget payroll costs. A rate-stabilisation policy — smoothing toward the funding target — would help.
> >
> > **External factors.** With duration $8$, a one-point fall in the discount rate raises liabilities by about $8\%$, to $\$15.1$ billion. If the assets are mainly equities that do not respond:
> >
> > $$
> > \begin{align*}
> > \text{Funded ratio} &= \frac{12.6}{15.12} \\
> > &= 83.3\%
> > \end{align*}
> > $$
> >
> > A recession would also shrink payroll (the assessment base) while lengthening claim durations — both external, both unfavourable.
> >
> > **Viability.** Viable: the funding policy is credible and the surcharge affordable. The weaknesses are the interest-rate mismatch and the rate volatility. **Recommendation:** match part of the liabilities with duration-matched bonds, and adopt a funding corridor that phases in surcharges and rebates rather than swinging rates each year.
