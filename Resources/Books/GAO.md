The GAO's December 2022 report on **third-party litigation financing (TPLF)** — an outside funder paying for a lawsuit in exchange for a share of the recovery. Read for [[Exam 6C (CAS)|Exam 6C]] objective A3, trends in [[Tort Litigation|tort litigation]]. The syllabus **excludes the appendices**.

## What the report finds

- **What TPLF is.** A funder who is not a party to the suit provides money to a litigant or a law firm in return for a share of any recovery. The plaintiff owes nothing if the case fails, which is what distinguishes funding from a loan.
- **Two distinct markets.**
  - *Commercial* — millions of dollars advanced to corporate litigants or to law firms, often against a portfolio of cases rather than a single suit.
  - *Consumer* — small advances, typically under \$10,000, to individual plaintiffs for living expenses while a personal-injury claim is pending.
- **Who the funders are.** Private firms drawing investment capital from sources including endowments and pension funds. The practice gained material traction in the U.S. from around 2010.
- **The data gap.** Basic market facts — funders' rates of return, and the total amount of funding provided — are not publicly available. Experts told GAO that collecting them would place a burden on the courts.
- **Disclosure and regulation.** No federal law regulates TPLF specifically. Some states cap consumer funding charges. There is no nationwide disclosure requirement in federal litigation, though individual courts have ordered disclosure in particular cases.

## Why it is on the syllabus

TPLF changes the economics of a claim on the plaintiff's side: it funds cases that would otherwise settle early or not be brought, and it introduces a party with an economic interest in the outcome who is invisible to the defendant and its insurer. That bears directly on claim frequency, settlement behaviour, duration and severity trend — the assumptions a Canadian [[Appointed Actuary|appointed actuary]] sets for liability lines. The report is American; the syllabus uses it for the mechanism and the trend, not for Canadian law.
