![[Alberta Auto Reform - Cover.svg]]

A September 2020 report to Alberta's Minister of Finance on fundamental reform of the province's automobile insurance compensation system. Its authors, the Automobile Insurance Advisory Committee appointed in December 2019 (Chris Daniel, chair; Shelley L. Miller, Q.C.; Dr. Larry Ohlhauser), were asked for recommendations built on a private sector delivery model, fair, accessible and affordable insurance, timely and appropriate outcomes when claims are made, and a viable and sustainable system. It concluded that the optimal and only solution for long-term price stability is to replace the hybrid tort/no-fault model with a pure no-fault care and compensation model.

## I Executive Summary
- The committee found that since 1988 the cause of high premiums was ever-increasing bodily injury loss costs — specifically non-pecuniary general damage awards from the [[Tort Litigation|tort]] litigation process — and that health outcomes improved where tort systems were replaced with [[No-Fault Insurance|no-fault]] models.
- Alberta's one reform since 1990, in 2003, restricted non-pecuniary awards for soft tissue injuries (the Minor Injury Regulation; see [[Minor Injury Guideline]]), enacted the Diagnostic and Treatment Protocol Regulation (DTPR) and a Grid; the committee found the first narrowed from its original intent and the DTPR short of its potential, and the reforms did not produce long-term sustainability, affordability or accessibility.
- Jurisdictions that eliminated tort in favour of comprehensive care and income replacement saw stable premiums; those that rebalanced tort with no-fault benefits saw only temporary stability ([[Automobile Insurance Reform]]).
- It recommends a pure no-fault model with a redesigned continuum of care, a long-term care program for the catastrophically injured funded by a pool, and a Traffic Injury Regulator — a Board and Tribunal over four arms, including medical and claims assessment panels and a reconstituted Automobile Insurance Rate Board.
- The consulting actuary's report showed the committee's Model I would be expected to reduce premiums by 9.4% for the majority of consumers buying the full package of insurance.

- Reformed models that retain tort features result in continuing premium instability; in the New South Wales and Ontario reforms, tort "found areas for regrowth", and preserving any component of tort is inconsistent with the needs of traffic injured.
- The principle of deterrence is no longer a convincing justification for tort in auto insurance: enforcement, penalties, education and higher premiums for reckless drivers achieve it more effectively.
- Medical review panels under an administrative model should replace "duelling doctors" and the delays of tort litigation, and the original DTPR design should be extended to all other injuries.
- The Judgment Interest Act should set the rate for non-pecuniary damages equal to that for pecuniary claims and suspend judgment interest on non-pecuniary damages for two years from the date of loss.
- Pure no-fault regimes like Manitoba's and Québec's are within provincial legislative authority, and *Morrow v. Zhang* satisfied the committee that a Charter challenge would be untenable if reform is a balanced, interdependent package.
