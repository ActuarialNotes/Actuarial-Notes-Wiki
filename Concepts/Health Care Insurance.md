**Health Care Insurance** in Canada is the single-payer, provincially administered system ("medicare") that covers medically necessary hospital and physician services for all residents, funded from general taxation rather than from risk-based premiums. Private insurance is prohibited from duplicating covered services in most provinces and instead fills the **supplementary** layer — drugs, dental, vision, paramedical, and hospital upgrades.

- **The five principles of the *Canada Health Act***, which provinces must satisfy to receive full federal transfer payments: **public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability and accessibility**. These are the framework's defining list and are examinable directly.
- **Federal-provincial structure.** Health care delivery is provincial ([[Federal-Provincial Jurisdiction]]); the federal role is the **spending power** — the Canada Health Transfer, conditional on the five principles. Ottawa influences a system it does not administer by paying for part of it.
- **What is not covered** is where private insurance lives: prescription drugs outside hospital, dental, vision, physiotherapy and psychology, private or semi-private rooms, and out-of-country care. Group benefit plans covering these are a very large private market beside the public one.
- **Why single-payer at all:** health care is the extreme [[Adverse Selection]] case — the sick would buy, the healthy would not — and administrative cost is far lower with one payer than with many. The counter-arguments are queues for elective procedures, absence of a price signal, and no consumer choice of insurer.
- **Interaction with P&C insurance** is direct and examinable: provincial health insurers hold **statutory subrogation** rights to recover the cost of treating accident victims from at-fault parties, so auto and liability insurers pay health-system costs through subrogated claims. Auto [[Statutory Accident Benefits|accident benefits]] similarly pay for treatment the public system does not cover.
- **Out-of-country travel insurance** exists precisely because portability covers only the provincial rate, which is a small fraction of a foreign hospital bill.

> [!example]- Who Pays for the Accident Victim's Care {Example}
> A person is seriously injured in an automobile collision caused by another driver. Their care includes: emergency and surgical treatment in hospital, six months of physiotherapy, prescription drugs at home, a modified vehicle, and lost income.
>
> Identify the payer for each, and the coordination between them.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > - **Hospital and physician care** — the **provincial health plan**, at no charge to the patient. The province may then **subrogate** against the at-fault driver's liability insurer to recover its cost, so a P&C insurer ultimately pays for part of it.
> > - **Physiotherapy** — outside the *Canada Health Act* basket, so the **auto insurer's [[Statutory Accident Benefits|accident benefits]]** respond, up to the applicable injury-tier limit ([[Minor Injury Guideline|MIG]] or non-catastrophic).
> > - **Prescription drugs at home** — accident benefits again, with a private group drug plan as an alternative payer; coordination-of-benefits rules decide the order.
> > - **Vehicle modification** — accident benefits, within the medical/rehabilitation limit, and effectively only available at the non-catastrophic or [[Catastrophic Impairment|catastrophic]] tier.
> > - **Lost income** — accident benefits income replacement first, then a [[Tort Litigation|tort]] claim against the at-fault driver for the shortfall, with benefits already received deducted as a [[Collateral Benefits|collateral benefit]].
> >
> > The structural point: Canada's auto insurers are, in effect, a **second health payer** for accident victims, covering exactly what medicare does not. That is why Canadian auto accident benefit costs are dominated by paramedical treatment rather than by hospital care — and why a change in what the public plan covers moves auto insurance costs directly.
