Given the following data as of December 31, 2018:

| Accident/Calendar Year | Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000s) | Earned Premium (\$000s) |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 7,200 | 10,400 |
| 2017 | 6,300 | 11,000 |
| 2018 | 4,700 | 11,500 |

| Cumulative Age-to-Ultimate Factors | 12-Ult | 24-Ult | 36-Ult | 48-Ult |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | 1.764 | 1.260 | 1.050 | 1.000 |

| Annual Trends | |
|---|---|
| Claims | 3.0% |
| Premium | 2.0% |

| Effective Date | Rate Change |
|---|---|
| July 1, 2016 | 4.0% |
| July 1, 2017 | 2.0% |

- All policies have an annual term and are written evenly throughout the year.

Calculate ultimate claims for accident year 2017 using the Cape Cod technique.

### Answer
Approximately $7,932 thousand

### Explanation
The Cape Cod technique estimates a **single** expected claims ratio from all years combined, using *used-up* (on-level, trended) premium as the exposure base, then applies it to the unreported portion of the accident year in question.

$$\text{Ultimate}_i = \text{Reported}_i + ECR \times \text{Adjusted Premium}_i \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{CDF_i}\right)$$

**Step 1 — on-level factors (parallelogram method).** Annual policies written evenly, so a July 1 rate change affects $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.5)^2 = 12.5\%$ of that calendar year's earned premium. Current rate level is $1.04 \times 1.02 = 1.0608$.

| CY | Rate level composition | Average rate level | OLF to current |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | $87.5\%$ at $1.00$, $12.5\%$ at $1.04$ | $1.00500$ | $1.05552$ |
| 2017 | $12.5\%$ at $1.00$, $75\%$ at $1.04$, $12.5\%$ at $1.0608$ | $1.03760$ | $1.02236$ |
| 2018 | $12.5\%$ at $1.04$, $87.5\%$ at $1.0608$ | $1.05820$ | $1.00246$ |

**Step 2 — put claims and premium on a common level.** Working at the 2017 level (any common level gives the same final answer):

*Trended claims* — reported claims only, **not** developed to ultimate:

$$2016: 7{,}200 \times 1.03 = 7{,}416 \qquad 2017: 6{,}300 \qquad 2018: \frac{4{,}700}{1.03} = 4{,}563$$

$$\sum = 18{,}279$$

*Used-up premium* — earned premium put on the 2017 rate level, trended to 2017, then multiplied by the **percentage reported** $1/CDF$:

| AY | EP | On-level to 2017 | Premium trend | % reported | Used-up premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 10,400 | $1.05552/1.02236 = 1.03243$ | $\times 1.02$ | $1/1.050 = 95.24\%$ | 10,429 |
| 2017 | 11,000 | $1.00000$ | $\times 1$ | $1/1.260 = 79.37\%$ | 8,731 |
| 2018 | 11,500 | $1.00246/1.02236 = 0.98054$ | $\div 1.02$ | $1/1.764 = 56.69\%$ | 6,267 |

$$\sum = 25{,}428$$

**Step 3 — the expected claims ratio**, one ratio for all years combined rather than an average of yearly ratios:

$$ECR = \frac{18{,}279}{25{,}428} = 0.7189$$

**Step 4 — apply to accident year 2017.** The ratio and the premium are both at the 2017 level, so no further adjustment is needed:

$$\text{Ultimate}_{2017} = 6{,}300 + 0.7189 \times 11{,}000 \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{1.260}\right)$$

$$= 6{,}300 + 0.7189 \times 11{,}000 \times 0.20635 = 6{,}300 + 1{,}632 = \$7{,}932 \text{ thousand}$$

Normalizing everything to the 2018 level instead gives $ECR = 0.7118$, which de-trends to $0.7118 \times (1.02/1.03) = 0.7049$ against an AY2017 premium of $11{,}218$ at that level — and the same ultimate of \$7,932 thousand. The choice of common level is arbitrary; only consistency between the ratio and the premium it multiplies matters.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to calculate ultimate claims for a single accident year using the Cape Cod technique, adjusting historical premium and claims with the given rate changes and annual trends. Various combinations of trending/de-trending and on-leveling/de-leveling are mathematically equivalent and produce the same answer.

Common mistakes included failing to de-trend and back out the rate change from the expected claims ratio to put it on the 2017 level; developing claims to ultimate before using them in the expected claims ratio; using full trended on-level earned premium instead of *used-up* premium in the ratio; selecting or averaging individual expected claims ratios by accident year rather than computing one ratio across all years; using incorrect trend periods; setting ultimate claims equal to premium times the expected claims ratio instead of reported claims plus expected *unreported* claims; and calculating the ultimate for the wrong accident year.
