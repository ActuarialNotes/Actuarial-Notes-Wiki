Given the following information as of December 31, 2016:

**Cumulative Paid Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 1,150 | 2,250 | 3,000 |
| 2015 | 1,250 | 2,400 | |
| 2016 | 1,550 | | |

**Cumulative Reported Claims (\$000) as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 5,150 | 7,200 | 8,000 |
| 2015 | 4,800 | 6,700 | |
| 2016 | 4,750 | | |

**Cumulative Reported Claim Counts as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 102 | 107 | 108 |
| 2015 | 96 | 101 | |
| 2016 | 99 | | |

**Open Claim Counts as of (months)**

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 52 | 28 | 12 |
| 2015 | 46 | 25 | |
| 2016 | 42 | | |

- Reported claim counts exclude claims closed without payment.
- Historical claim cost inflation is 0%.

## Part a (0.75 points)

Calculate the paid to reported claim ratio triangle and briefly describe what it suggests about changes in:

i. Settlement rates
ii. Case reserve adequacy

### Answer
The ratios increase down every column. That is consistent with a speed-up in settlement rates, with a weakening of case reserve adequacy, or with both — this single diagnostic cannot separate the two.

### Explanation
Divide cumulative paid by cumulative reported at each age:

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | $1{,}150/5{,}150 = 22.3\%$ | $2{,}250/7{,}200 = 31.3\%$ | $3{,}000/8{,}000 = 37.5\%$ |
| 2015 | $1{,}250/4{,}800 = 26.0\%$ | $2{,}400/6{,}700 = 35.8\%$ | |
| 2016 | $1{,}550/4{,}750 = 32.6\%$ | | |

A diagnostic triangle is read **down the columns**, comparing the same maturity across accident years; reading across a row only shows the ordinary maturing of a year and says nothing about change.

Both columns rise: at 12 months 22.3% to 26.0% to 32.6%, and at 24 months 31.3% to 35.8%. The ratio is $\text{paid}/(\text{paid} + \text{case})$, so it can rise for either of two reasons.

**i. Settlement rates.** If claims are being closed faster, more of each year's dollars have been paid by a given maturity, which lifts the numerator. The rising ratios are consistent with a **speed-up in settlement**.

**ii. Case reserve adequacy.** If case reserves are being set less adequately — a change in reserving philosophy, for instance — reported claims are lower at each age while paid is unaffected, which also lifts the ratio. The rising ratios are equally consistent with a **weakening of case reserve adequacy**.

The paid-to-reported ratio on its own cannot distinguish the two causes; that is exactly why the additional diagnostics of part b are needed.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute a paid to reported claim ratio triangle and draw inferences from it about possible changes in settlement rates and in case reserve adequacy.

Common errors included interpreting the triangle "across the rows" instead of "down the columns", and asserting that no inference could be drawn from the diagnostic at all.

## Part b (1.5 points)

Calculate the closed to reported claim count ratio triangle and the average case outstanding triangle and briefly describe what the triangles suggest about changes in:

i. Settlement rates
ii. Case reserve adequacy

### Answer
Closed-to-reported count ratios rise down every column, confirming a speed-up in settlement rates. Average case outstanding is essentially flat down each column, with the latest diagonal slightly lower, so case reserve adequacy is broadly unchanged — at most slightly weakened in calendar year 2016.

### Explanation
**Closed to reported claim count ratio.** Closed counts are not given directly; back them out as reported less open. At 12 months, for example, accident year 2014 has $102 - 52 = 50$ closed.

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | $50/102 = 49.0\%$ | $79/107 = 73.8\%$ | $96/108 = 88.9\%$ |
| 2015 | $50/96 = 52.1\%$ | $76/101 = 75.2\%$ | |
| 2016 | $57/99 = 57.6\%$ | | |

**Average case outstanding.** Case outstanding is reported less paid; divide by the open count. At 12 months for 2014, $(5{,}150 - 1{,}150)/52 = 76.9$ (\$000).

| Accident Year | 12 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 76.9 | 176.8 | 416.7 |
| 2015 | 77.2 | 172.0 | |
| 2016 | 76.2 | | |

**i. Settlement rates — from the closed-to-reported count triangle.** Reading down the columns, the proportion of each year's claims already closed at a given maturity is rising: 49.0% to 52.1% to 57.6% at 12 months, and 73.8% to 75.2% at 24 months. Claims are being **settled faster**, which confirms the first of the two competing explanations offered in part a.

**ii. Case reserve adequacy — from the average case outstanding triangle.** This is the diagnostic that isolates adequacy, because it is unaffected by how many claims have closed. With claim cost inflation at 0%, an unchanged reserving philosophy would leave the columns roughly flat, and that is what they show: 76.9, 77.2, 76.2 at 12 months and 176.8, 172.0 at 24 months. There is no evidence of a material change in **case reserve adequacy**; the slight dip on the latest diagonal is small enough to be random fluctuation.

An argument that adequacy has weakened slightly in calendar year 2016 is also defensible, and was accepted: the whole latest diagonal is below the one before it, and if settlement has genuinely sped up then the claims left open should be the slower, larger ones, so a flat average case is arguably a mild weakening.

The pair of triangles is the point of the question. Part a's rising paid-to-reported ratio had two candidate causes; the count triangle establishes that settlement speed is one of them, and the average case triangle shows that the other, a deterioration in case adequacy, is at most a minor contributor.

### Examiner Report
Candidates were expected to compute a closed to reported claim count ratio triangle and an average case outstanding triangle, then draw the settlement-rate inference from the first and the case-adequacy inference from the second.

Common errors included interpreting the triangles across the rows rather than down the columns; claiming no inference could be drawn; and calculating average *reported* claims per claim rather than average *case outstanding* per open claim.
